## 1. Published models of the jaw harp itself, and published code

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed or conference paper presenting a synthesis model (physical or signal) of the jaw harp was found in any of the venues searched; the scientific literature stops at the sound-production mechanism (Adkins 1974: the lamella makes one short pulse each time it passes through the frame, and the mouth cavity is the resonator those pulses excite). The only published code found is one 2026 open-source plugin ("SuperDuper Kubyz", MIT), advertised as a physical model but implemented as 16 additive sine harmonics into a parallel bank of band-pass "formant" filters, and its own author measured that 16 harmonics cover only "the lower third" of the instrument.

### Cited Findings

#### Adkins 1974, JASA 55(3), 667-670 [PR] (abstract)
- Citation confirmed: C. J. Adkins, "Investigation of the sound-producing mechanism of the jew's harp", J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 55 (3), 667-670 (1974). — [doi:10.1121/1.1914580](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1914580)
- Mechanism: "the jew's harp excites the mouth cavity by producing short pulses when the lamella passes through the frame. A model is proposed for the mechanism by which the pulses are generated." — [doi:10.1121/1.1914580](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1914580)
- Two regimes of the mouth, stated in the time domain: "When the mouth cavity is tuned to low frequencies, its natural resonance time is longer than the period between successive pulses and the harmonic series of the fundamental frequency of the lamella dominates the sound as the mouth tuning is changed. At high frequencies, the mouth cavity and ear respond to each successive pulse independently so that the dominant sensation of pitch coincides with the natural resonance frequency of the mouth and may be tuned continuously." — [doi:10.1121/1.1914580](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1914580)
- A secondary description of the pulse model (student report, with Adkins' figure redrawn): while the tongue is clear of the frame "the airflow around the tongue is relatively free"; when it is level with the arms "the airflow becomes interrupted" and "the tongue acts like a piston driving forward the air in front of it, and dragging the air behind"; when the tongue clears the arms "the pressure balances out in front and behind the tongue, causing air to flow backwards". — [Li 2015](https://courses.physics.illinois.edu/phys406/sp2017/Student_Projects/Spring15/Simon_Li_Physics_406_Final_Report_Sp15.pdf) [HOBBY]
- A search summary states that a citing article says Adkins "created an electric analog" of the instrument; this could not be checked against the paper. (snippet) — [doi:10.1121/1.1914580](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1914580)

#### Ledang 1972 and Crane 1968 [PR] (abstract / secondary only)
- Ledang, "On the Acoustics and the Systematic Classification of the Jaw's Harp", Yearbook of the International Folk Music Council: existing theories of the sound-producing mechanism are called unconvincing; the paper reports "an empiric investigation by means of electronic sound spectrographs" and proposes a new general view. (snippet of the abstract page; the Cambridge record shows copyright 1973, other citations give 1972) — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/yearbook-of-the-international-folk-music-council/article/on-the-acoustics-and-the-systematic-classification-of-the-jaws-harp/FFA2A840F93D1BFC82157BC4BB81730B)
- Ledang's gap experiment as retold in a student report: he varied the space between the arms; when it "were too wide, the sound played would decay much quicker". — [Li 2015](https://courses.physics.illinois.edu/phys406/sp2017/Student_Projects/Spring15/Simon_Li_Physics_406_Final_Report_Sp15.pdf) [HOBBY]
- Crane ("The Jew's Harp as Aerophone", Galpin Society Journal 1968) and Ledang classified the instrument as an aerophone, arguing that it functions fully only when a stream of air moves past the tongue; Hornbostel-Sachs lists it as a plucked idiophone (121.22). (snippet) — [Doktorski, free-reed taxonomy](https://henrydoktorski.com/free-reed/description/taxonomy.html); [Wikipedia, Jew's harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew%27s_harp)
- "Despite its 'chameleonic' nature and different constructions, the JH remains easily recognizable (Ledang, 1972)." — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051) [PR]

#### Trias 2010 [THESIS] (not obtained; known only through secondary sources)
- Sylvain Trias, "Helmholtz & coupled resonators acoustics in jew's harp playing", Master thesis in Traditional Arts, Telemark University College, 2010; reported length 109 pages. — [noseflute.org blog](https://nose-flute.blogspot.com/2014/09/nose-flute-physics-ii.html) [HOBBY]; (snippet) [Tran Quang Hai blog](http://tranquanghaidanmoi.blogspot.com/2012/06/httpsylvaintriascom.html)
- Content as reported by the blog and by Trias in correspondence quoted there: the mouth is treated as a Helmholtz resonator; Trias used about 1 cm^2 for the lip opening and estimated the mouth section at 9 cm^2; the melody comes from "the change of volume of the front resonant cavity" (blog's translation of his French); frequencies near 500 Hz need a very high larynx position (his reference to thesis p. 14 and p. 89). — [noseflute.org blog](https://nose-flute.blogspot.com/2014/09/nose-flute-physics-ii.html) [HOBBY]
- Helmholtz formula as used by the blog author (not Trias' own text): F = (c / 2π)·√(A2 / (A1·l1·l2)), with A1 = 9 cm^2 (mouth section), A2 = 0.25 cm^2 (lip opening), l1 = 7 cm (mouth depth), l2 = 1 cm (lip depth), c = 34300 cm/s, giving 343.88 Hz against a measured 347 Hz for a nose flute. — [noseflute.org blog](https://nose-flute.blogspot.com/2014/09/nose-flute-physics-ii.html) [HOBBY]
- How Trias' thesis is used in a peer-reviewed paper: jaw harp prosody "engages two different domains, vocal and instrumental, where the vocal controls the dynamics and the instrumental the frequency of the harmonic components (Trias, 2010)". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051) [PR]

#### Li 2015, University of Illinois Physics 406 report [HOBBY: student report, not peer reviewed]
- Two steel harps (tenor in F, alto in G), six recordings each: normal mouth, breathing out, "increased lung capacity and holding breath", and the silent mouth shapes of "A", "O", "I". — [Li 2015](https://courses.physics.illinois.edu/phys406/sp2017/Student_Projects/Spring15/Simon_Li_Physics_406_Final_Report_Sp15.pdf)
- Normal mouth: "the odd harmonics dominate the spectrum, with another peak around 3000 Hz". Breathing out: the general spectrum is unchanged apart from amplitudes. Holding the breath with the mouth connected to the lungs: "We no longer see a resonant peak around 3000 Hz." — [Li 2015](https://courses.physics.illinois.edu/phys406/sp2017/Student_Projects/Spring15/Simon_Li_Physics_406_Final_Report_Sp15.pdf)
- Peaks read from the harp spectra: mouth shape "A": about 1000, 3200 and 4200 Hz (tenor), 1000, 3000 and 3600 Hz (alto); "O": 3300 and 4200 Hz (tenor), 3000, 3600 and 5000 Hz (alto); "I": 3300 and 4200 Hz (tenor), 3000 and 3600 Hz (alto). The author's own white-noise measurement of his mouth did not match these ("most of the 'natural' resonances did not match up") and he suspects measurement error. — [Li 2015](https://courses.physics.illinois.edu/phys406/sp2017/Student_Projects/Spring15/Simon_Li_Physics_406_Final_Report_Sp15.pdf)
- No model, equation or code is given; the report retells Adkins and Ledang. — [Li 2015](https://courses.physics.illinois.edu/phys406/sp2017/Student_Projects/Spring15/Simon_Li_Physics_406_Final_Report_Sp15.pdf)

#### Published code: "SuperDuper Kubyz" and the iOS app "Jaw Harp Synth" (KubizBeat) [CODE] [MKT]
- Repository `fortunto2/superduper-dsp`: "36 free open-source audio plugins in Rust (CLAP, VST3, AU) for macOS and Windows", MIT licence, created 2026-05-16, last push 2026-11-13, 8 stars at the time of reading. — [github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp](https://github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp)
- Marketing claim: the plugin page calls Kubyz a "physical model of the Bashkir jaw harp, with a vowel pad that shapes it like a mouth"; the App Store text calls the iOS app "the first physics-based jaw harp synthesizer for iPhone" with a "Physics-based sound engine modeled on real Yakutian kubyz acoustics". — [author's project page](https://rustman-blog.pages.dev/projects/project-superduper-dsp); [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id6758465213) [MKT]
- What the code is: "Kubyz voice — additive 16-harmonic oscillator + ADSR + 1–6 band formant bank". Sixteen sine oscillators at n·f0 with a fixed amplitude table (`N_HARMONICS = 16`), summed, scaled by 0.25 and passed through `tanh`; then the formant stage; then a DC blocker. — [voice.rs](https://github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp/blob/main/synth-core/src/kubyz/voice.rs); [mod.rs](https://github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp/blob/main/synth-core/src/kubyz/mod.rs)
- The author's stated reason for additive: the tone "is defined as much by the *which harmonics* present as by the formant cavities of the player's mouth. Pre-baking one wavetable would freeze the harmonics". — [voice.rs](https://github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp/blob/main/synth-core/src/kubyz/voice.rs)
- Mouth model: 1 to 6 parallel band-pass biquads (RBJ band-pass, 0 dB peak), each with centre, bandwidth and gain; Q = f / max(bw, 20 Hz) clamped to 0.5 … 12 ("Above ~12 a band-pass rings forever on a sustained drone"); the weighted sum is multiplied by a make-up gain of 1.8 and passed through `tanh`; a dry/wet "Vox Mix". Coefficients are recomputed only when a band's tuning changes. — [formant_bank.rs](https://github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp/blob/main/synth-core/src/kubyz/formant_bank.rs)
- Vowel pad targets are speech values ("Peterson-Barney male-average formants"), in Hz, F1 / F2 / F3: ɑ 730 / 1090 / 2440; ɛ 530 / 1840 / 2480; i 270 / 2290 / 3010; o 570 / 840 / 2410; u 300 / 870 / 2240. F1 is on the pad's Y axis ("jaw aperture"), F2 on the X axis ("tongue position"). — [plugin README](https://github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp/blob/main/effects/superduper-kubyz/README.md)
- Mouth motion is an LFO that walks the pad cursor along a closed curve (Circle, Sine, Figure-8, Triangle, Line), with rate, depth, tempo sync and stereo pan; default MIDI mapping CC1 = mouth depth, CC2 = mouth stereo, CC11 = F1, CC74 = F2, CC71 = F3. — [plugin README](https://github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp/blob/main/effects/superduper-kubyz/README.md); [trajectory.rs](https://github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp/blob/main/synth-core/src/kubyz/trajectory.rs)
- Velocity: at velocity 1 all formant frequencies are multiplied by (1 + shift); factory presets use shift 0.15 (0.10 for the sample-fitted one). — [voice.rs](https://github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp/blob/main/synth-core/src/kubyz/voice.rs); [presets.rs](https://github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp/blob/main/effects/superduper-kubyz/src/presets.rs)
- Envelope, stated in the presets: "a real jaw harp's tongue is plucked once, rings out, decays. NOT a piano with sustain — sustain must be 0". Attack 0.003 s, decay 1.2 s ("Bashkir Kubyz"), 1.0 s ("Khomus Sample"), 1.8 s ("Real D2"), sustain 0, release 0.3 s. — [presets.rs](https://github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp/blob/main/effects/superduper-kubyz/src/presets.rs)
- Preset harmonic tables (dB relative to harmonic 1, harmonics 1 to 16), from the author's analysis of recordings. "Bashkir": 0.0, 6.6, 19.0, 24.1, 17.0, 38.6, 9.4, 16.7, 15.2, 17.9, 19.9, 9.8, 14.3, -0.5, 7.6, 3.3 with formants 705 / 1301 / 2165 Hz, bandwidths 200 / 300 / 400 Hz, gains 1.0 / 0.9 / 0.75. "Khomus sample": 0.0, 2.6, 17.6, 24.8, 25.4, 31.4, 19.6, 20.0, 7.9, 19.7, 16.4, 16.8, 16.4, 8.0, 16.3, 3.1 with formants 702 / 1365 / 2115 Hz. "Real D2" (fitted to one recording of a D2 harp): 0.0, -33.83, -9.50, -14.71, -16.52, -26.03, -11.07, -20.27, -16.22, -9.19, 27.25, -0.99, 34.42, 20.46, 45.10, 13.55 with formants 809 / 1091 / 1242 Hz and bandwidths 50 Hz each. — [presets.rs](https://github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp/blob/main/effects/superduper-kubyz/src/presets.rs)
- The author's comment on the fitted preset: the fundamental "is *quiet* compared to the 11th–15th partials, which carry the whistling colour"; "mouth-cavity resonances on harmonics 11–15 are the whistling part of the sound"; the 50 Hz bandwidths are "because the cavity resonates on a single harmonic each". — [presets.rs](https://github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp/blob/main/effects/superduper-kubyz/src/presets.rs)
- Analysis tool that produces such presets: f0 by autocorrelation (lags for 40 to 400 Hz), harmonic amplitudes from a Hann-windowed FFT (peak within ±3 bins of n·f0), "formants" as the three strongest peaks of a 50-bin moving average between 200 and 3500 Hz, bandwidth at -3 dB. Stated limits: single steady notes only; "tight resonances on single harmonics will read as 'narrow formants' with the bandwidth equal to one harmonic spacing". — [kubyz_analyser README](https://github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp/blob/main/tools/kubyz_analyser/README.md)
- The author's own verdict on the limits, written as comments (in Russian, my translation) in a test program dated 2026-09-07: with the reed at 68.93 Hz "16 harmonics … end at 1103 Hz"; the factory formant set 705 / 1301 / 2165 "puts TWO of the three above the instrument's entire spectrum"; measured level around F1 = 705 Hz was -28.5 dB, around F2 = 1301 Hz -65.0 dB, around F3 = 2165 Hz -68.8 dB, "95% of the energy below 759 Hz". Conclusion for "additive versus physical model": "a real jaw harp's whistling mouth resonances live well above 1.1 kHz, so 16 harmonics is not an approximation of the instrument but its lower third. Raise to ~48 (3.3 kHz at this tuning) and the question about vowel colour becomes askable." — [kubyz_f2.rs](https://github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp/blob/main/synth-core/examples/kubyz_f2.rs)
- Same file, on the spectrum shape: "The shape of the series is the jaw harp's signature, not a roll-off: maximum at H6 (414 Hz), H1 53 dB quieter than it (the radiator is small relative to the wavelength of 68.93 Hz), and only after the hump comes -12.6 dB/oct." (my translation; these are the author's measurements of his own material, not a published study). — [kubyz_f2.rs](https://github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp/blob/main/synth-core/examples/kubyz_f2.rs)
- Artefacts the code works around: all 16 phases are scattered at note-on "so they don't all start in lockstep and produce a noticeable transient"; a separate 2 ms amplitude ramp and a DC blocker were added against what a user reported as a "light crackle" and "a quiet background rumble"; voice stealing uses a choke fade of about 4 ms. — [voice.rs](https://github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp/blob/main/synth-core/src/kubyz/voice.rs)

### Inferences
(Mine, from the cited findings.)
- The literature gives a source-filter picture with a twist: the "source" is not the lamella's own vibration spectrum but a train of short flow pulses timed by the lamella (Adkins), and the "filter" is the mouth. A linear resonator driven by a pulse train reproduces both of Adkins' regimes without special handling: a slowly decaying (narrow) resonance integrates many pulses and so picks out harmonics; a quickly decaying one rings separately after each pulse and is heard at its own frequency.
- If one pulse is produced on each pass through the frame, there are two pulses per lamella period, of opposite flow direction. A train of alternating-sign pulses at 2·f0 contains only odd harmonics of f0. This would explain by symmetry why three independent sources report odd-harmonic dominance (Li, Nikolsky, Vandré, see section 5), and it predicts that any asymmetry (rest position of the lamella off the frame plane, different gap on the two sides, breath flow) adds even harmonics. Adkins' full text was not read, so this is a hypothesis, not his statement.
- The "Real D2" preset supports the same reading: its three detected "formants", 809, 1091 and 1242 Hz, are 11.04, 14.88 and 16.94 times the stated f0 of 73.3 Hz, i.e. harmonics 11, 15 and 17, all odd; and harmonics 11, 13 and 15 stand 14 to 35 dB above their even neighbours (H11 − H12 = 28.2 dB, H13 − H12 = 35.4 dB, H13 − H14 = 14.0 dB, H15 − H14 = 24.6 dB, H15 − H16 = 31.6 dB; my arithmetic from the table).
- The published code is a signal model, not a physical one: nothing in it depends on lamella dimensions, gap, pluck or airflow. Its two structural limits are the fixed 16-partial source (band-limited to 16·f0, about 1.1 kHz for a bass harp) and formant targets taken from speech vowels instead of from jaw harp playing (sections 4 and 5 give jaw-harp-specific ranges).
- Whether the iOS app runs the same DSP as the Rust code cannot be verified: the Rust module says it was "extracted … so iOS/live2play reuses the exact same engine", but the App Store text lists features ("5 cavity resonance modes", inward / outward articulation) that are not in the Rust voice.

- Ledang 1972 and Crane 1968: not read (paywalled / JSTOR). Leipp's work on the guimbarde (cited by Nikolsky et al. as Leipp 1963 and 1967) could not be located online.

## 2. Models of related systems that transfer, with structure, artefacts and cost

### Takeaway
Everything needed exists as documented building blocks, and the cheap ones fit best: a band-limited pulse train (BLIT / BLEP family, a few operations per pulse) into a handful of time-varying-stable two-pole resonators (state-variable or rotation forms, 2 to 5 multiplies per sample each). The self-oscillating free-reed models (Millot and Baumann; Puranik and Scavone; Adachi et al.) solve a problem the plucked jaw harp does not have (they need an air supply to start and sustain the reed) and are run at 4 × 44.1 kHz with an iterative solver in the one paper that states its settings.

### Cited Findings

#### Free reeds (harmonica, accordion, harmonium, Asian free reeds)
- Review, free-reed sound: the reed "vibrates back and forth across the frame that houses it"; "the airflow rate occurs in two puffs per cycle, each puff corresponding to the reed opening on one or the other side of the reed plate"; the spectrum "has abundant higher harmonics, as the approximate square waveform would suggest"; "the sound quality can be modified by the presence of a resonator". — [Cottingham 2011, Physics Today 64(3), 44-48](https://aip.brightspotcdn.com/PTO.v64.i3.44_1.online.pdf) [JOURN, review by a researcher]
- Reed motion: "the reed motion is almost perfectly sinusoidal during playing because of the non-striking nature" of the free reed. — [Millot and Baumann 2007 (arXiv copy)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.01606) [PR]
- Millot and Baumann, "A proposal for a minimal model of free reeds", Acta Acustica united with Acustica 93 (2007), 122-144: a lumped model intended "to build models of free reed instruments which permit numerical simulations"; a new definition of the airflow section "which takes into account the entire escape area under the reed and the reed thickness"; a minimal configuration that lets both (-,+) and (+,-) reeds go unstable; "the playing frequency of a (+,-) reed can vary in a large range according to the size of the volume upstream of the reed"; playing frequency "nearly independent of the excitation"; "free reeds may present inverse bifurcations"; "the reed thickness can have a great influence on the sound magnitude, the playing frequency and the magnitude of the reed displacement". Discretisation: "Except for the reed motion equation, the numerical equivalents are derived with replacement of derivatives using the backward Euler numerical scheme." Authors' verdict: waveforms and spectra "are realistic compared to real blows and overblows produced on a diatonic harmonica" and with a time-varying excitation "the sound produced is quite realistic for the listener", but "an experimental validation should be done". — [Millot and Baumann 2007 (arXiv copy)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.01606) [PR]
- The Millot-Baumann equations as restated by Puranik and Scavone (restored from damaged text): (1) mass conservation in the upstream volume, (V1 / c0^2)·d(p1 − patm)/dt = ρair·(u0 − u); (2) unsteady Bernoulli in the near-field duct, p1 = p2 + ρair·(L2 / S2)·du/dt; (3) Bernoulli across the reed, p2 = patm + ½·ρair·vj^2; (4) reed as a lumped oscillator, d²ζ/dt² + (ω0 / Q)·dζ/dt + ω0^2·ζ = μ·(p2 − patm); (5) total flow = pumped flow + jet flow, u = Sr·dζ/dt + α·Su·vj, with Su the "useful area of the reed aperture" = Wreed·√((y + Δy)^2 + h^2) (square root restored; h is not defined in the extracted text), α a factor for vena contracta and side apertures, Sr the reed area. — [Puranik and Scavone 2023](https://dael.euracoustics.org/confs/fa2023/data/articles/000853.pdf) [CONF]
- Known weakness of the lumped model, from the same authors' harmonium work: "some model parameters had to assume unrealistic values for a numerically stable synthesis. For example, the thickness of the reed would be several times that of its length and its width"; and "the reed practically had a sinusoidal motion", so the measured growth of reed harmonics with blowing pressure was missing. — [Puranik and Scavone 2023](https://dael.euracoustics.org/confs/fa2023/data/articles/000853.pdf) [CONF]
- Puranik and Scavone, "Clamped bar model for free reeds", Forum Acusticum 2023: the lumped reed is replaced by an Euler-Bernoulli bar clamped at one end (restored): ρr·Ar·∂²y/∂t² + R·∂y/∂t + E·I·∂⁴y/∂x⁴ = Sr·(p2 − patm), with y(0,t) = ∂y/∂x(0,t) = ∂²y/∂x²(L,t) = ∂³y/∂x³(L,t) = 0; material damping terms are neglected "since it is known the brass reeds used in harmonium are lightly damped". Numerics: the implicit scheme of Chaigne and Doutaut for the bar, backward differences for the flow equations, "a time sampling rate of 4 × 44100 Hz", "a grid with N = 40 sections", and "a Newton-Raphson scheme described by Millot". Parameters (Table 1): E = 125 GPa, reed length 16 mm, width 2 mm, thickness 0.4 mm, damping parameter R = 0.065, reed chamber volume V1 = 0.0013 m^3, near-field length L2 = 50 mm and area S2 = 6.4 × 10^-5 m^2, ρair = 1.1769 kg/m^3, ρr = 8490 kg/m^3, initial tip gap Δy = -0.1 mm. — [Puranik and Scavone 2023](https://dael.euracoustics.org/confs/fa2023/data/articles/000853.pdf) [CONF]
- Their measurements (laser vibrometer on a B5, 990 Hz harmonium reed): at about 0.2 kPa "the motion of the reed is largely sinusoidal while the higher harmonic components become more prominent at the higher blowing pressures (1.4-1.6 kPa)"; the measured reed response has peaks near the fundamental (≈ 990 Hz) and the third natural frequency (≈ 16000 Hz) but no clear formant at the second (≈ 6000 Hz); "the effect of the reed could be to filter the driving signal"; the model "still uses arbitrary parameters" for the near- and far-field dimensions. No cost figure and no real-time claim. — [Puranik and Scavone 2023](https://dael.euracoustics.org/confs/fa2023/data/articles/000853.pdf) [CONF]
- Why a free reed self-oscillates at all: "self-sustained oscillations occur without acoustic coupling"; "The excitation force is found to be associated with the inertial load of the unsteady flow through the reed gaps"; radiated sound "is found to be dominated by the dipolar source associated by the strong momentum fluctuations of the flow through the reed gaps". (abstract) — [Ricot, Caussé, Misdariis 2005, JASA 117(4), 2279-2290](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1852546) [PR]
- Fletcher's valve framework: for a valve with one degree of freedom "four simple configurations are possible"; self-oscillation needs particular ranges of inlet and outlet impedance; when the ducts are shorter than a quarter wavelength "oscillation takes place close to that resonance frequency"; the analysis covers "only the initiation of oscillations of small amplitude". (abstract) — [Fletcher 1993, JASA 93(4), 2172-2180](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.406857) [PR]
- Experiment against "a simple nonlinear theory that parametrizes aerodynamic effects by means of a simple damping coefficient together with a contraction coefficient for the flow": acceptable agreement for threshold pressure, amplitude and frequency against reservoir volume; "There are just two parameters in the theory, describing jet contraction and aerodynamic damping". (abstract) — [Tarnopolsky, Fletcher, Lai 2000, JASA 108(1), 400-406](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.429473) [PR]
- Mouth-coupled free reed, the closest structural analogue: a 2026 model "couples reed vibrations, airflow through the reed openings, and the acoustic resonance on the mouth side of a harmonica hole", uses cylindrical tubes of varied length as the "mouth", runs in the time domain and reproduces blow bend, draw bend and overblow. (abstract; no cost figure in it) — [Adachi et al. 2026, JASA 159(5), 4191-4203](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0043784) [PR]
- Player's tract on the harmonica: the reeds "can be made to vibrate at varying frequencies depending on the size and shape of the player's vocal tract". — [Bahnson, Antaki, Beery 1998, JASA 103](https://www.cs.bu.edu/fac/snyder/TablatureWeb/Bahnson%20JASA%201998.pdf) [PR]
- A jaw harp can be sounded as a free reed: held to the lips it will "begin to sound without being plucked, just by breathing in or out" if "the reed has to be shifted a little from the level of the frame, and the air flow has to pull it back"; "In this way the jew's harp works just like a harmonica." — [Vandré, Tones and Pitches](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html) [HOBBY]

- Rhodes / Wurlitzer, FPGA: a real-time physical model "using field programmable gate array (FPGA) hardware"; the Wurlitzer reed "is modeled as a non-uniform cantilever beam with large-deflection effects" plus a pickup transfer function; the Rhodes tine tip "vibrates in an approximately sinusoidal motion" while "the direct-out signal measured behind the pick-up has a more complex waveform, showing the influence of the magnetic field of the pick-up". — [Pfeifle 2017, DAFx-17](https://dafx.de/paper-archive/2017/papers/DAFx17_paper_79.pdf) [CONF]

- Hybrid alternative on the market: the Bastl Kalimba (2026) uses real tines picked up by microphones to excite an internal 6-voice engine that combines physical modelling and FM. (snippet) — [Bastl Instruments](https://bastl-instruments.com/instruments/kalimba) [MKT]

#### Source-filter voice synthesis
- Klatt 1980: formant resonators as second-order difference equations y(nT) = A·x(nT) + B·y(nT − T) + C·y(nT − 2T), with C = −exp(−2π·BW·T), B = 2·exp(−π·BW·T)·cos(2π·F·T), A = 1 − B − C (restored; the extraction prints π as "PI"), by the impulse-invariant transformation; an antiresonator is the mirror image. Cascade configuration for sonorants, parallel for fricatives and bursts; "Five resonators are appropriate for simulating a vocal tract with a length of about 17 cm"; sampling rate 10 000 Hz; 39 control parameters updated every 5 ms; output computed "in nonreal time". — [Klatt 1980, JASA 67(3)](https://www.fon.hum.uva.nl/david/ma_ssp/doc/Klatt-1980-JAS000971.pdf) [PR]
- Klatt's own warning on time variation: "Large sudden changes to these constants may introduce clicks and burps in the synthesizer output. Fortunately, acoustic theory indicates that formant frequencies must always change slowly and continuously relative to the 5-ms update interval". — [Klatt 1980](https://www.fon.hum.uva.nl/david/ma_ssp/doc/Klatt-1980-JAS000971.pdf) [PR]
- STK `VoicForm`: "Four formant synthesis instrument": "an excitation singing wavetable (looping wave with random and periodic vibrato, smoothing on frequency, etc.), excitation noise, and four sweepable complex resonances"; "enough data is there to support either parallel or cascade synthesis". The class comment says cascade is used, but the active `tick()` code feeds the same excitation (voiced source through a one-zero and a one-pole, plus enveloped noise) to four `FormSwep` filters and sums them; the cascade lines are commented out. — [VoicForm.h](https://github.com/thestk/stk/blob/master/include/VoicForm.h) [CODE]
- STK `FormSwep`: "a formant (resonance) which can be 'swept' over time from one frequency setting to another", with sweep rate and target. — [FormSwep.h](https://github.com/thestk/stk/blob/master/include/FormSwep.h) [CODE]
- FOF (Csound `fof`, "loosely based on Michael Clarke's C-coding of IRCAM's CHANT program (Xavier Rodet et al.)"): the output is a stream of sinusoid bursts started at the fundamental rate, each with a spectral peak at the formant frequency and a rise / decay envelope; `kband` is the formant bandwidth at -6 dB; `kris` "inversely determines the skirtwidth (at -40 dB) of the induced formant region"; one generator makes one formant, four or more are summed for a voice; typical vocal values for rise, duration, decay are .003, .02, .007 s; `iolaps` slots must cover the maximum of `xfund * kdur`. Cost statement: "Computation speed depends on kdur, xfund, and the density of any overlaps." — [Csound manual, fof](https://csound.com/manual/opcodes/fof/) [CODE, documentation]
- FOF is also in the Faust library as `fof`, `fofSH`, `fofCycle`, `fofSmooth` and as complete source-filter voices `SFFormantModelFofCycle`, `SFFormantModelFofSmooth`, `SFFormantModelBP` (the last with band-pass filters). — [faustlibraries/physmodels.lib](https://github.com/grame-cncm/faustlibraries/blob/master/physmodels.lib) [CODE]
- VOSIM (Kaegi and Tempelaars, 1970s; Csound `vosim`): each period is a burst of squared-sine pulses followed by silence; pulse length 1/kForm sets the formant, event length 1/kFund the pitch, with a per-pulse decay, pulse count and pulse-width factor. Stated artefacts: "One drawback is that the signal is not band-limited"; changing the pulse count makes steps in the spectrum; the bandwidth "is determined by rate of silence to pulses" and cannot be set independently; "kForm should be higher than kFund, otherwise only silence is output". — [Csound manual, vosim](https://csound.com/manual/opcodes/vosim/) [CODE, documentation]
- Formants directly from summation formulas: "voice synthesis using three vocal formants can be carried out using DSF"; "For K formants, we need 2K DSF units" (Moorer's two-sided variant needs K). — [Stilson and Smith 1996](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~stilti/papers/blit.pdf) [CONF]
- Glottal pulse: Pink Trombone's source is a Liljencrants-Fant waveform computed per sample (`normalizedLFWaveform`); aspiration noise for the glottis is white noise through a band-pass at 500 Hz, Q 0.5, and frication noise for the tract through a band-pass at 1000 Hz, Q 0.5. — [Pink Trombone source](https://dood.al/pinktrombone/) [CODE]

#### Vocal-tract waveguides
- Kelly-Lochbaum: the tract as a chain of cylinders with lossless scattering at each area step, "the digital waveguides are degenerated to single-sample width"; unlike bilinear-transform models "the frequency axis is not warped"; as summarised from the page, the approach "is prone to aliasing when the parameters vary over time (or if nonlinearities are present)". — [J. O. Smith, PASP, Singing Kelly-Lochbaum Vocal Tract](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp/Singing_Kelly_Lochbaum_Vocal_Tract.html) [PR, online book]
- Junction equations: f_i^+(t) = [1 + r_i]·f_{i-1}^+(t − T) − r_i·f_i^−(t); f_i^−(t + T) = r_i·f_{i-1}^+(t − T) + [1 − r_i]·f_i^−(t); r_i = (R_i − R_{i-1}) / (R_i + R_{i-1}), in [−1, 1] (subscripts restored from garbled markup). The one-multiply form needs "only one multiplication and three additions … per junction". — [PASP, Kelly-Lochbaum Scattering Junctions](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp/Kelly_Lochbaum_Scattering_Junctions.html); [PASP, One-Multiply Scattering Junctions](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp/One_Multiply_Scattering_Junctions.html) [PR, online book]

- STK `Modal`: "an excitation wavetable, an envelope, an oscillator, and N resonances (non-sweeping BiQuad filters)". — [Modal.h](https://github.com/thestk/stk/blob/master/include/Modal.h) [CODE]

The library's test list includes a mode swept between 50 Hz and 5 kHz ten times a second and a mode whose frequency jumps. - Mathews and Smith 2003, two-pole filters whose frequency and damping can change every sample: a complex-multiply ("2D rotation") resonator in which "both the frequency and the damping can be changed without producing discontinuities in the filter output"; with exact arithmetic it is "guaranteed to be stable for any value of f"; "Using 64 bit floating point arithmetic seems to be entirely adequate for musical purposes". #### Band-limited pulse and impulse-train generation
- Why it is needed: a pulse train made by rounding pulse times to the sample grid aliases "across the entire frequency band", "highly significant and audible". — [Stilson and Smith 1996, ICMC](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~stilti/papers/blit.pdf) [CONF]
- BLIT closed form: y(n) = (M / P)·Sinc_M[(M / P)·n], Sinc_M(x) = sin(πx) / (M·sin(πx / M)), P the period in samples (not an integer), M "the largest odd integer not exceeding the period" (the number of harmonics). The same paper covers discrete summation formulae (Moorer; Csound's `buzz` and `gbuzz` "implement this method"), BLIT by sums of windowed sincs read from an oversampled table, and bipolar BLITs for square and triangle waves. Integration caveat: with pure integrators numerical errors "accumulate … causing unwanted (and unpredicatble) offsets", so leaky integrators are used. — [Stilson and Smith 1996](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~stilti/papers/blit.pdf) [CONF]
- STK `Blit`: implements that closed form; "If nHarmonics is 0, then the signal will contain all harmonics up to half the sample rate. Note, however, that this setting may produce aliasing in the signal when the frequency is changing (no automatic modification of the number of harmonics is performed by the setFrequency() function)." — [Blit.h](https://github.com/thestk/stk/blob/master/include/Blit.h) [CODE]
- MinBLEP: integrate a minimum-phase windowed-sinc impulse into "a minimum-phase bandlimited step (a MinBLEP)" and mix it in at each discontinuity ("don't just jump to the new level, mix in a MinBLEP"); it needs no integration stage; the underlying table is oversampled and windowed. — [Brandt 2001, ICMC](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~eli/papers/icmc01-hardsync.pdf) [CONF]
- Cost, table-based BLIT / BLEP against polynomial forms: DPW needs "only up to several multiplications and additions per sample"; BLIT with a third-order B-spline fractional-delay filter "requires up to three multiplications and two additions" for each band-limiting sample and "computes only four samples every period"; table-read BLIT-SWS and BLEP "read a lookup table using linear interpolation, which typically involves several multiplications and additions as well as accessing memory twice", for four and eight samples per transition in the compared versions; "the average number of computations per sample is quite small, especially at low fundamental frequencies". The third-order B-spline BLIT is "perceptually free of aliasing within practically used fundamental frequencies". — [Nam, Välimäki, Abel, Smith 2010, IEEE TASLP 18(4)](https://mac.kaist.ac.kr/pubs/jnam-taslp2010.pdf) [PR]
- Cost, polynomial BLEP: additional operations per period of oscillation over the trivial waveform (Table IX, restored from scrambled columns): 2-point PolyBLEP 4 multiplications and 4 additions; 4-point Lagrange PolyBLEP 14 and 15; 4-point B-spline PolyBLEP 13 and 15; plus one multiplication for the fractional delay, or a division "if the fundamental frequency changes from the previous discontinuity". A table BLEP of length K needs "2K reads, 2K + 1 multiplications, and 2K additions". On BLIT: integration "may cause numerical problems when it is implemented with finite precision" (snippet for this last clause). — [Välimäki, Pekonen, Nam 2012, JASA 131(1)](https://mac.kaist.ac.kr/pubs/ValimakiPeknenNam-jasa2012.pdf) [PR]

#### Stability and artefacts when a filter's frequency moves fast
- Requirements and result: the filter should be "time-varying stable, artifact-free, computationally efficient"; "It is proposed to use the state variable filter (SVF) for this purpose. A novel proof of its stable time-varying behavior is presented." The form analysed is the trapezoidal-integrator (bilinear) SVF. — [Wishnick 2014, DAFx-14](https://www.dafx14.fau.de/papers/dafx14_aaron_wishnick_time_varying_filters_for_.pdf) [CONF]
- Which structures fail: "a time-variant filter that has the coefficients of a stable time-invariant filter at each point in time may still be unstable" (after Laroche); "Structures such as Direct-Form II, lattice, and normalized ladder are not necessarily stable when coefficients are changed. Coupled form, also known as normal form or Gold and Rader, is stable in the time-varying case." Even stable structures "can still produce objectionable artifacts". — [Wishnick 2014](https://www.dafx14.fau.de/papers/dafx14_aaron_wishnick_time_varying_filters_for_.pdf) [CONF]
- Listening test (MUSHRA) on rapidly modulated filters: "The results confirm the SVF's good performance, in comparison to other filter structures"; output-switching schemes scored "bad" to "poor"; among the time-varying-stable filters a stabilised transposed direct form II scored best; "a class of time-varying filter techniques useful for speech coding are unsuitable for musical applications". For DC input the SVF produces no switch-time transient in its state. — [Wishnick 2014](https://www.dafx14.fau.de/papers/dafx14_aaron_wishnick_time_varying_filters_for_.pdf) [CONF]
- The trapezoidal SVF derivation commonly used in plugins (capacitor currents by the trapezoidal rule, solved without a unit delay in the feedback loop). Only the title page and first equations of this document survived text extraction. — [Simper (Cytomic) 2013, SvfLinearTrapOptimised2](https://cytomic.com/files/dsp/SvfLinearTrapOptimised2.pdf) [HOBBY, technical note by a developer]
- Shipping code that modulates SVF coefficients at control rate: Rings and Elements recompute g, r and h once per block (2 kHz) with polynomial tangents and run the filter loop above. — [rings/dsp/resonator.cc](https://github.com/pichenettes/eurorack/blob/master/rings/dsp/resonator.cc); [elements/dsp/resonator.cc](https://github.com/pichenettes/eurorack/blob/master/elements/dsp/resonator.cc) [CODE]

### Inferences
(Mine; arithmetic from the cited values, assumptions stated.)
- Plucked versus blown. All the free-reed models above are autonomous oscillators: they exist to explain how steady air pressure starts and sustains the reed. A jaw harp note is a pluck that rings down, so the lamella's motion can be prescribed (a decaying near-sinusoid at f0, which is also what free reeds are measured to do) and only the conversion from lamella position to a flow pulse through the gap needs modelling. That removes the stiff coupled solve (4 × 44.1 kHz, Newton-Raphson, 40 bar sections in Puranik and Scavone) and the numerical-stability problems reported for the lumped model. The flow terms of equations 3 and 5 (aperture area as a function of displacement, pumped flow Sr·dζ/dt) are the parts that transfer.
- Pulse width should follow amplitude. If a pulse lasts while the lamella is inside the frame's thickness h, then for x(t) = A·sin(2π·f0·t) with A much larger than h the crossing takes about Δt = h / (2π·f0·A), a fraction h / (π·A) of each half period. With f0 = 73 Hz and an assumed h = 1 mm (illustration only, no sourced dimension), A = 5 mm gives Δt ≈ 0.44 ms (first spectral null of a rectangular pulse near 2.3 kHz) and A = 1 mm gives Δt ≈ 2.2 ms (null near 460 Hz). So a harder pluck is brighter, and each note darkens as it decays, with no separate filter envelope. This follows from Adkins' mechanism as summarised, not from his equations.
- The lamella's own bending modes cannot be the source of the harmonic series. For a uniform clamped-free bar the Euler-Bernoulli ratios are (4.6941 / 1.8751)^2 = 6.27 and (7.8548 / 1.8751)^2 = 17.55 (standard eigenvalues, quoted from memory, not from a fetched source). Check against Puranik and Scavone's Table 1: f1 = (1.8751^2 / 2π·L^2)·t·√(E / 12ρ) = 968 Hz for L = 16 mm, t = 0.4 mm, E = 125 GPa, ρ = 8490 kg/m^3, against their 990 Hz reed, and 6.27 and 17.55 times that are 6.07 and 17.0 kHz against their "≈ 6000 Hz" and "≈ 16000 Hz". For a 73 Hz uniform lamella the same ratios give about 457 Hz and 1281 Hz; real lamellae are tapered and carry a bent trigger, so the true values differ. These inharmonic modes can at most add a faint "clank" at the pluck (as on the kalimba, where they decay quickly); the harmonic comb comes from the periodic gap flow. Two or three extra resonators would cover them if wanted.
- Cost per voice of a pulse-plus-resonators design, counted from the cited code and tables: stmlib's SVF is 5 multiplies and 7 additions per sample if the products g·hp and g·bp are reused (my count of the five lines quoted above); the Faust mode filter is 3 multiplies; Mathews-Smith forms are 2 or 4; a Klatt direct-form resonator is 3 multiplies and 2 additions but is not safe under fast modulation. A band-limited pulse costs 4 multiplies and 4 additions per pulse with a 2-point PolyBLEP, or four B-spline samples of at most 3 multiplies each. Sixteen voices with five SVF resonators each are 80 filters, about 400 multiplies per sample: 17.6 million per second at 44.1 kHz and 38.4 million at 96 kHz. For scale, Rings runs up to 60 such filters per sample at 48 kHz (14.4 million multiplies per second for the filters alone, same count) on a 168 MHz microcontroller, so the 96 kHz, 16-voice case is 2.7 times one Rings.
- The alternatives cost more for the same job. Additive needs one sine per audible harmonic: Nikolsky et al. count 145 harmonics above the noise floor on a khomus, i.e. 2320 sine oscillators for 16 voices, and the 16-partial compromise is the documented failure of the one published implementation. A Pink-Trombone-style tract is 44 oral plus 28 nasal sections stepped twice per sample: 144 junction updates per sample per voice, 2304 for 16 voices (1408 without the nose), each at least one multiply and three additions, and it is "prone to aliasing" when the shape moves. FOF costs grow with f0 × grain duration (overlaps) and are per formant. VOSIM is cheap but "not band-limited" and cannot set bandwidth independently.
- Coefficient updates for a fast "wow": the formant frequency moves over hundreds of hertz within a note, so the resonators need a structure that is stable and click-free under per-block or per-sample changes: the trapezoidal SVF (proof and listening test in Wishnick) or the Mathews-Smith rotation / waveguide forms. Direct-form biquads are the documented risk (Wishnick on stability, Klatt on "clicks and burps", the Faust note on precision at high sample rates). Updating at a 2 kHz control rate with a polynomial tangent, as Rings and Elements do, bounds the update cost.
- Parallel versus cascade: Klatt uses cascade for vowels because the relative formant amplitudes then come out right automatically, and parallel when amplitudes must be set individually. For a jaw harp the "formants" are few, narrow and individually controlled (melody resonance, accompaniment resonance, a fixed instrumental hump, see section 5), which points to a parallel bank with explicit gains; the one published jaw harp code and STK's `VoicForm` both sum their resonators in parallel.
- Odd / even balance is a one-parameter control in several of these blocks: a bipolar BLIT (difference of two impulse trains half a period apart) gives odd harmonics only, and mixing in a unipolar train restores the even ones; Rings exposes the same idea as separate odd and even outputs and as the POSITION control.

### Gaps
- No source gives measured CPU share or voices-per-core for formant, FOF or Kelly-Lochbaum voices on a current desktop CPU. The only hard figures found are operation counts and the embedded targets (Rings: 60 modes at 48 kHz on an STM32F4 at 168 MHz; Elements at 32 kHz; the Rhodes thesis' default of 5 voices without a CPU percentage; Pfeifle's FPGA).

### Inferences
(Mine.)
- The sample libraries converge on the same control map, which tells what players of these libraries expect: note = one pluck; velocity = pluck strength (and at the top, breath noise); mod wheel = mouth, either as a crossfade between three or so recorded positions or as a filter on top; round robins (4 to 7) against repetition; separate menus for vowels and techniques. Chromatic playing is an add-on built by repitching or by a script, not the default; several packs keep the harp at its recorded pitch.
- The two ways libraries implement the mouth both have a known ceiling. Crossfading between recorded positions cannot produce a continuous formant glide (it fades one fixed formant out and another in), and a filter on top of a recording made with some mouth shape adds a second formant to the one already baked in. A synthesized resonator has neither limit; this is the one thing a synthesis engine can offer that the sampled products cannot.
- Fixed pitch is a recurring practical complaint (tuning knob in MODWHEEL's product, "out-of-key" presets, "mostly tuned to D"), so free choice of the lamella pitch per note is a selling point by itself.
- The "phone at the lips" apps are the cheapest correct model of the mouth: they use the real one. They show that a plain twang source is enough to be recognised once a real mouth filters it, which is consistent with Adkins.

### Gaps
- No press review of any jaw harp library was found (Sound On Sound, KVR and VI-Control were searched; the VI-Control thread "Jaw harp for exs 24" returned HTTP 403). User feedback is limited to the handful of comments quoted above; nothing was found on "machine-gun" repetition or on the "boing" specifically, in praise or in complaint.
- 8Dio details are from search excerpts only (HTTP 429 on every attempt); sample counts per articulation, round-robin depth and control mapping are unknown.
- Mapping details (what velocity and aftertouch do) are not published for Precisionsound, MODWHEEL, GBR Loops, EvA or SwarPlug.

### Inferences
(Mine.)
- The forum consensus (fixed pitch, key tracking moved from the oscillator to the filter) matches how the instrument is played: the keyboard or controller should be able to address the resonance, not only the lamella pitch. It conflicts with the brief that each MIDI note is a pluck at the lamella's fundamental only if no second control (mod wheel, aftertouch, a second key zone, CC74) is given to the mouth.
- All hobby recipes use a sawtooth or similar full-spectrum source. A sawtooth has all harmonics at -6 dB per octave; the cited measurements (section 5) call for odd-harmonic dominance, a weak fundamental and energy out to well above 3 kHz, so the source shape is the first thing these recipes get wrong. A clavinet through a wah, the one "analogue" suggestion, is closer to a pulse train into a single moving band-pass.
- "Formant filter" in these threads means a vowel filter with speech targets. Jaw harp playing uses mainly one strong, narrow, continuously tuned resonance (the second formant region, about 500 to 2000 Hz) plus a weaker low one; speech vowel tables move two broad formants together and do not expose that.

### Gaps
- No tutorial with numbers (filter frequencies, Q, envelope times) was found in any language searched (English, German, Russian, French, Japanese).
- The ScopeUsers thread could only be read as a search excerpt; the VI-Control and MAGIX threads could not be read at all.
- YouTube descriptions were not searchable in depth; a recipe may exist only in a video.

## 5. Perceptual cues: what makes a jaw harp sound real or fake

### Takeaway
The measured signature is specific: a very large number of narrow, low-noise harmonics (about 145 on a khomus, six times a sung vowel), odd harmonics stronger than even, a weak fundamental with the peak several harmonics up, a fixed "instrumental" hump low in the spectrum, and one strong narrow mouth resonance that the player tunes between roughly 0.5 and 2 to 3 kHz, with a second weaker one below it. Glottis and breath are audible controls. No controlled listening study on imitation was found; these cues come from analysis papers, one hobby measurement site and developer notes.

### Cited Findings

#### Spectrum of a played khomus against voice (Nikolsky, Alekseyev, Alekseev, Dyakonova 2020, Frontiers in Psychology 10:3051) [PR]
- Vowel [a] on a khomus: "There are 145 harmonics discernible above the noise floor, peaking at f9"; the same vowel sung (bass) has 23 active harmonics and spoken 19, both peaking at f1. — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- "JH reserves higher intensity for odd harmonics, as opposed to speaking and singing." "The JH's harmonics display a much greater odd/even contrast than singing/speaking vowels do." — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- Narrow partials: "the khomus f2 occupies a range between 141 and 151 Hz, making 117 cents", against 243 to 297 Hz (349 cents) for the sung and 197 to 238 Hz (325 cents) for the spoken second harmonic, measured at the harmonic's baseline level. — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- Low noise: "a much smaller noisiness of the khomus 'A'", visible as the absence of "furry" jiggles in the spectral contour, which are prominent in singing and more so in speech, "especially >f7". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- Summary sentence: "A JH vowel engages six times more 'active' (i.e., salient) harmonics than singing, and almost eight times more than speaking. F1 in the JH is much softer than in speaking and singing. The JH's F2 is much stronger." "JH harmonics are much narrower in bandwidth and are compromised by less noise." — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- Two kinds of formant: "F1 is instrumental; it homogenizes JH articulations and depends mostly on JH's construction. F2 is vocal; it differentiates between JH articulations and depends mostly on the vocal tract configuration." "JH vowels retain F1 at the same level (per instrument), but move vocal formants." — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- Where they sit, in harmonic numbers, on the Yakut khomus analysed: the instrumental formant "falls on f5 in all vowels except 'Ü' and 'I,' where F1 broadens to f3–f5"; the vocal formant falls on "f13 for 'O,' f17 for 'A,' f23 for 'Ö,' f19 for 'Y,' f21 for 'Ä,' and f21-f23 for 'I.'"; "The border between the 'instrumental' and 'vocal' ranges lies ≈f6–f8." — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- Vowels that share a formant position differ in fine structure: "U" differs from "O" by "a smoother shape and consistent dynamic prevalence of odd over even harmonics (>f13)"; "Ü" from "Ö" by "an even greater contrast of odd/even harmonics and louder high harmonics >f47". Each vowel's signature is the number of active harmonics, their envelope, "an odd/even harmonic ratio" and "the harmonic composition of formants". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- Four articulation "poles" and their emphasised bands: "2.4–3.1 kHz for 'front,' 0.3–1.6 kHz for 'back,' 1.2–2.5 kHz for 'high,' and 0.6–0.9 kHz for 'low' vowels"; "back" differs from "low" by stronger harmonics 1 to 4 and "low" has a "much narrower bandwidth of its lowest formant". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- Material: "JHs made of organic materials usually deviate from the harmonic series in the tuning of partials. A JH can also omit a harmonic (usually f2), or generate a non-serial partial or a subharmonic". Texture by material: a grass harp gives "five undifferentiated melodic parts", "A metallic JH generates homophony: a single melody with accompaniment". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- Layers heard in one tradition (Chuvan, frame harps): "(1) the FF; (2) its subharmonic chest resonance an octave below; (3) the middle melodic layer above FF controlled by the player's breathing and tongue position, exhibiting 'talking' qualities; and (4) a high melodic layer". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- Recording and analysis settings behind these figures: 44.1 kHz / 16 bit recorder, iZotope RX with a 262144-sample FFT, Hann window; averages of 4 takes (figure A) and a pool of 15 performances by 3 performers on 3 harps (figure B). — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)

#### Time-domain cue (Adkins 1974) [PR] (abstract)
- With the mouth tuned low the ear hears the lamella's harmonic series, stepping from harmonic to harmonic as the mouth moves; with the mouth tuned high "the dominant sensation of pitch coincides with the natural resonance frequency of the mouth and may be tuned continuously". — [doi:10.1121/1.1914580](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1914580)

#### Hobby measurements by a player (Vandré, Audacity spectra) [HOBBY]
- Example fundamentals: "98 Hz (tone G) or 58.3 Hz (B1)"; another harp tuned A1 at 55 Hz. Partials sit exactly at multiples of the fundamental, with and without the mouth: "the peaks of both curves are at the same frequencies". — [Vandré, Tones and Pitches](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html)
- Without the mouth (frame in a vice): "the even numbered overtones are louder than the odd ones" (his overtone k is harmonic k + 1, so odd harmonics are the stronger ones); he attributes this to "a spring fixed at one end". — [Vandré, Tones and Pitches](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html)
- The mouth as a filter, measured: subtracting the vice spectrum from a played tone leaves a "formant-mountain around 1200 Hz" at the pitch played; tapping the cheek with the same mouth shape and no harp gives a spectrum with the same peak. — [Vandré, Tones and Pitches](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html)
- Ranges: "The most pronounced resonance region is the second formant of the vocal tract, being somewhere in the range from 500 to 2000 Hz"; "The first formant can be anywhere in the range between 250 and 1000 Hz" and gives accompanying notes; "In my experience tones with a frequency of up to about. 2300 Hz can be played"; Regina Plate's book is cited for 500 to 2000 Hz, "corresponding to two octaves". "Several formants can be active at the same time" (example: e3 with g1 underneath, then swapped). — [Vandré, Tones and Pitches](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html)
- A lower-pitched harp gives more playable steps: "With the multiplication tables from 55 you will get more steps in the resonance range between 250 and 2000 Hz than with those from 98 Hz." The 10th overtone is 49 cents off equal temperament. — [Vandré, Tones and Pitches](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html)
- Open against closed tract: with the glottis closed or the tongue closing at the soft palate ("ng"), "The even-numbered overtones … are amplified and can be played especially clearly, but the odd-numbered overtones are not" (in harmonic numbering: the odd harmonics are favoured); with the tract open (some breathing) odd-numbered overtones can be played too. Confirmed by his spectra; "The reason for this is not clear to me so far." Norwegian players alternate open and closed for successive scale tones. — [Vandré, Tones and Pitches](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html)
- Glottis as an effect: when it is closed "in addition to the 'normal' overtones deeper overtones are amplified. Two overtones can be heard simultaneously", and "the opening and closure of the glottis" is itself heard. — [Vandré, Playing Techniques](http://www.rvandre.de/playtech.html)
- Breath: "When breathing in and out, the air stream amplifies the fundamental note"; breathing between plucks gives "rasping sounds". Tongue articulation ("de de de", "doodle") "opens and closes the oral cavity, allowing and interrupting resonance". — [Vandré, Playing Techniques](http://www.rvandre.de/playtech.html)
- Pluck and reed effects: plucking in both directions for fast rhythms; three-finger tremolo; touching the vibrating reed with a nail raises the pitch briefly (mordent); pinching the reed mutes it and raises the pitch; with the lips closed "the reed claps against them" (percussive effect). — [Vandré, Playing Techniques](http://www.rvandre.de/playtech.html)
- Melody is played with the tongue movements of "u" to "i" (as in whistling) in the second-formant region; low accompaniment with "u - o - a - o - u" formed in the throat while the lips stay on the frame. — [Vandré, Playing Techniques](http://www.rvandre.de/playtech.html)

### Inferences
(Mine; arithmetic and readings of the cited findings.)
- Frequencies behind Nikolsky's harmonic numbers. Their second harmonic spans 141 to 151 Hz, so f0 is about 73 Hz (and 1200·log2(151/141) = 119 cents, matching their 117). Then: spectral peak f9 ≈ 660 Hz; instrumental formant f5 ≈ 365 Hz (f3 to f5 ≈ 220 to 365 Hz); vocal formant f13 to f23 ≈ 950 to 1680 Hz; border f6 to f8 ≈ 440 to 585 Hz; 145 harmonics reach about 10.6 kHz. The vocal-formant range agrees with Vandré's 500 to 2000 Hz for the melody resonance, and the instrumental hump with the developer's "maximum at H6 (414 Hz)" on a 68.93 Hz reed.
- Why the border sits there. A resonance of quality Q at frequency F has an amplitude decay time of Q / (π·F). With two pulses per lamella period the gap between pulses is 1 / (2·f0), so the resonance outlasts the gap only for F below about 2·Q·f0 / π. For Q = 10 (the value Fletcher et al. 2006 use for vocal-tract resonances, as quoted in the sibling project's didgeridoo notes, not re-read here) and f0 = 73 Hz this is about 465 Hz, inside Nikolsky's f6 to f8 border. Below it the mouth can only reweight harmonics; above it each pulse rings on its own and the resonance is heard as a pitch that glides. This links Adkins' two regimes to Nikolsky's "instrumental" and "vocal" ranges; it rests on an assumed Q.
- Consequences for an imitation, in order of how often the sources mention them: (1) the source must be bright and wide: energy to at least 3 kHz (Nikolsky's "front" band, Li's 3 kHz peak), better 10 kHz; a 1.1 kHz source was judged inadequate by its own author; (2) odd harmonics above even, with the balance itself a timbre control (it differs between vowels and between open and closed glottis); (3) a weak fundamental and a fixed low hump that does not move with the vowel; (4) one narrow, strong, continuously tunable resonance in roughly 0.5 to 2.3 kHz as the main playing control and a second, weaker one in 250 to 1000 Hz; speech vowel tables (two broad formants moving together) are the wrong control space; (5) partials that are clean and exactly harmonic, with little noise, for a metal harp; noise belongs to the breath, as a separate layer between and under the plucks; (6) a pluck that rings down in the order of half a second to two seconds with no sustain stage; (7) pitch fixed per note with no vibrato; the only pitch gestures reported are momentary rises when the reed is touched or pinched.
- Likely reasons a sampled or simple synthetic jaw harp sounds fake, read off the same cues: a static formant (one recorded mouth shape per sample) where the real resonance is always moving; crossfades instead of glides; a sawtooth-like source with strong even harmonics and a strong fundamental; resonances as broad as speech formants; repitching a sample, which moves the mouth resonance along with the lamella.
- Glottis and breath deserve their own controls because they change which harmonics can be brought out (closed: odd harmonics only; open: both) and add the low "accompaniment" resonance, the rasp and the 3 kHz peak. A plain two-state switch between "open" and "closed" behaviour would already cover what Vandré and Li measured.

### Gaps
- No perceptual experiment (listening test, discrimination or realism rating) on jaw harp imitation was found. The cue list is assembled from analysis and from makers' practice, so the ranking in the inferences is an argument, not a result.
- Nikolsky et al. analyse sustained "vowels" of one instrument type (Yakut khomus) for a paper on music cognition; attack transients, decay times, pluck noise and level dependence are not measured there. The supplementary appendix with material-dependent deviations was not read; nor was their Russian-language series on materials (the PDF link returned an HTML page).
- Decay time and its dependence on mouth coupling for steel harps: no measurement found (only the bamboo kumbing figures from a course wiki without method).
- The odd / even question has three explanations in the sources and none is settled: the pulse symmetry argued in section 1 (inference), Vandré's "spring fixed at one end" (a hobby explanation), and the closed-tube behaviour of the tract when the glottis is closed (his measurement, reason "not clear" to him). The UBC wiki states the opposite ordering.
- Li's mouth-resonance measurement did not match his harp spectra and the peaks he lists near 3 to 4.2 kHz are far above the 0.5 to 2 kHz melody range of the other sources; whether these are tract resonances, a property of the harps or an artefact is open.
