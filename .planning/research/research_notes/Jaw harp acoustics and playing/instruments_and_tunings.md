## 1. Typology and regional types: construction, dimensions, materials, mass, how held and plucked

### Takeaway
Two families exist. Heteroglot bow-shaped harps (a steel reed fixed to a forged, bent or cast frame, pressed against the teeth, reed tip plucked by a finger) have frames of about 50–145 mm, reeds of 45–105 mm and masses of 7–115 g in current production (giants reach 230 mm and 500 g); idioglot lamellate harps (reed cut from one strip of bamboo, palm or sheet brass, held at the lips) are about 30–340 mm long, weigh 2–12 g in brass, and are sounded by plucking the frame end beyond the reed's hinge or by jerking a string. Size and shape alone do not fix the sound: reed material, stiffness and the reed-to-frame gap do.

### Cited Findings

Classification and terms
- [AUTHORS' INTERPRETATION] Idioglot = "the tongue or lamella is cut from the frame itself"; heteroglot = "the lamella is made separately from the frame, and then attached and adjusted to it". Sound is produced by the lamella vibrating between "two parallel arms of the frame, and the vibration of the lamella is initiated by plucking, tapping or pulling on a string." — [Kolltveit 2016](https://musark.no/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/J-Harps-of-Bone-Wood-and-Metal.Kolltveit-2016.pdf) [R]
- [AUTHORS' INTERPRETATION] Sachs's two groups are frame harps (Rahmenmaultrommeln; organic materials and sheet metal) and bow-shaped harps (Bügelmaultrommeln; iron and copper alloy). "Bow-shaped jew's harps are always heteroglot; frame jew's harps are usually idioglot, but not always": the Taiwanese lubu has "one or multiple separate tongues, usually made of brass or copper, that are secured to a bamboo frame with a fine thread", and on Enggano frame and lamella are both metal. Dournon-Taurelle and Wright instead distinguish "type à languette encadrée (LEC), where the lamella is shorter than the frame" and "type à languette hors du cadre (LHC), where the lamella is longer than the frame, and additionally bent out from the plane of the frame". — [Kolltveit 2016](https://musark.no/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/J-Harps-of-Bone-Wood-and-Metal.Kolltveit-2016.pdf) [R]
- [CLAIMED] Hornbostel–Sachs 121.22 "Heteroglot guimbarde (the lamella is attached to the frame)"; harps are further sorted by frame shape (rod or plaque), number of tines, and "whether the tines are plucked, joint-tapped, or string-pulled". — [Wikipedia, Jew's harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew%27s_harp) [F]
- [CLAIMED] "Two forms are in practical use today: frame jaw harps, by far the most common, and lamellate jaw harps. Their principal difference is how they are held. A frame jaw harp is pressed against and supported by the teeth. A lamellate jaw harp is held freely in the hand and only lightly enclosed by the lips to obtain an effective volume." — [Oberton Pro, Choosing a Jaw Harp](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/choice.htm) [R]
- [CLAIMED] "First, a jaw harp's appearance tells you almost nothing about its sound. In simplified terms, size, shape, colour, material and manufacturing method do not individually determine the acoustic result." "All else being equal, a long reed will produce a lower sound than a short one. Put a much stiffer metal in the long reed, however, and it may sound higher than the short, soft reed." — [Oberton Pro, Choosing a Jaw Harp](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/choice.htm) [R]
- [CLAIMED] "The differences depend more on the maker, model and individual instrument than on geography." — [Oberton Pro FAQ](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/faq.htm) [R]

How heteroglot harps are built (general)
- [AUTHORS' INTERPRETATION] Reed attachment: most commonly the reed is set "in a groove, ground or cast in one surface of the frame" and clamped by hammering the groove edge over it; "more rarely, a slot is cut in the back of the frame, into which the end of the tongue is inserted and is then held in place by a metal wedge" (so a broken tongue can be replaced; Norwegian hand-made harps); or a screwed block, or a riveted plate. Frame: "bending a strip of iron wire, either square or hexagonal in section, into most of a circle or ovoid and then into two parallel arms", or cast. Reed: "spring or hammered steel, either parallel-sided ... or more often tapering from the fixed end to the tip"; "the end is bent up, usually at a slightly obtuse angle, and the tip is either curved over or bent round in a ring". — [Montagu 2020, pp. 26–27](https://www.jeremymontagu.co.uk/Trumps%20(Jews%20Harps)%20-%20Jeremy%20Montagu.pdf) [R]
- [AUTHORS' INTERPRETATION] "Most of the nineteenth-century and later English trumps had cast iron frames, and as a result they were poorly performing trumps because it was difficult to control the gap between reed and frame in this way. Most German and Austrian, and almost, perhaps all, hand-made trumps are made from forged or bent frames." — [Montagu 2020, p. 27](https://www.jeremymontagu.co.uk/Trumps%20(Jews%20Harps)%20-%20Jeremy%20Montagu.pdf) [R]
- [CLAIMED] "Tongues of 99.9% jaw harps are made of steel"; "Jaw harp frame material is not significant at all compared to the tongue material. The majority of frames are made of steel or brass". Forging "alone does not make a jaw harp better or worse"; a screw-mounted reed is not better than a riveted one. — [Oberton Pro FAQ](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/faq.htm) [R]
- [CLAIMED] "wood cannot provide the necessary accuracy and structural stability for a modern frame jaw harp. Put simply, the instrument should be metal; the particular metal is much less important." — [Oberton Pro, Choosing a Jaw Harp](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/choice.htm) [R]

Tuva: demir-khomus and the simple wooden types
- [MEASURED] Two forms: plate-shaped "fork" (пластинчатый «вилкообразный») and rod-bent arc-shaped (стержневой дугообразный). Traditional plate-shaped demir-khomus: length 6.0–7.5 cm; cheeks (arms) 4.5–5.3 cm long, narrowing; tongue conical, from 0.4 cm wide at the fixing to 0.1 cm at the bend; the bent end ("knee") 2.0–3.0 cm. Arc-shaped ones in museum collections 4.4–9.8 cm, most commonly 5.0–7.0 cm; a rarer horseshoe-based variant 5.1–5.9 cm. Individual museum pieces: 7.0 × 1.3, 7.1 × 1.1, 9.8 × 1.5, 6.7 × 1.0, 6.2 × 1.0, 7.2 × 0.9, 8.4 × 0.9 cm; a set of five plate-shaped ones 6.0, 6.1, 6.3, 6.5, 6.5 cm. — [Tiron 2025](https://fehu.philology.nsc.ru/journals/ykns/pdf/YFKNS_55/2025_%E2%84%963_04.pdf) [R]
- [MEASURED] Katanov 1889 (earliest record): tongue length 1¾ vershok (7.8 cm), base width ½ vershok (2.2 cm), width at the narrowing of the arms ¼ vershok (1.1 cm), base thickness 1/16 vershok (0.3 cm); "the thick end is taken in the left hand ... the wire is touched with the index finger of the right hand; the narrow end is clenched by the teeth". Aksenov 1964: a tuning-fork-shaped iron, sometimes brass or copper fork 5 to 6 cm long, 1 cm wide at the base and 0.5 cm at the top, with a thin steel rod vibrator whose bent end is 2 cm long. — quoted in [Tiron 2025](https://fehu.philology.nsc.ru/journals/ykns/pdf/YFKNS_55/2025_%E2%84%963_04.pdf) [R] (my translation)
- [AUTHORS' INTERPRETATION] Folk-made arc khomuses of the 1980s: body from a nail, tongue from a thick felting needle; one maker used truck piston rings, steel rulers or a scythe blade for tongues. "Чем тверже металл, тем ярче звук хомуса" (the harder the metal, the brighter the sound). Sizes vary because playing was solo; ensemble playing later required tuning the instruments to each other. A special technique turns the trigger into the mouth and moves it with the tongue; it needs a khomus with a shortened tongue. Newer techniques strike with all fingers, towards and away from the player. — [Tiron 2025](https://fehu.philology.nsc.ru/journals/ykns/pdf/YFKNS_55/2025_%E2%84%963_04.pdf) [R]
- [DESCRIBED] "In Tuva ... simple wooden jaw harps (Tuvan: iyash khomus) are made from a Y-shaped stick without the need for any tool ... the player grips the single end of the stick in the mouth while firmly holding one branch of the 'Y' and vibrating the other branch of the 'Y' with the fingers of the free hand." — [Nyshanov 2016](https://musc102.blogs.wesleyan.edu/files/2022/02/The-Music-of-Central-Asia_Chapters-15-16.pdf) [R]
- [CLAIMED] Tuvan usage groups under "khomus" the demir-khomus, the kulzun-khomus, the yyash-khomus (a simple twig) and the charty-khomus (a wood chip), per an article by Leo Tadagawa. — [search excerpt of Tadagawa's article on cyberleninka.ru](https://cyberleninka.ru/article/n/the-khomus-is-my-red-deer-on-which-i-fly-through-the-middle-world-khomus-in-the-shamanic-practice-of-tuva-research-issues) (snippet)

### Inferences (mine)
- Three ways of exciting the reed occur, and they differ in what the hand touches: (1) finger on the bent reed tip (all heteroglot bow harps; towards the player, away, or both); (2) finger pluck or tap on the frame end beyond the hinge, the reed itself untouched (đàn môi, kubing, Thai harps, kouxian, karinding); (3) a jerk on a string tied near the reed's base (genggong, mukkuri, jygach ooz komuz, Taiwanese harps, Melanesian tube harps). For a synthesizer, (1) allows two stroke directions with different noise (see part 4), (2) and (3) give one kind of attack only.
- Typical current steel harps, taken from the shop table: frame 80–110 mm, reed 60–90 mm, 20–50 g. Mini models are 50–75 mm and 7–20 g; bass and "designer" bronze models reach 110–145 mm and 100–115 g. Museum pieces of the 17th to 19th centuries are smaller (36–87 mm).
- The Montagu "width of gap" for the Schwarz harps (2–4 mm) is the width of the slot between the arms, not the clearance on either side of the reed; clearances are two orders of magnitude smaller (part 5).

- Kazakh shan-kobyz: only the name was found (ru.wikipedia lists шанкобыз without description). No dimensions, no maker data.

- Jofen (Molln): no catalogue, sizes or tunings found. Schwarz: only third-party size lists.
- Lamella thickness is almost never published for steel harps; only for sheet-brass idioglots (0.3–1.5 mm, Montagu) and bamboo (Met mukkuri). Reed width at the root is given by Montagu for nine harps (3.3–6.5 mm).

- Sicilian marranzano and Hutsul drymba: no measured dimensions found.

## 2. Pitch: which fundamentals instruments have

### Takeaway
Steel harps in current catalogues run from A0 (27.5 Hz) to E3 (164.8 Hz); the bulk sits between about 60 and 85 Hz (the median of 130 measured models is 70 Hz) and the note most often stocked or recommended is C2 to E2 (65–82 Hz). Brass multi-reed kouxian are sold higher, C3 to D#4. Traditions name pitch classes without octaves (Alpine A and E, Norwegian E, A, G, D, F, Kyrgyz A and E); the Tuvan fundamental is put in the "small (or great) octave", and the South Indian morsing is the one type conceived as high-pitched and tuned to the singer's tonic.

## 3. How pitch is adjusted, and by how much

### Takeaway
Makers lower pitch by adding mass at the tip (solder or tin, a soldered ball, copper wire, beeswax, clay, wax, a clip-on magnet) or by thinning the reed near its root, and raise it by shortening or lightening the reed or changing the angle of the bent end. Only one source states an amount: a clip-on magnet that lowers a Bashkir kubyz "by 5 steps"; all others say "slightly".

### Cited Findings
- [CLAIMED] "Basic principle: the frequency of sound is the frequency at which the reed vibrates. Imagine a pendulum ... a) By changing the mass: more - slower (sound lower), less - faster (sound higher). The most common way is to put a drop of tin on the tip of the tongue or in the corner of the knee and gently grind it down, looking at the tuner ... b) By changing the length ... you can imitate this by changing the angle of the knee. It is safer and most convenient when it is 90 degrees ... Bend it very carefully, a few degrees ... More than 30 degrees is better for such an angle not to change, it may break. ... c) ... you can reduce the stiffness of the reed by sharpening it as close to the base as possible - it will become softer, slower, the frequency will decrease." — [Oberton Pro FAQ](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/faq.htm) [R]
- [CLAIMED] "A metal ball may be added to provide the mass needed for a particular tuning; if the instrument reaches the intended note without it, the maker may leave it off." Szilágyi dorombs: "Randomly it can be weighted with tin, or copper wire"; "Round elbow tip can be present or not - it is just a tuning weight, not part of geometry." — [Oberton Pro FAQ](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/faq.htm), [ZS102](https://www.oberton.pro/en/shop/zs102-new-siberia-doromb.html), [ZS69](https://www.oberton.pro/en/shop/zs69-baikal-doromb.html) [R]

- [AUTHORS' INTERPRETATION] Genggong: gambir (red clay) is softened and "a small piece can be stuck to the free end of the instrument's tongue, at the point where it narrows. It is safer to tune this way, as removing any material from the instrument itself creates changes that cannot be undone." — [Morgan 2008](https://open.library.ubc.ca/collections/24/items/1.0066561) [R]
- [MEASURED] Wax weights seen on collection pieces: a New Guinea reed "loaded with black wax at one point for tuning"; a Malay-type harp with "a tuning-wax weight on the outer side at the end of the wider section of the reed just before the shoulder". — [Montagu 2020, pp. 11, 16](https://www.jeremymontagu.co.uk/Trumps%20(Jews%20Harps)%20-%20Jeremy%20Montagu.pdf) [R]
- [CLAIMED] "Die Tonhöhe des Grundtons wird in erster Linie bestimmt durch die Länge, Dicke, Härte und Form der Feder"; the reed's width mainly affects torsional vibrations. — [Wikipedia (de), Maultrommel](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maultrommel) [F]. "Výšku základního tónu udává délka a hmotnost jazýčku"; fine tuning "i změnou úhlu ohybu v jeho kolínku". — [Wikipedia (cs), Brumle](https://cs.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brumle) [F]
- [DESCRIBED] Norwegian wedge-mounted tongues are replaced by the smith in the key the owner asks for, "for around GB £10". — Folkestad in [Montagu 2020, p. 48](https://www.jeremymontagu.co.uk/Trumps%20(Jews%20Harps)%20-%20Jeremy%20Montagu.pdf) [R]
- [CLAIMED] The maker's Hz "depends greatly on temperature and humidity". — [doromb.com tuning chart](https://www.doromb.com/shop_ordered/27095/pic/doromb-hangolas-chart-HU.pdf) [R]

### Inferences (mine)
- "5 steps" is not defined on the page. If it means five semitones, the factor is 2^(−5/12) = 0.749, so a 95 Hz kubyz would drop to about 71 Hz; if it means scale steps the drop is larger. Either way a tip load can move the fundamental by several semitones, far more than the "very slightly" said of wax.
- Retuning from A = 440 to A = 432 is 1200 × log2(440/432) = 31.8 cents down, which the shop calls within the "small range" any harp can be retuned.
- The Classic FM wording that Blu Tack "sharpens" a note is most likely a journalist's slip: every other source, and the mass rule itself, has added mass lowering the pitch.

### Gaps
- No source gives cents or Hz per gram of added wax or solder, and none says how far filing raises a morsing.
- Whether temperature and humidity shift a steel reed measurably (the doromb.com remark) is not quantified anywhere I found.

## 4. The categories makers and players use, and what suits fast rhythmic versus slow overtone playing

### Takeaway
The working vocabulary is pitch (bass, tenor, soprano), timbre (smooth and "clean" versus crackling, "saturated", buzzing), reed stiffness (soft to hard, measured by one shop as 20–500 gf per 10 mm of tip travel), "mechanics" (how cleanly the reed and its bent end take strokes in both directions), sensitivity, volume and sustain. For fast rhythmic playing the sources recommend high pitch, a tight or stiff reed, a short stiff bent end and tolerance of strong two-way strokes, accepting a saturated timbre and shorter sustain; for slow overtone playing they recommend low to medium pitch, long sustain, a soft reed and above all a "clean" timbre with evenly loud harmonics, which goes with narrow, even gaps.

### Cited Findings

The vocabulary
- [CLAIMED] Seven properties: pitch, timbre, reed stiffness, mechanics, sensitivity, volume, construction. "As a rule, a lower jaw harp is slower and more inertial, while a higher one lends itself to fast playing. A middle register is usually best for general use and for a first instrument. The very lowest jaw harps demand exceptional manufacturing accuracy if they are to work across a broad overtone range ... Simple models are often limited in their upper harmonics and sound dull. Lower instruments generally respond well to deep breathing ...; higher instruments reveal very small articulatory movements more clearly." — [Oberton Pro, Choosing a Jaw Harp](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/choice.htm) [R]
- [CLAIMED] Timbre: "At opposite ends of one useful spectrum are rough, crackling voices and smooth voices. A crackling instrument may initially seem more unusual and interesting, but too much noise can obstruct deliberate control. A smooth instrument asks more confidence from the player". Stiffness "determines the tactile sensation of playing ... It mainly tells you how firmly the instrument must be held and how much striking force it expects." Mechanics: "Some work best slowly and gently; others reward fast, precise movement. Mechanical quality also includes the ability to retain a clean sound under strong or varying strikes and in both directions." Sensitivity: "The same striking force can produce very different volume on different jaw harps." — [Oberton Pro, Choosing a Jaw Harp](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/choice.htm) [R]
- [CLAIMED] "Musicality" or "purity" is "the uniformity of the loudness of each note (harmonic overtone) that the jaw harp emits, and the discernibility of notes against the background of many random sounds". ### Inferences (mine)
- The shop's stiffness figure converts to a spring constant: 100 gf per 10 mm = 0.981 N / 0.010 m = 98 N/m. The catalogue range 20–500 gf is therefore about 20–490 N/m, with most frame harps at 50–160 gf (49–157 N/m).
- An order-of-magnitude effective tip mass follows from m = k / (2πf)², treating the static tip stiffness as the modal stiffness (which it only approximates): Iremel (150 gf, 50 Hz) about 1.5 g; a typical 100 gf, 70 Hz harp about 0.5 g; mini đàn môi (90 gf, 112 Hz) about 0.18 g; Matilda (25 gf, 65 Hz) about 0.15 g. Stiff and low together mean a heavy reed (hard to stop, strong push on the finger); soft and low means a light, slow reed that is easily overdriven.
- Read together, the sources give two profiles a preset designer can use. "Rhythm" profile: fundamental from about 75 Hz up to 165 Hz (or a loud 60–85 Hz Glazyrin-type harp), reed 120–190 gf, short bent end, saturated buzzing spectrum, short to medium decay, equal loudness on both stroke directions. "Overtone" profile: 27–75 Hz, reed 20–90 gf (or a stiff but long bass reed as on the Iremel), long decay, clean spectrum with evenly loud harmonics, one stroke direction, quieter. Oberton's own summary ("lower ... slower and more inertial", "higher ... fast playing") and the doromb.com chart agree; no source contradicts it.
- "Saturated", "oversaturated", "mechanical" and "crackling" are used by the shop for the same thing: strong broadband and inharmonic content that masks individual harmonics. "Clean", "smooth", "even" mean harmonics of similar loudness with little noise. The first is what the shop pairs with percussive use, the second with melody.

### Gaps
- Almost all of this vocabulary comes from two commercial sources (Oberton Pro, doromb.com) and one thesis. Players' forums and video descriptions were not reached; the Oberton forum was not read.
- No source states pluck rates or tempi that a given harp can sustain.
- danmoi.com product texts (a large body of such descriptions) are known only from excerpts.

## 5. Gap clearance between lamella and frame arms

### Takeaway
Published clearances run from 10 µm per side on the tightest modern Russian harps to 100–300 µm on Altai, Indian and bamboo harps and 0.5–1.5 mm on poor factory copies; one experimental result (Ledang) puts the upper limit for distinct partials at 0.2 mm. Tight gaps are said to make a harp louder, more sensitive and richer in high partials, and to make it clank when the frame is squeezed.

### Cited Findings
- [MEASURED] Gaps by type (ranges across specimens): Glazyrin workshop models 10–20 µm or 10–80 µm; other Russian makers 10–100 µm (most between 20 and 70); Szilágyi dorombs 30–150 µm; Vietnamese thin brass đàn môi 10–50 µm; Khakassian and Kyrgyz 30–100 µm; Yakut khomuses mostly 40–150 µm (one model 10–100, one 80–200); Altai komuses 100–300 µm; Indian morchangs 150–250 µm; Czech brumle 30–50 and 100–150 µm; bamboo đàn môi 100–300 µm; megakhomus 300–350 µm; factory "Chinese" frame harps 500–1500 µm. "Gaps under load" (the calculated gap left when the harp is held against the teeth with minimum force) run from 7 µm upward, 7 to about 300 µm for everything except the factory "Chinese" harps. — [Oberton Pro measurement table](https://www.oberton.pro/en/lab.htm) [R]
- [AUTHORS' INTERPRETATION] Ola Kai Ledang "noticed that makers and players of jew's harps know that the arms of the frame are as important as the lamella, and that the distance between the inner edges of the arms and the lamella should be as small as possible in order to make the instrument sound well. Through a series of experiments he demonstrated that this distance has to be very small, not exceeding 0.2 millimetres, to produce distinct partials." Crane likewise held that "the parallel ends of the frame should enclose the lamella tightly, and that the lamella alone cannot produce a harmonic series of partials". — [Kolltveit 2016, citing Ledang 1972 and Crane 1968](https://musark.no/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/J-Harps-of-Bone-Wood-and-Metal.Kolltveit-2016.pdf) [R]
- [CLAIMED] "The gaps between the reed and the decks must be minimal and perfectly even." "A gap of 0.5 mm may look very small, but only by the standards of a tram door." "Gap width affects maximum volume, the ease with which that volume is reached, and the breadth of the spectrum. Smaller gaps produce greater volume and a broader spectrum. Larger gaps produce a quieter, duller sound in which mostly low components remain. The requirement becomes less severe as the instrument's fundamental frequency falls: on a very low jaw harp, the difference between 20 and 100 micrometres may be modest, while on a high instrument it is enormous." — [Oberton Pro, Advice for Jaw Harp Makers](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/makingtips.htm) [R]
- [CLAIMED] "Even gaps and parallel edges, together with the material and geometry, suppress isolated unwanted noises and determine overall musicality"; uneven gaps give "buzzing that swallows or masks individual harmonics and gives the entire timbre a mechanical colour". The last part of the gap matters most: "volume depends on the speed at which a part of the reed passes the decks, and that speed is greatest at the free end ... Maximum attention should therefore go to the final part of the gaps". — [Oberton Pro, Advice for Jaw Harp Makers](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/makingtips.htm) [R]
- [CLAIMED] "gaps size is the first level of technical jaw harp quality and the smaller they are the better the sound (volume/sensitivity is higher and spectrum is wider). But smaller gaps increases requirements for handling accuracy"; "Wide gaps decrease sensitivity of the instrument, its loudness and spectral width." — [Oberton Pro FAQ](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/faq.htm) [R]
- [CLAIMED] Clanking: "The jews harp tongue touches the frame because the player himself presses the frame too hard. The instruments having tiny gaps ... and soft frame, are prone to clanking." Causes listed: squeezing the prongs with the hand, pressing too hard on the teeth, squeezing with the lips, moving the lower jaw, an oblique stroke. Frame stiffness (force to close the gaps) ranges in the table from 40–120 gf (a thin Szilágyi frame) to several thousand gf; "In such cases frame stiffness may be insufficient, and metal clank will appear as the tongue will touch the frame." — [Oberton Pro, How to hold](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/handling/) and [FAQ](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/faq.htm) [R]
- [CLAIMED] "Beginning makers often say that narrow gaps are impossible because the jaw harp will clang. The answer is to learn to hold it accurately". — [Oberton Pro, Advice for Jaw Harp Makers](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/makingtips.htm) [R]
- [AUTHORS' INTERPRETATION] "It is essential that the gap between the reed and its frame is as narrow as possible so that the vibration of the reed is coupled to that of the player's mouth." A Nepalese tourist harp is "useless because the reed is only 1.45 mm wide all the way down ... for the gap between the arms, which is between 6.3 and 3.7 mm wide". Thai and Cambodian bamboo harps have adjustable "wings" beside the narrow part of the reed "to control the width of the gap". — [Montagu 2020, pp. 6, 19–20, 33](https://www.jeremymontagu.co.uk/Trumps%20(Jews%20Harps)%20-%20Jeremy%20Montagu.pdf) [R]
- [CLAIMED] "je enger der Abstand zwischen Feder und Bügeln ist, desto besser ist der Obertongehalt". — [Wikipedia (de), Maultrommel](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maultrommel) [F]
- [CLAIMED] "The gap between the spring and the arms are set very precise, therefore protect it from any serious physical impact." The Black Fire Hard needs "egy kis megszokást" (a little getting used to) "a nagyon precízen beállított légrés miatt" (because of the very precisely set air gap). "A szűk rés megakadályozza, hogy a levegő nyomása gyorsan kiegyenlítődjön" (the narrow gap prevents the air pressure from equalising quickly); the pressure difference is put at 1% of atmospheric pressure (0.01 bar). — [doromb.com manual](https://www.doromb.com/shop_ordered/27095/pic/doromb-users_manual-UK.pdf) [R], [Black Fire Hard](https://www.doromb.com/Black-Fire-Hard) [F], [doromb.com, about the doromb](https://www.doromb.com/dorombrol) [F]
- [CLAIMED] A retailer listing of a Glazyrin harp cites "precision gaps ranging from 10-80 microns"; a buyer writes that reed and frame are so close that "it's super easy for even pros to clatter". — desertcart listing (snippet)
- [CLAIMED] Screw-mounted reeds: "On instruments with very small gaps, even a slight change in alignment after loosening and retightening the screws can make the reed hit the frame or change the timbre." — [Oberton Pro FAQ](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/faq.htm) [R]

### Inferences (mine)
- Ledang's 0.2 mm limit and the shop's table agree: every harp the shop praises for overtones is at or below about 100 µm, and the types it calls dull or souvenir-grade (Altai komus 100–300 µm, bamboo 100–300 µm, factory "Chinese" 500–1500 µm) are at or above the limit.
- For a synthesizer the gap is thus a timbre and loudness control with a floor and a failure mode: narrower gap gives more level and more high partials for the same stroke, and beyond a threshold of stroke force or "grip" the reed strikes the frame (a metallic clank). The shop's remark that gap width matters more for high harps than for low ones suggests scaling the effect with the fundamental.
- The two hundredfold spread between "gaps" and Montagu's "width of gap" (2–4 mm) is a difference of definition: the first is clearance on each side of the reed, the second the slot between the arms.

### Gaps
- Ledang 1972 was not read; the 0.2 mm figure is quoted through Kolltveit. Adkins 1974 and Leipp 1963 were not obtained.
- No independent (non-vendor) measurement of clearances on named instruments was found, and the vendor does not say how it measures them.
- No source states the clearance of Molln harps or of Norwegian munnharper.

## 6. Sustain and loudness: claims and measurements

### Takeaway
I found no sustain time in seconds for any named metal harp, from a maker or a measurement. The only timed figure is a student recording of a bamboo kumbing (about 0.4 to 0.7 s). Everything else is ordinal: bass harps and soft long reeds ring long, stiff short reeds and the morsing ring short, a reed bent out of plane rings shorter, and the sound of a harp taken off the teeth "almost immediately fades". Loudness is given only in words ("barely audible beyond a few meters").

### Cited Findings

### Inferences (mine)
- Ordering that the sources support, from longest to shortest ring: very low soft-reed bass harps (Tibet A0–C1, Suntar, Borivoy) > medium steel harps with tight gaps > high stiff harps (Pripyat) and the morsing > bamboo and plastic lamellate harps (kumbing about half a second). No source lets me attach seconds to the first three classes; any decay times in the synthesizer's programs for metal harps will have to come from recordings, not from the literature gathered here.
- Sustain as heard is not a property of the reed alone: the frame must be coupled to the teeth (Trident remark), and a mistuned rest position of the reed shortens it (the "buzz" article). Both are candidate performance controls.

### Gaps
- No sustain time in seconds for any named model from Glazyrin, Szilágyi, Molln, Yakut makers or Schlütter; searches in English and Russian ("sustain", "послезвучие", "секунд") returned only qualitative wording.
- No sound pressure level in dB for any jaw harp was found.
- The kumbing figures come from an unsigned student wiki and were read through an extract; treat as low reliability.

## 7. Multiple harps and multiple reeds: changing the fundamental within a piece

### Takeaway
Players get more than one fundamental in four ways: swapping between two to four separate harps held in the hands or joined by a clip (Alpine practice; pairs a fourth or fifth apart, trios on tonic, dominant and subdominant), sets of 8 or 13 harps covering a diatonic or chromatic octave, several reeds on one body (kouxian with 3 to 5 leaves, Taiwanese harps with 4 reeds, two-reed and three-reed modern harps), and ensembles in which each player holds a different pitch (Kyrgyz trios, Balinese lanang–wadon pairs). A clip-on magnet is a fifth, lesser route.

### Cited Findings
- [CLAIMED] Austria: "an alternating technique, in which a player switches between two to four differently tuned Jew's harps"; played "either as a solo instrument or in a duet or trio, but most frequently in combination with other instruments". — [Austrian Commission for UNESCO, Jew's harp playing in Austria](https://www.unesco.at/en/culture/intangible-cultural-heritage/national-inventory/news-1/article/jews-harp-playing-in-austria) [F]
- [CLAIMED] "Um den Tonumfang zu erweitern, wechseln Musiker im Spiel oft zwischen mehreren in den Händen gehaltenen Maultrommeln"; in the Austrian manner the harps are tuned to tonic, dominant and subdominant; example on the notes A and E. — [Wikipedia (de), Maultrommel](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maultrommel) [F]
- [CLAIMED] Wimmer-Bades sells tuned pairs A+E, G+D, C+F and trios A+E+D, G+C+F (the trio text also names A+E+G, G+D+C, C+F+A) with a wooden connecting clip, for "Spiel mit mehreren Maultrommeln". — [Wimmer-Bades](https://www.maultrommel.at/produkte/gestimmte-maultrommel/) [F]. Schwarz also sells a "Connecting Clip for Jaw Harp". — [Thomann](https://www.thomann.co.uk/schwarz_jews_harp_fun_nr._12.htm) [F]
- [CLAIMED] Szilágyi: "Joyn" connector for two or more dorombs, which sets them at a 10 degree angle to each other; sets "Black Fire - 1 oktáv diatonikus (8 db)" tuned "A1, H1, C2, D2, E,2 F,2 G2, A2" and "1 oktáv kromatikus (13 db)". — [doromb.com Joyn](https://www.doromb.com/joyn), [diatonic set](https://www.doromb.com/Black-Fire-1-oktav-diatonikus-8-db), [sets](https://www.doromb.com/spl/118794/Szettek) [F]
- [CLAIMED] Glazyrin's eight-harp set C2 to C3: "has sufficient uniformity allowing free use of different notes without wasting time on switching between instruments of different styles". — [Oberton Pro, DG997](https://www.oberton.pro/en/shop/dg997-tuned-jews-harps-set.html) [R]
- [CLAIMED] History: Scheibler's "Aura" mounted up to ten harps on a disc, each on a different fundamental, allowing chromatic sequences; Franz Koch (1761–1831) played two at once; Karl Eulenstein (1802–1890) played four, joined by silk strings; Albrechtsberger wrote seven concerti for Jew's harp, mandora and orchestra (1769–1771). — [Wikipedia, Jew's harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew%27s_harp) [F]. Montagu gives the Aura as "a group of up to a dozen trumps clamped together", invented in 1816. — [Montagu 2020, p. 23](https://www.jeremymontagu.co.uk/Trumps%20(Jews%20Harps)%20-%20Jeremy%20Montagu.pdf) [R]
- [AUTHORS' INTERPRETATION] "Multi-reed trumps are quite common in some areas, mainly in the Far East ... They allow for two or more fundamentals and therefore can provide different series of overtones." The Yi triple kouxian is "played similarly to the aura, by moving each over the mouth as is appropriate", the lowest reed in the middle. — [Montagu 2020, pp. 23–24](https://www.jeremymontagu.co.uk/Trumps%20(Jews%20Harps)%20-%20Jeremy%20Montagu.pdf) [R]
- [CLAIMED] Kouxian: several leaves "lashed together at one end and spread in a fan formation"; modern ones with three or more may be tuned to the first tones of the minor pentatonic scale. — [Wikipedia, Kouxian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kouxian) [F]. Brass ones come with three or four tongues; a bamboo set has five pentatonic tones. — [ASZA.com](https://asza.com/Instruments/ijkouqin.shtml) [F]. Chinese originals are sold as sets of two, three and four; Vietnamese-made single tongues can be combined freely. — [danmoi.com](https://www.danmoi.com/jews-harps-jawharps/pocketsynthesizer-polyphonic-jaw-harp) (snippet). The French article gives up to five tongues. — [Wikipedia (fr), Guimbarde](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guimbarde_(instrument)) [F]
- [MEASURED] Taiwan: bamboo frames "with 4 metal reeds" (L 9.4 and 7.5 cm), "The pitch of each lamella varies". — [Geidai 129](https://www.geidai.ac.jp/labs/koizumi/gcat0/english/html-text/129.html), [130](https://www.geidai.ac.jp/labs/koizumi/gcat0/english/html-text/130.html) [R]
- [CLAIMED] Two reeds on one frame: Glazyrin DG983 "Dynyan-Suygan" has "two reeds, tuned to different keys, giving common musical intervals - m2/M2/m3/M3/P4/P5 ... playing both reeds at once ... Separate strokes are possible too, but not very handy." Three: the Vietnamese cast "Trident" (110 × 140 × 7 mm, 180 g, reeds 58, 65 and 86 mm, centre reed bass), on which "changing jaw harps during the playing, especially fast, most likely will not work". — [Oberton Pro, DG983](https://www.oberton.pro/en/shop/dg983-dynyan-suygan-jews-harp.html) [R], [Trident](https://www.oberton.pro/en/shop/trezubec.html) [F]
- [CLAIMED] Double đàn môi: a "Perfect Fifth Set" of two tuned harps; a "Solid" double-reed model; the Mondharp "S" with two tuned reeds in several intervals. — [danmoi.com Perfect Fifth](https://www.danmoi.com/dan-moi-jaw-harps-perfect-fifth-set_1), [Solid double-reed](https://www.danmoi.com/double-reed-dan-moi-sturdy-jews-harp-bold-guimbarde-solid), [Mondharp S](https://www.danmoi.com/mondharp-s-shape) (snippets). A Laotian dual-blade strip, by contrast, has two tongues with "no perceptible difference in pitch". — [Montagu 2020, p. 23](https://www.jeremymontagu.co.uk/Trumps%20(Jews%20Harps)%20-%20Jeremy%20Montagu.pdf) [R]
- [DESCRIBED] Kyrgyz ensemble piece for "a wooden jaw harp and two metal jaw harps tuned a fourth apart (the difference between the scale degrees mi and la)". — [Nyshanov 2016](https://musc102.blogs.wesleyan.edu/files/2022/02/The-Music-of-Central-Asia_Chapters-15-16.pdf) [R]
- [AUTHORS' INTERPRETATION] Bali: genggong are played "in groups of anywhere from two to twenty people, each playing interlocking kotekan patterns with a partner", always in lanang–wadon pairs about a minor third apart. — [Morgan 2008](https://open.library.ubc.ca/collections/24/items/1.0066561) [R]
- [CLAIMED] "The trump can only achieve such things by having more than one reed or more than one instrument". — [Montagu 2020, p. 1](https://www.jeremymontagu.co.uk/Trumps%20(Jews%20Harps)%20-%20Jeremy%20Montagu.pdf) [R]

### Inferences (mine)
- The usual Alpine pairings are a fourth or fifth apart (A+E, G+D, C+F), i.e. frequency ratios 4:3 or 3:2, and trios add the third primary degree; a MIDI-driven engine reproduces "Wechselspiel" simply by letting the fundamental jump by those intervals between notes, with the previous harp either stopped (lifted off the teeth) or left ringing briefly.
- With two reeds sounded together (Dynyan-Suygan, double đàn môi) both harmonic series are filtered by the same mouth, so a duophonic mode with one shared formant filter is the faithful model; the kouxian is the same with 3 to 5 reeds plucked singly or in quick succession.

### Gaps
- No source states which intervals traditional Chinese kouxian sets use, beyond "pentatonic" and "traditional tuning".
- Karl Eulenstein is often said to have used 16 harps; the sources I could read give four at once. Fox's anthology, which reprints Scheibler, was not obtained.
- How Alpine players damp or overlap the harp being put down is not described in any source read.

## 8. Electric and amplified jaw harps

### Takeaway
Nothing I could verify describes a jaw harp with a built-in pickup. What the sources document is acoustic amplification (a horn attached to the frame, a resonator) and the routine use of microphones.

### Cited Findings
- [MEASURED] "Jewsaphone", American, 1930s, metal: "H. approx. 17.8 cm (7 in.), Diam. of bell approx. 10.5 cm (4 1/8 in.)" (a Jew's harp with a horn bell). — [Met 504195](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/504195) [R]
- [MEASURED] "Trump Phono-Harp by Troman": frame L 80.5; W 51.5; horn "Bell L 115; ⌀ 84", the cone marked "Pat. No. 11185/30". — [Montagu 2020, p. 39](https://www.jeremymontagu.co.uk/Trumps%20(Jews%20Harps)%20-%20Jeremy%20Montagu.pdf) [R]
- [AUTHORS' INTERPRETATION] Bali: a resonator, the glumpah, "amplifies the sound of the genggong so that the player can better hear their part in ensemble playing"; it "doesn't make genggong sound significantly louder to an audience". "Genggong players today are almost always amplified with microphones, though some of the subtlety of the instruments' overtones is inevitably lost in the process." — [Morgan 2008](https://open.library.ubc.ca/collections/24/items/1.0066561) [R]
- [CLAIMED] Magnets fitted to steel harps are described as changing pitch or adding weight, never as picking up sound (see part 3); a magnetised frame and reed "begin to influence each other - the sound deteriorates". — [Oberton Pro FAQ](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/faq.htm) [R]

### Inferences (mine)
- A steel reed moving past a coil would work as a magnetic pickup in principle, but such a pickup would sense reed velocity, not the mouth-filtered sound, so it would give the bare fundamental and its mechanical overtones without the vowel melody; this may be why amplified playing relies on microphones. This is my reasoning, not a sourced statement.

### Gaps
- Searches for electric or pickup-equipped harps in English, German and Russian returned only autoharp and concert-harp pickups, and the search allowance ran out before further queries. Whether commercial "electric" jaw harps or contact-microphone practices exist is therefore unanswered here.
- The accessory page of the Szilágyi shop lists no pickup or microphone (first page only checked).

## 9. History and organology, so far as it explains the sound

### Takeaway
The bow-shaped iron harp spread in Europe from about 1200 and was mass-produced (Molln, Valsesia, Thuringia, later Birmingham), with a long-term shift from small bows with long arms to large open bows with short arms; the oldest finds anywhere are Chinese bone idioglots with a string hole. Researchers who looked at how it sounds (Crane, Ledang) concluded that the frame edges are as important as the reed, which is why the tightly fitted forged harp sounds richer than cast or souvenir ones.

### Cited Findings
- [AUTHORS' INTERPRETATION] Kolltveit's catalogue of archaeological finds records 830 harps (United Kingdom 173, Switzerland 137, Sweden 118; 275 from castles); the oldest from datable contexts are from around 1200 AD, most from 1200–1700; the forms develop "from jew's harps with small bows and long arms toward instruments with large open bows and short arms"; manufacture had the character of mass production. — [Kolltveit, web summary of Jew's Harps in European Archaeology](https://musark.no/jews-harp-in-european-archaeology/) [F]
- [MEASURED] Medieval and later English finds: copper-alloy frame from the Thames foreshore, "Frame L 50.5; OW 24.5; top of bow to bend c. 20.8; length of upper arm 27.5 ... width of gap 4.2 reducing to 2.5; max thickness 3.7; open channel for reed 1.9"; larger copper-alloy frame from near Oxford (Kolltveit's "Stafford type"), "Frame L 86.7; OW 35.5; top to bend 32.9; length of upper arm 57.4 ... width of gap 6.4, narrowing to 4.4; max thickness 8.4". — [Montagu 2020, pp. 34–36](https://www.jeremymontagu.co.uk/Trumps%20(Jews%20Harps)%20-%20Jeremy%20Montagu.pdf) [R]
- [AUTHORS' INTERPRETATION] Oldest finds: bone harps from Jianping Shuiquan, Liaoning (Lower Xiajiadian culture, 2000–1200 BC), "the oldest jew's harps ever found"; a bone one from near Chifeng (1200–600 BC), length 9.8 cm, with "a small hole in the base end of the lamella [that] probably served for attaching a string"; four bamboo ones from Jundushan (700–500 BC), about 10 cm; a bone one from Morin Tolgoi, Mongolia, 12.5 cm. Two heteroglot pieces from Omiya, Japan (first half of the 10th century, 12.8 and 12.4 cm) would be "the oldest heteroglot jew's harps discovered so far". "The most plausible assumption is that both forms of jew's harps developed in Asia and spread westwards, independently of each other." — [Kolltveit 2016](https://musark.no/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/J-Harps-of-Bone-Wood-and-Metal.Kolltveit-2016.pdf) [R]
- [AUTHORS' INTERPRETATION] Classification follows the sound mechanism: Crane "claimed that the jew's harp functions only when a stream of air passes the embouchure portion of the instrument" and proposed reclassifying it as a free aerophone; Ledang reached the same conclusion from the gap experiments (part 5); Kolltveit keeps "plucked idiophone". — [Kolltveit 2016](https://musark.no/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/J-Harps-of-Bone-Wood-and-Metal.Kolltveit-2016.pdf) [R]. Montagu: "my own inclination today would be to shift it into the aerophone section"; the idioglot reed is "clearly identical, save for size, with the blown free reed" of Southeast Asian mouth organs. — [Montagu 2020, pp. 6, 21](https://www.jeremymontagu.co.uk/Trumps%20(Jews%20Harps)%20-%20Jeremy%20Montagu.pdf) [R]
- [AUTHORS' INTERPRETATION] Overtones "are not necessarily harmonic, especially in the upper range" (reporting Adkins 1974). — [Montagu 2020, p. 6](https://www.jeremymontagu.co.uk/Trumps%20(Jews%20Harps)%20-%20Jeremy%20Montagu.pdf) [R]. For the genggong, "the spectrum of sound generated by the lamella is full of many non-harmonic partials" (reporting Rai 2004). — [Morgan 2008](https://open.library.ubc.ca/collections/24/items/1.0066561) [R]
- [CLAIMED] Molln's guild of Maultrommel makers existed from 15 October 1679 to 1938; in 1818, 34 masters; the Schwarz firm (as of 1986) exported "jährlich etwa 300.000 Stück in 19 Länder". — [Wikipedia (de), Maultrommel](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maultrommel) [F]
- [AUTHORS' INTERPRETATION] "While there will always be discussions as to where the best trumps come from, many people say that the Siberian ones are the best of all. The Norwegian instruments are often thought to be the next best (or the best, depending on taste)". — [Montagu 2020, p. 50](https://www.jeremymontagu.co.uk/Trumps%20(Jews%20Harps)%20-%20Jeremy%20Montagu.pdf) [R]
- [AUTHORS' INTERPRETATION] In Tuva the arc-shaped rod form is recorded from the late 19th century and the plate ("fork") form only from the 1970s; the plate form is the one professional players now use. — [Tiron 2025](https://fehu.philology.nsc.ru/journals/ykns/pdf/YFKNS_55/2025_%E2%84%963_04.pdf) [R]

### Inferences (mine)
- The historical European harp (small, 36–87 mm, in museum collections) would on the size-pitch trend of the modern table sit in the upper part of the range, and the Molln "45 to 105 mm" selection of 1967 covers the same span as today's A1 to D3 offer; no source measured the pitch of old instruments, so this stays a guess.
- The organological argument (free reed in a tight slot) and the makers' advice (gaps first) point the same way: the timbre of a jaw harp model is set less by the frame's shape than by the reed's stiffness, its bent end, and the clearance and evenness of the slot. Those are the parameters a physical or semi-physical model should expose.

### Gaps
- Kolltveit's book (dimensions and typology of the 830 finds), Wright 2015 (British manufacture, including Birmingham makers and export sizes), Plate 1992 and Klier 1956 were not obtained; the statements above come from Kolltveit's web summary and his 2016 article.
- No source read gives the pitch of surviving historical instruments.
