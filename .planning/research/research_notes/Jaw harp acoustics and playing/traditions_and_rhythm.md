## 1. South Indian morsing (Carnatic music)

### Takeaway
The morsing is the one jaw harp tradition conceived as percussion: it shadows the mridangam inside the Carnatic percussion group, its player silently "speaks" the drum syllables (konnakol) into the instrument, and published descriptions call the sound loud, high-pitched and staccato with a short sustain. I found no source that maps individual syllables (ta, dhi, thom, nam) to a pluck direction or breath direction, and no published tempo in bpm for morsing playing; the nearest measurement is the stroke density of tani avartanam recordings (mean 8.6 strokes per second) in a dataset that does not include the morsing.

**The tani avartanam (the percussion solo) and where the morsing sits in it**
- `[OBSERVED]` The tani "is performed in the same tāla, and metrical tempo as the main item". The mridangam "always starts first by playing sarvalaghu (SV) patterns", complex patterns come in gradually, in a rhythmic structure called *naḍe* ("usually in chaturaśra at first"); an elaboration on one rhythmic theme is an *abhiprāya*; the second percussionist follows in the same *naḍe*; in the second round the mridangist may change the *naḍe* (to tiśra, for example) and the other may switch (to khaṇḍa); "Each abhiprāya ends with a pattern called korvai, which is repeated thrice to arrive at downbeat." — [Gowriprasad et al. 2023](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2023/paper/000005.pdf)
- `[OBSERVED]` Then the *korapu* (koraippu), "usually seen as a question-answer": "The duration of the rhythm patterns in korapu keeps reducing progressively from full cycle, half cycle, quarter cycle until it finally reduces to a single beat." Then the artists "start playing together playing faster with crisp strokes (farans)", leading to the *mohra* and a longer *korvai*. — [Gowriprasad et al. 2023](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2023/paper/000005.pdf)
- `[CLAIMED]` "During the Thani Avartanam, the percussionists perform in order: the Mridangam, Kanjira [...], and Ghatam", then all play together from the koraippu; the solo "is not limited to just these three percussion instruments". Elsewhere the same article lists "kanjira, morsing, and ghatam vidwans" exchanging patterns with the mridangam. — [Wikipedia: Mridangam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mridangam)
- `[CLAIMED]` Early 20th-century "full-bench" concerts (Konerirajapuram Vaidyanatha Iyer, Naina Pillai, Chitoor Subramania Pillai) had konnakkol and morsing artistes sharing "the honours with the mridangists". — [chinnapapa blog: "MORSING"](https://chinnapapa.blogspot.com/2008/07/morsing.html)
- `[DESCRIBED]` Bhagyalakshmi M. Krishna (as reported): "Around 1910, is when it was first seen in classical concerts". — [Lakshmi Anand, "Bhagyalakshmi M. Krishna"](https://lakshmianand.com/?p=3384)

**Measured tempo and density (percussion solos; morsing not in the dataset)**
- `[MEASURED]` Dataset: about 12 hours of tani audio; each tani 6 to 29 minutes, mean 11 minutes; four tālas (ādi, miśra chāpu, khaṇḍa chāpu, rūpaka) and five naḍe-s; instruments labelled: "22 mridangam, >12 ghatam, >8 khanjira". — [Gowriprasad et al. 2023](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2023/paper/000005.pdf)
- `[MEASURED]` "The mean and std. deviation of strokes per second, as obtained in the entire dataset, are 8.6 and 3.8, respectively." (average stroke density over 4-second frames). "Even while playing a slower tempo, we observe at least more than 8-10 strokes [...] in a window length of 4-5 seconds." "Each rhythmic structure is presented at multiple speeds." — [Gowriprasad et al. 2023](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2023/paper/000005.pdf)
- `[CLAIMED]` Carnatic speed categories are defined by strokes per beat: "Chauka (one stroke per beat), Vilamba (two strokes per beat), Madhyama (four strokes per beat), Drut (eight strokes per beat) and lastly Adi-drut (16 strokes per beat)"; a *kāla* change typically doubles the speed (first speed, second speed, and so on). "A tala does not have a fixed tempo (laya)". — [Wikipedia: Tala (music)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tala_(music))
- `[DESCRIBED]` "Double speed nuances, for example, are really hard to play on this instrument." (Sharma, on the morsing). — [Lakshmi Anand, "V. Sai Subramaniam"](https://lakshmianand.com/?p=4027)

**Sound character**
- `[OBSERVED]` "The morsing is made of very thick iron, and the lamella has very high tension and short sustain. The result is a loud, high-pitched staccato effect, and as a result, morsing is mostly used to provide rhythmic accompaniment although it sometimes takes virtuosic turns in percussion ensembles." (Morgan credits a personal communication with Jim Santi Owen, 2008.) — [Morgan 2008, "Organs and Bodies: The Jew's Harp and the Anthropology of Musical Instruments", MA thesis, University of British Columbia](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)
- `[OBSERVED]` "The morsing is unique among Jew's harp traditions in that the instrument is conceptualized in terms of its rhythmic possibilities, in contrast to its usual function as a melodic instrument." — [Morgan 2008](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)
- `[OBSERVED]` Spectral place among the Carnatic percussion (from the paper's spectral illustration of damped and resonant strokes): the mridangam covers "both the low-frequency and mid-frequency spectra"; the ghatam "occupies a little over the mid-frequency band"; "morsing predominantly spreads over the high-mid frequency spectrum and has a larger resonance"; the khanjira "occupies a low-frequency spectrum a bit less than the left side of the mridangam". No frequencies in Hz are given. — [Gowriprasad et al. 2023](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2023/paper/000005.pdf)
- `[CLAIMED]` "Others speak into the instruments while playing, thus giving it the effect of a light haunting echo."; "By constricting the space in the mouth the nostrils can produce sounds in different phases, similar to phasers in electronic music."; the sound is said to resemble the didgeridoo (flagged "citation needed" in the article). — [Wikipedia: Morsing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morsing)
- `[CLAIMED]` Classic FM's own description: also used for "comical sound effects – wobbles, boings and onomatopoeic springing sounds". — [Rosie Pentreath, Classic FM, 13 Jan 2020](https://www.classicfm.com/discover-music/instruments/morsing-introduction-tuning-use/)
- `[DESCRIBED]` Other musicians on Bhagyalakshmi's playing: "rhythmic patterns of different sounds and tonal qualities" (Sukanya Ramgopal); "an extremely sweet and aesthetic turn on the morsing" (Amrutha Venkatesh). — [Lakshmi Anand, "Bhagyalakshmi M. Krishna"](https://lakshmianand.com/?p=3384)

**Konnakol / solkattu syllables and how they reach the instrument**
- `[CLAIMED]` Common solkattu groupings: 2 "Tha Ka"; 3 "Tha Ki Ta"; 4 "Tha Ka Dhi Mi"; 5 "Tha Dhi Gi Na Thom"; 6 "Tha Ka Dhi Mi Tha Ka"; 7 "Tha Ka Di Mi Tha Ki Ta"; 8 "Tha Ka Dhi Mi Tha Ka Jho Nu"; 9 "Tha Ka Dhi Mi Ta Dhi Gi Na Thom". — [Wikipedia: Konnakol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konnakol)
- `[CLAIMED]` The mridangam's patterns are "silently recited while playing the morsing [...] But while playing on morsing you don't actually make sound of reciting the syllable but just move your tongue that way so that the air passages gets blocked and cleared in a pattern so as to produce the sound of the ferns." — [Wikipedia: Morsing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morsing)
- `[OBSERVED]` "the solkattu syllables are enunciated into the instrument in a kind of projected whisper. **Plucking, tongue, nose, breath (only what is specific to this tradition)**
- `[CLAIMED]` Held in the left hand with the forks pressed on the upper and lower front teeth; "The trigger is plucked with the tip of the index finger." Pitch colour is changed by tongue movement "while making nasal sounds"; "the syllable /ŋg/ or a variant thereof, is sounded through the nose while air is pushed out or pulled in through the mouth"; "Movement of the player's tongue with constant plucking can produce very fast patterns of sound." — [Wikipedia: Morsing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morsing)
- `[CLAIMED]` "This is plucked with the Index finger of the right hand (backwards, not forwards)". Breath gives further control, but "extensive use of this technique for fast, staccato effect quickly results in hyperventilation"; "you can produce a very long duration note on the morsing by sucking air through it", "sometimes (depending on the instrument) without even plucking it first". — [chinnapapa blog: "MORSING"](https://chinnapapa.blogspot.com/2008/07/morsing.html)
- `[DESCRIBED]` "The frame is held between the teeth and sound is produced by plucking the tongue with the forefinger or middle finger"; lips and the plucking finger "can both get cut and bleed after a long duration of playing". — [Lakshmi Anand, "Bhagyalakshmi M. Krishna"](https://lakshmianand.com/?p=3384)

- `[CLAIMED]` "Like the mridangam, the morsing is tuned to the tonic, which means you have to have one for each sruti." "Fine tuning is achieved by placing small amounts of bee's wax on the end of the tongue." — [chinnapapa blog: "MORSING"](https://chinnapapa.blogspot.com/2008/07/morsing.html)

### Inferences
(Mine, not from a source.)
- The article's word "ferns" is very likely *farans* (the fast, crisp patterns named in the ISMIR paper); the two sources then agree that the morsing's showpiece material is fast subdivided patterns.
- A default "morsing" voice should be short, bright, loud and dry: one hit per syllable, little ring between hits. The longer, vowel-swept, sustained sound belongs to the Siberian and European styles below.
- With a mean of 8.6 strokes per second (sd 3.8) in tani recordings, and four strokes per beat as the middle speed, a plausible working range for morsing-style programs is roughly 5 to 12 hits per second, i.e. sixteenth notes at about 75 to 180 bpm. This is my arithmetic on a dataset of mridangam, ghatam and khanjira, not a morsing measurement; players say double-speed playing is hard on the morsing, so the upper end should be used sparingly.
- "Sruti 2 to 5" in the South Indian *kattai* numbering would be about D to G (taking 1 = C); I did not find a source stating this mapping for the morsing, so the construction and tuning notes should confirm it.
- Three event types beyond the plain pluck are attested and worth separate triggers: an unplucked breath sound (the "gumki" effect "with no plucking at all"), a long inhaled note, and nasal or tongue-gated repetitions within one ring.
- Program structure can follow the tani: steady sarvalaghu groove; themed variations in fours, then threes or fives; a call-and-response that halves each time (full cycle, half, quarter, one beat); a fast unison passage; a cadence figure played three times to land on the downbeat.

## 2. Sakha (Yakut) khomus

### Takeaway
The khomus is a solo, improvising instrument of speech-like articulation and sound-painting, not a time-keeper: the old manner *syyia tardyy* ("talking khomus") gives one stroke per sung or silently spoken syllable at a steady moderate pace, while the modern concert style strings together named effects (cuckoo, lark, goose, horse trot, dripping thaw, round-dance rhythm) and changes rhythm several times in one piece. Sources agree on a handful of real Sakha terms but give no tempo figures and no notated rhythm.

### Cited Findings

**Role: solo, private, speech-like**
- `[OBSERVED]` "The bulk of the JH's autochthonous repertory consists of speech-like articulations and special sound effects". Playing many jaw harps together "would mess up the clarity of articulation, preventing the parsing of meaningful musical elements such as tabygyr (staccato)"; "Therefore, autochthonous styles are strictly soliloquy-based." — [Nikolsky, Alekseyev, Alekseev & Dyakonova 2020, "The Overlooked Tradition of 'Personal Music' and Its Place in the Evolution of Music", Frontiers in Psychology 10:3051](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- `[OBSERVED]` "except for the recently invented JH models, traditional instruments are barely audible beyond a few meters." — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- `[OBSERVED]` "Yakuts do not consider the khomus to be frequency-based. They evaluate its sound not as 'pitch,' but as 'coloration.'" Playing tunes such as "Mary had a little lamb" on it is called an "inauthentic, Western-style treatment". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- `[CLAIMED]` Before the revolution Yakut khomus music had a "сугубо личным, интимно-камерным" (strictly personal, intimate chamber) character; it was played in grief, in love confessions (linked to the love-song genre *туойсуу*), as a way to "talk", as girls' and women's "khomus songs" (*хомус ырыата*), in group playing, and at the *ысыах* festival. — [Ivanova 2015, "Хомус как инструмент культовых обрядов коренных народов Якутии", Lomonosov-2015 conference report (student paper)](https://conf.msu.ru/archive/Lomonosov_2015/data/6975/uid82785_report.pdf)
- `[OBSERVED]` Romantic duets exist: "Serenading" khomus proceeds "in a responsorial setting, but ends with simultaneous playing". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- `[CLAIMED]` "The 'talking vargan' method is used by some lyrical storytellers, who are able to pronounce words while playing the khomus." — [Wikipedia: Jew's harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew%27s_harp)

**Named styles and techniques (Sakha terms as the sources spell them)**
- `[OBSERVED]` *Syyia tardy* ("moderate playing"), nicknamed "talking khomus", "is closely related to the vocal toyuk". Gloss: "Syyia in Yakut literally means 'slowly, staidly,' and tardy—'to yank, pull,' as to pluck the JH's lamella. Syyia tardy signifies a particular playing technique, where silent mouth articulations as though speak out syllables while the plucked lamella keeps vibrating. Its technique is also used in other styles that require increased clarity of articulation". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- `[CLAIMED]` The same manner, written «сыыйа тардыы» and translated literally as "постепенно дергающая манера игры" (gradually plucking manner of playing): its feature was "соответствии одного ритмического удара по кончику язычка хомуса одному слогу выговариваемого текста или распеваемого напева песен типа «дэгэрэн» или «дьиэрэтии»" (one rhythmic stroke on the tip of the tongue of the khomus for one syllable of the spoken text or sung tune of *degeren* or *dieretii* songs). Improvisations in this manner are called a potpourri, «ырыа - ункуу матыыптарын тардыы» ("мелодии песен и танцев", melodies of songs and dances). — [Ivanova 2015](https://conf.msu.ru/archive/Lomonosov_2015/data/6975/uid82785_report.pdf)
- `[DESCRIBED]` Spiridon Shishigin: "I know that there is a traditional style 'syiyija tardyiyi' (temperate play) when rhythm remains almost the same. An executant plays this way when he is alone. He is not interested both in sound power and its variety. He plays not for people. [...] Whereby he can also croon to himself toyuk-song what he has just composed." — [Varganist.ru: "Spiridon Spiridonovich Shishigin and khomus" (interview by Vladimir Markov, 2009)](https://www.varganist.ru/53e.html)
- `[OBSERVED]` Two vocal styles that khomus playing follows: *degeren* ("rhythm-oriented, metrically regular") and *dieretii* ("smooth, metrically free style"); *toyuk* is "the most ancient form of dieretii yrya—a smooth-flowing, drawn out improvisational singing style". In one recorded improvisation (Agrafena Ptitsyna, singing and playing at once) "The performer starts in the style of degeren [...] but after 30 s switches to another style, dieretii [...] and thereafter keeps alternating between the two." — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- `[OBSERVED]` A typical "scenario" on the subject of *ysyakh* (the summer solstice festival) "usually involves performance of the okhuokhai rhythmo-formula (festive round dance), the crescendo effect (as if gradually approaching the festival), kuoregei (imitation of a larch-call, associated with sunrise), kebe (cuckoo call, announcing the onset of summer), tabygyr (associated with the sound of the horse trot and droplets of melting icicles), and uyuu (articulation of words representing shouting of excited people and singing of dance-leaders)" (citing Shishigin 1995 and Alekseev 1988). — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- `[CLAIMED]` Older players' improvisations set familiar *degeren* tunes in the middle, framed by an introduction and a conclusion, "с небольшими вставками приемов «куорэгэйдэтии» («соловьиная трель»)" (with small inserts of the *kuoregeidetii* technique, glossed there as "nightingale trill"). — [Ivanova 2015](https://conf.msu.ru/archive/Lomonosov_2015/data/6975/uid82785_report.pdf)
- `[OBSERVED]` "Yakut khomusists frequently engage the timbral vocable 'hyttya'." A song in *degeren* style with "regular dance-like rhythm" was reworked on khomus by Fedora Gogoleva; the khomus version "keeps the degeren style rhythm". The vocal style *tangalai yryata* is "palatal singing", "where tongue is abruptly pushed to the soft palate while taking a loud inspiration". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- `[OBSERVED]` "JH players choose a specific articulation (vowel or syllable), melodic intonation (a succession of pitches), imitation of a natural sound (birdcall) or a special technical device (staccato) for their specific expression. They use it either for repetition, variation, or contrast to some other thematic element – generating a sort of expressive 'scenario' for a musical piece." — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)

**How the named effects are made (Varvara Stepanova's lesson, written up by Vladimir Markov)**
- `[DESCRIBED]` Lark: "Yakut music players have some method similar to 'the lark's song'. It is produced by quick tongue movements back and forth."; exercise: "sing syllables like a lark: iy ia io iu". — [Varganist.ru: "Varvara Stepanova's master class" (2009)](https://www.varganist.ru/43e.html)
- `[DESCRIBED]` Goose: "goose squawk is produced by quick touches of the tip of the tongue to the palate", moving the contact point "from the teeth to the soft palate" to change the sound. — [Varganist.ru: Stepanova master class](https://www.varganist.ru/43e.html)
- `[DESCRIBED]` "Drops" (rain drops): "press the reed of the khomus by the index finger of your left hand [...] exactly in the middle of its length from the back side of the khomus. The right hand should strike the reed from both sides", adding "side tones by breath and by movements of your tongue". — [Varganist.ru: Stepanova master class](https://www.varganist.ru/43e.html)
- `[DESCRIBED]` Cuckoo: "The cuckoo word should pronounced by syllables Ka-Ku", tried higher and deeper. — [Varganist.ru: Stepanova master class](https://www.varganist.ru/43e.html)
- `[DESCRIBED]` Horse: the left hand slows the reed as for the drops; "Strikes should be done on the reed by three fingers of the right hand by turns", with a relaxed hand. — [Varganist.ru: Stepanova master class](https://www.varganist.ru/43e.html)
- `[DESCRIBED]` "The reed pause", "Actually a new method invented by Fedora Gogoleva", done with the tongue, the lips or a finger: "slowing down is made only at inhalation"; "The sequence of actions in any method is always the following one: a strike, then inhalation, then a pause, then nose-blow." — [Varganist.ru: Stepanova master class](https://www.varganist.ru/43e.html)
- `[DESCRIBED]` "Throat style", printed as "БЭЛЭС ДОР5ООНО": "a strike, then a sloe [slow] inhalation, then a hard exhalation with guttural side tone." — [Varganist.ru: Stepanova master class](https://www.varganist.ru/43e.html)
- `[DESCRIBED]` The low "Khenkeley", called by the interviewer "a glottal- pulmonary sound": Shishigin says to enlarge the mouth cavity to the maximum, aiming the speech organs at "the sound 'О'", and for a lower sound still to involve "larynx and chest cavity". — [Varganist.ru: Shishigin interview](https://www.varganist.ru/53e.html)

**Imitation of nature and animals**
- `[OBSERVED]` The Sakha think the khomus "has a strong power to call the summer after severe long winter, not only because it can produce the different sound of nature including dripping of melting ice, birds' chirping, the clatter of horse hooves and so on, but also since it is made by a blacksmith." — [Tadagawa 2017, "The khomus is my red deer…", The New Research of Tuva no. 2](https://nit.tuva.asia/nit/article/download/718/1107)
- `[CLAIMED]` The museum's own text: "the performer can imitate the thud of hoods [hooves], wolf's howling, blowing of air, song of skylark"; "From the dawn of time performer is happened to play khomus like he is talking." — [Google Arts & Culture: People of the World Khomus Museum and Center story](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/TQUhc2QFnaBTLQ)
- `[DESCRIBED]` Shishigin's own book (quoted by the interviewer): "you should use a khomus with quite low basic tone for imitation a cuckoo an, a skylark and to mouth the words of a song"; his best cuckoo instrument has a basic tone he judges "a note between 'E' and 'F'". — [Varganist.ru: Shishigin interview](https://www.varganist.ru/53e.html)
- `[DESCRIBED]` Albina Degtyareva: as a child on a horse "I took a deep breath and gave a wild scream like a sudden laugh or a neighing horse!" and "I learned how to make this sound while playing my khomus." — [Google Arts & Culture: "The World of Ayarkhaan"](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/kgXxV5RhQEfaKA)
- `[CLAIMED]` "With the different sounds of voice and of khomus Ayarkhaan can at the same time simulate sounds of animals, birds and the nature". — [WOMEX: Ayarkhaan](https://www.womex.com/virtual/khomus_universe/ayarkhaan)

**Rhythm, tempo, instrument choice, improvisation**
- `[DESCRIBED]` The interviewer observes that in Yakut-style improvisation "rhythm often changes cardinally several times in one composition"; Shishigin: "There are a lot of executants who use both different rhythm and different styles in one performance", and of himself "I am not making my play be in one rhythm or not changing." — [Varganist.ru: Shishigin interview](https://www.varganist.ru/53e.html)
- `[DESCRIBED]` "a khomus with low sounding sounds longer and suits temperate playing. Quick playing needs a khomus with high tone." He keeps several concert instruments for this reason. — [Varganist.ru: Shishigin interview](https://www.varganist.ru/53e.html)
- `[DESCRIBED]` On improvising: "In the beginning you should learn all the playing technique, but by no means demonstrate your skill during playing. A set of sounds is not music yet."; concert improvisations start and end on the same theme; "it is necessary to learn to pronounce at least all the sounds of the Yakut language to play the Yakut khomus well." — [Varganist.ru: Shishigin interview](https://www.varganist.ru/53e.html)
- `[DESCRIBED]` Degtyareva: "I began to search for a special breathing technique, subordinating the tempo and rhythm of playing to this melody"; her school is characterised as "distinguished by special musicality and rhythm, a clear command of breathing techniques, a variety of voice techniques". — [Google Arts & Culture: "The World of Ayarkhaan"](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/kgXxV5RhQEfaKA)
- `[CLAIMED]` The modern "звукоизобразительно-импровизационный" (sound-painting, improvisational) style enriched the repertoire with compositions close to *toyuk*, *dieretii* and *осуохай* (the round dance), widened the overtone range, pushed the khomus toward the concert stage, "а также поднял вопросы нотирования" (and raised the question of notation). — [Ivanova 2015](https://conf.msu.ru/archive/Lomonosov_2015/data/6975/uid82785_report.pdf)

**Sound of the articulations (measured)**
- `[MEASURED]` Four "poles" of khomus articulation (performed by Ivan Alekseyev) each have a salient register: "2.4–3.1 kHz for 'front,' 0.3–1.6 kHz for 'back,' 1.2–2.5 kHz for 'high,' and 0.6–0.9 kHz for 'low' vowels." — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- `[OBSERVED]` Players' sensations: "front" syllables are "tense and unpleasantly 'tart'"; "back" syllables "feel pleasantly relaxed"; "high" syllables resemble "smiling"; "low" syllables activate the soft palate "into a 'cupola'" and most players associate them "with the sound of a big church-bell" (Erkin Alekseyev's descriptions). — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- `[OBSERVED]` Modernised instruments maximise loudness for the stage and are considered by some "not simply an augmented khomus, but a new instrument in terms of acoustics and art". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- `[CLAIMED]` The loud, singing instrument is called «этигэн хомус» ("громкозвучного, певучего хомуса"); the bent tip of the tongue is *чыычаах* ("птичка", little bird), with a round ear *эминньэх* on its end. In the 1950s A. I. Chakhov and G. A. Grigoryan experimented with amplifying the sound, producing a "khomus with a ring" that did not spread. — [Ivanova 2015](https://conf.msu.ru/archive/Lomonosov_2015/data/6975/uid82785_report.pdf); [Gogolev, Egorov & Rufov 2023, "Хомус в исторической памяти Якутии", Человек и культура no. 6](https://nbpublish.com/library_read_article.php?id=69313)

### Inferences
(Mine.)
- "kuoregei (imitation of a larch-call)" in the Frontiers footnote is almost certainly a misprint for "lark-call": the varganist.ru lesson and the museum text both speak of the lark or skylark. The Lomonosov student paper's gloss "nightingale trill" for the same word disagrees; I would trust "lark".
- "БЭЛЭС ДОР5ООНО" is printed with a digit standing in for a Sakha letter (probably «бэлэс дорҕооно»); I report it as printed and did not find a second source for the term or its literal meaning.
- For the synthesizer: a "khomus" voice wants long sustain, a low fundamental, and per-hit vowel or formant targets in the four measured registers (about 0.3–1.6, 0.6–0.9, 1.2–2.5 and 2.4–3.1 kHz); rhythm comes less from the pluck than from what happens inside one ring (tongue flutter for the lark, palate taps for the goose, breath pulses, pauses with the reed stopped).
- Useful built-in patterns with documentary support: one-hit-per-syllable steady pulse (*syyia tardyy*); a round-dance ostinato (the *okhuokhai* formula, pattern itself not documented); a horse trot made of three quick strokes on a damped reed; damped "drops" struck from both sides; a two-note "Ka-Ku" cuckoo; strike, inhale, pause, nasal release.
- Piece form: free alternation of metrically regular and metrically free passages, with a crescendo used as a device.

### Gaps
- No tempo in bpm, no metre and no transcription of khomus rhythm found; the *okhuokhai* rhythm formula is named but not written out anywhere I could read.
- The primary methods (I. E. Alekseev, "Искусство игры на якутском хомусе", 1988; S. Shishigin, "Играйте на хомусе"; Shishigin 1995; Zhirkova 1991) were not available online; the terms above reach me through Nikolsky et al., an interview, a lesson write-up and a student paper. A fuller list of named strokes surely exists there.
- No technique statement in their own words found for Olena Uutai or Yuliyana Krivoshapkina (the Dan Moi interview with Uutai returned HTTP 403; the Montana Folk Festival page showed one truncated sentence).
- The Vilyui (Suntar) school of playing is named in an abstract ([Nikitina 2024](https://nlrs.ru/open/114715)) but not described.
- Museum collection size differs between sources: 1613 (Russian Wikipedia), "circa 1700" ([Italian Wikipedia](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scacciapensieri)).
- How the horse-trot and "drops" patterns actually sound (tempo, accent) needs the ear.

## 3. Rajasthani morchang and other South Asian jaw harps (murchunga, binayo, gogona)

### Takeaway
In Rajasthan the same iron instrument is a folk percussion colour for song and dance, played by hereditary musician communities alongside dholak and khartal; sources are encyclopedic and thin, with no technique, tempo or stroke names. Nepal's murchunga is described as having more sustain; the bamboo binayo and the Assamese gogona are separate plucked-lamella instruments.

### Cited Findings
- `[CLAIMED]` "In Rajasthan it is known as morchang and is used as a percussion instrument in lok geet (folk music)."; players include "the Barmer Boys" and "Rajasthani folk music players from the traditional entertainer tribe of Laggas" (sic; presumably the Langa community). It was used in Hindi film music by R. D. Burman and S. D. Burman. — [Wikipedia: Morsing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morsing)
- `[CLAIMED]` (snippet) "Traditionally played by Langa and Manganiyar musicians, hereditary Muslim musician communities of the Thar region, it accompanies folk singing and dancing." — [National Virtual Library of India: Morchang](https://apitesting.nvli.in/musical-instruments/ghan-vadya/morchang) (connection refused; not read)
- `[CLAIMED]` (snippet) A genre guide pairs it with dholak and khartal as the rhythmic core and names its contribution as "the twang of morchang". — [Melodigging: Rajasthani folk music](https://www.melodigging.com/genre/rajasthani-folk-music)
- `[CLAIMED]` "The instrument is used as part of the rhythm section in various styles of Indian folk and classical music." Indian morchangs are made "mainly in brass, iron, copper and silver". — [Wikipedia: Jew's harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew%27s_harp)
- `[CLAIMED]` Older Indian types are wooden or bamboo string-pulled lamellae: the Assamese "гагана (гангинна)" and the Rajasthani "гхоралио" (ghoralio); "Варганы используются в ансамблях для ритмического сопровождения пения и танцев" (jaw harps are used in ensembles for rhythmic accompaniment of singing and dancing). — [Russian Wikipedia: Варган](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Варган)
- `[DESCRIBED]` "The morchang, Sai says, is easier to play" (than the morsing). — [Lakshmi Anand, "V. Sai Subramaniam"](https://lakshmianand.com/?p=4027)
- `[CLAIMED]` (snippet) In Rajasthan and Gujarat the jaw harp is a folk instrument; it is not played in Hindustani classical music. — [Dan Moi: "Moorsing, Morchang and Gogona – the Jew's harps of India"](https://www.danmoi.com/moorsing-morchang-and-gogona-the-jews-harps-of-india)
- `[CLAIMED]` Nepal: the murchunga "is very similar to an Indian morsing or morchang in that the tongue (or twanger) extends beyond the frame, thus giving the instrument more sustain." — [Wikipedia: Jew's harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew%27s_harp)
- `[CLAIMED]` The binayo is a Kiranti bamboo instrument (from *malingo* bamboo), "6 inches long and 1 inch in width", popular in Sikkim, Darjeeling, eastern Nepal and Bhutan. — [Wikipedia: Binayo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binayo)
- `[CLAIMED]` The gogona (Assam, Bihu music) is bamboo or horn with a forked end; "The solid end is gripped with the teeth and the free ends are then struck repeatedly with the fingers"; women dance Bihu "to the rhythms of the dhol and pepa" with it. — [Wikipedia: Gogona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gogona)
- `[CLAIMED]` In Sindhi music the *changu* "can be an accompaniment or the main instrument"; a noted player is Amir Bux Ruunjho. — [Wikipedia: Jew's harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew%27s_harp)

### Inferences
(Mine.)
- The morchang's rhythmic role is that of a continuous ostinato colour beside hand drums and clappers, not the syllable-exact shadowing of the Carnatic morsing; a "morchang" program can be a steady repeated cell with breath accents. This rests on thin sources.

### Gaps
- No ethnomusicological description of morchang technique, breath accents, typical cycles (for example keherwa or dadra) or tempo was found; the British Library Endangered Archives item page returned empty, the NVLI page was unreachable, and the Dan Moi article returned HTTP 403.
- Nothing rhythm-specific found for the murchunga or binayo.

## 4. Inner and North Asia and Russia: Tuvan and Altai khomus / komus, Kyrgyz temir ooz komuz, Kazakh shankobyz, Bashkir kubyz, Russian vargan

### Takeaway
Across Siberia and Central Asia the jaw harp is mostly a solo, personal instrument of sound imitation and wordless or mouthed song; Tuvan sources name distinct "singing" and "speaking" ways of playing, and Kyrgyz playing consists of short programmatic pieces (*küü*) about animals and landscape, later arranged for ensembles and notated. I found nothing that quantifies rhythm or tempo in any of these traditions.

### Cited Findings

**Tuva**
- `[OBSERVED]` Ways of playing recorded in 2010: "1) Imitation of sounds of nature, 2) Execution of the 'melodies' of songs such as kozhamyk [...], called yrladyp oinaary or 'singing play', 3) Silent execution of the 'words' of well-known kozhamyk and other songs called chugaaladyp oinaary or 'speaking play', 4) 'Uzun-khoyug' (literary 'long-soft'), a genre which is expressed only by musical instruments and throat-singing, 5) Simultaneous execution of the khomus playing and throat-singing." — [Tadagawa 2017](https://nit.tuva.asia/nit/article/download/718/1107)
- `[OBSERVED]` Instruments recorded: "demir-khomus (iron Jew's harp), kulzunkhomus (bamboo Jew's harp), chartykhomus (mouth resonating wood chip), yyash-khomus (mouth resonating branch of tree)" (two names lost their hyphens at line breaks in the PDF text; presumably kulzun-khomus and charty-khomus); also "playing the Jew's harp with a metal hammer", an invention "for the players who lost their teeth". — [Tadagawa 2017](https://nit.tuva.asia/nit/article/download/718/1107)
- `[OBSERVED]` A woman shaman, Dezhit Tozhu, played the khomus with eyes closed during the *kamlanie*; for her it was "a red deer that can fly like a bird", which she called "chagaa daiym — small horse". — [Tadagawa 2017](https://nit.tuva.asia/nit/article/download/718/1107)
- `[OBSERVED]` A recorded example of "Temir-khomus (Altaic JH) [...] imitating something that human voice cannot—the sound of the water stream, Tuva". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- `[CLAIMED]` In Tuvan music the jaw harp is used together with overtone and undertone singing; the style became known through Huun-Huur-Tu and Yat-Kha. — [German Wikipedia: Maultrommel](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maultrommel)

**Altai**
- `[OBSERVED]` "In Altai, the JH is regarded as a herding instrument"; vocally imitating the jaw harp "with help from the fingers" is "a popular activity among Altai children". Solitary use "prevails over the Volga Plateau", "Tuva/Altai" and elsewhere. — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- `[OBSERVED]` (snippet) In Altai the player simply mouths ordinary words into the instrument, in contrast to the Hmong system built on tones. — [Language Documentation and Description article 2939](https://www.lddjournal.org/article/id/2939/) (bot-protection page; not read, authors not captured)
- `[CLAIMED]` *Челер комус* is a reed or bamboo lamella with a cord for jerking the tongue, used until the early 20th century in the Ulagan district; *темир комус* is metal. — [Russian Wikipedia: Варган](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Варган)

**Kyrgyzstan**
- `[OBSERVED]` Two kinds: wooden (*jygach ooz komuz*, now thread-pulled) and metal (*temir ooz komuz*). On the wooden one "küüs played on the instrument tend to be short and simple. Such küüs mostly represent birds and animals, e.g., 'Kükük' (Cuckoo) and 'Jorgo' (Pacer horse), or describe patterns of nomadic lifestyle"; pitches are produced "by widening or narrowing the oral cavity and closing or opening the throat". — [Nyshanov 2016, "Kyrgyz Jaw Harps", ch. 15 in Levin, Daukeyeva & Köchümkulova (eds.), The Music of Central Asia, Indiana University Press](https://musc102.blogs.wesleyan.edu/files/2022/02/The-Music-of-Central-Asia_Chapters-15-16.pdf)
- `[OBSERVED]` The metal jaw harp repertory falls into "1. Variations on songs", "2. Küüs that represent nature and animals, e.g., 'Karacha torgoi' (Black lark), 'Turumtai' (Hawk), 'Erkin too' (Free mountain), 'Ker özön' (Wide valley)", "3. Küüs that narrate personal stories". "Variation and improvisation are characteristic of Kyrgyz music"; in one example "the performer plays a traditional küü, gradually accelerating the tempo, then adding a second traditional theme and improvising on it." — [Nyshanov 2016](https://musc102.blogs.wesleyan.edu/files/2022/02/The-Music-of-Central-Asia_Chapters-15-16.pdf)
- `[OBSERVED]` Formerly women's and children's instruments; ensembles became popular in the 20th century; "Many jaw harp melodies were recorded and notated." New *küüs* are mostly for ensemble and "notated in Western staff notation"; one ("Jangylyk") uses "a wooden jaw harp and two metal jaw harps tuned a fourth apart". A performer, "generally a woman", may pluck "with the fingers of both hands while making smooth, sweeping gestures". — [Nyshanov 2016](https://musc102.blogs.wesleyan.edu/files/2022/02/The-Music-of-Central-Asia_Chapters-15-16.pdf)
- `[CLAIMED]` The temir komuz is iron, "usually with a length of 100–200 mm"; range "generally hovers around an octave"; pieces "have been notated by Aleksandr Zataevich in two or three parts. Apparently an octave drone is possible, or even an ostinato alternating the fifth step of a scale with an octave." In the marionette play *tak-teke* (jumping goat) the player moves a goat puppet tied to the plucking hand. — [Wikipedia: Temir komuz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temir_komuz)
- `[OBSERVED]` An early Soviet-era "JH orchestra of Kyrghyz young women" was described (Vinogradov 1938) as a "haphazard juxtaposition" of instruments "without any coordination", each playing "according to their own whims". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)

**Kazakhstan**
- `[DESCRIBED]` Aizada Kemel plays the shankobyz in the last section of a piece with dombyra: "That is the gallop of horses. I imagined our warriors riding out". The paper describes the shankobyz as "a metal jaw harp that produces sound when held between the teeth and plucked". — [The Astana Times, 13 Apr 2025](https://astanatimes.com/2025/04/kazakh-musician-recreates-steppe-soundscape-using-voice-traditional-instruments/)
- `[CLAIMED]` Shishigin's collection in the Yakutsk museum includes "казахских нотированных варганов" (Kazakh "notated" jaw harps). — [Russian Wikipedia: Музей и центр хомуса народов мира](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Музей_и_центр_хомуса_народов_мира)

**Bashkortostan and the Volga–Ural region**
- `[OBSERVED]` "in Bashkortostan, leisure sessions might engage up to 3 women playing together" (Shchurov 1995); Udmurt, Bashkir, Tatar and Komi jaw harps of the Kama region are similar in construction, sound and technique. — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- `[CLAIMED]` (snippet) Kubyz playing is used to imitate sounds of nature (water, the clatter of horses, animal sounds); the instrument was revived and popularised by the virtuoso Robert Zagretdinov. — [culture.ru: master class "Игра на кубызе"](https://www.culture.ru/events/5341729/master-klass-igra-na-kubyze)
- `[CLAIMED]` One Bashkir type (*агас кубыз*) is a lamella whose tongue is jerked by a thread. — [Russian Wikipedia: Варган](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Варган)

**Russia**
- `[CLAIMED]` The oldest Russian instrument is a 13th-century find from Novgorod (13 jaw harps of the 13th–15th centuries found there); another Russian name is *зубанка*; the verb «варганить» first meant playing the vargan. — [Russian Wikipedia: Варган](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Варган)
- `[CLAIMED]` "The Jew's harp was banned in the USSR during the regime of Joseph Stalin due to its closeness to Shamanism." — [Wikipedia: Jew's harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew%27s_harp)
- `[OBSERVED]` "The jaw harp is the principal musical instrument for at least 21 ethnicities in Russia". Nivkh players mark the dog-racing genre with the "Kan Vai" rhythmic formula and an onomatopoeic "khav-khav" articulation; a self-entertainment pattern uses "sustained monotones on 'ko lyo-lyo-lyo,' with vibrato of the lower lip or tip of the tongue". A Mansi girl repeated the phrase "Gav-ri-ke, gav-ri-ke, vas pyrish, vas pyrish!" on the jaw harp (*tumran*). — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- `[OBSERVED]` Four layers in Chuvan jaw harp music: "(1) the FF; (2) its subharmonic chest resonance an octave below; (3) the middle melodic layer above FF controlled by the player's breathing and tongue position, exhibiting 'talking' qualities; and (4) a high melodic layer that changes the sustained phonemic formants which resembles singing." — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)

### Inferences
(Mine.)
- The recurring rhythmic images in this region are animal gaits (horse trot and gallop, pacer horse, dog barking) and bird calls; they are natural names for built-in patterns, but none of the sources gives the patterns themselves.
- The Kyrgyz "octave drone or fifth–octave ostinato" and the gradually accelerating *küü* are two concrete behaviours a program can imitate.

### Gaps
- No rhythm or tempo data for any of these traditions. Suzukei's books on Tuvan khomus, Zagretdinov on the kubyz and Zataevich's notations were not accessible. The Russian vargan revival (Pokrovsky ensemble and later) and the Altai komus with *kai* singing are not covered by anything I could read; the web-search allowance ran out before these could be searched further.

## 5. South-East and East Asian lamellate harps: Hmong ncas / đàn môi, Philippine kubing, Balinese genggong, Ainu mukkuri, Chinese kouxian

### Takeaway
These are quiet, intimate instruments of courtship and disguised speech, with one strongly rhythmic exception: the Balinese genggong, played in pairs and larger groups in interlocking sixteenth-note figuration with alternating exhaled and inhaled notes, which is the best-documented jaw harp rhythm found in this research.

### Cited Findings

**Hmong (ncas; Vietnamese đàn môi)**
- `[OBSERVED]` "The Hmong use the jaw harp to convey messages in courtship. A young man serenades the one he has chosen by composing his thoughts into words and then expressing them through the jaw harp. The young woman replies with her own instrument. Hmong is a tonal language with eight tones [...] Thus, the 'talking drums' of Africa have a counterpart in the 'talking reeds' of the Hmong." The jaw harp is held at the mouth "while inhaling or exhaling at each rhythmic stroke". — [Smithsonian Festival of American Folklife 1980 program book article](https://folklife-media.si.edu/docs/festival/program-book-articles/FESTBK1980_09.pdf)
- `[OBSERVED]` "The final communications of courtship are sent on a metal Jew's harp called nja which resonates to the vowels of the mouth cavity and the wooings which are too intimate to translate." — [Folkways liner notes, "The Music of the Hmong People of Laos"](https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/ARH00446.pdf)
- `[OBSERVED]` (snippet) In Hmong the surrogate speech rests on the tone system, reproduced through oral harmonics and not the larynx, with consonant contrasts carried by the plucking pattern. — [Language Documentation and Description article 2939](https://www.lddjournal.org/article/id/2939/) (not read)
- `[OBSERVED]` Jaw harp speech surrogates are reported among the Khmu, Hmong and Ifugao, "this latter being of interest because Ifugao is not a tonal language". — [McPherson, "The talking balafon of the Sambla" (author manuscript)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10166939)
- `[CLAIMED]` The đàn môi is a brass lamella "held against the lips while the lamella is plucked", which leaves the player "freer to shape their oral cavity"; the ncas "is commonly used in Hmong courting practices". — [Wikipedia: Đàn môi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90%C3%A0n_m%C3%B4i); [Wikipedia: Hmong music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hmong_music)
- `[OBSERVED]` Across cultures the jaw harp serves "Concealing the identity of a 'serenader' or his/her message from bystanders"; serenading duets "involve taking turns, like interlocution". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)

**Philippines (kubing) and neighbours**
- `[CLAIMED]` "The kubing is traditionally considered an intimate instrument, usually used as communication between family or a loved one in close quarters"; men use it "for short distance courtship". Names by group include kobing (Maranao), kolibau (Tingguian), ulibaw (Kalinga), kulaing (Yakan). — [Wikipedia: Kubing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kubing)
- `[CLAIMED]` Sundanese karinding: "the rhythm of the karinding music is determined by the movement of the tongue and lips"; "Its voice sounds like a big frog in the middle of rice fields." — [Wikipedia: Karinding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karinding)
- `[CLAIMED]` Cambodian angkuoch is called "both a wind instrument and percussion instrument"; Taiwanese Amis and Atayal harps were used for serenading and lovers' communication. — [Wikipedia: Jew's harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew%27s_harp)

**Bali (genggong)**
- `[OBSERVED]` Construction and stroke: an idioglot harp of sugar-palm leaf stem, "14cm to over 20cm" long; a string on the right end "is grasped by the right hand and jerked crisply to the right to set the frame and the lamella into motion". "The genggong lamella has a relatively short sustain, and must be constantly kept in motion by pulling the attached string. A loose but precise snapping of the wrist at regular short intervals not only keeps the genggong sounding, but affects the music played on it." — [Morgan 2008](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)
- `[OBSERVED]` Ensemble: "played in groups of anywhere from two to twenty people, each playing interlocking kotekan patterns with a partner"; instruments "are always used in lanang-wadon pairs" (*lanang* male, high; *wadon* female, low); the fundamentals of a pair "usually hovered around an interval of a minor third". The *lanang* leads. — [Morgan 2008](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)
- `[OBSERVED]` Vowels as notes: "the pitches called 'ning nong neng nung nang' are articulated by the vowels 'i o e u a'"; the *wadon* typically ranges from the 5th to the 9th harmonic. "genggong uses an open glottis [...] That is what gives genggong its characteristically deep and guttural sound." — [Morgan 2008](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)
- `[OBSERVED]` Breath as rhythm: "As kotekan patterns generally consist of sixteenth notes, half the notes are played while exhaling and the other half are played by inhaling. When playing a melody consisting of quarter notes or eighth notes, this technique is unnecessary as its primary function is to facilitate speed." The exhaling technique is called *ngengkahin* (after Rai 1998). — [Morgan 2008](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)
- `[OBSERVED]` Accent pattern (Morgan's Figure 5.5, genggong wadon): ning – exhale – accent; nong – inhale – passing note; neng – exhale – accent; nung – inhale – passing note; nang – exhale – accent. "There are, then, two layers in a genggong kotekan pattern: the first is the overall melodic flow of sixteenth notes, and the second is the rhythm that emerges as certain notes are accented and others are not." — [Morgan 2008](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)
- `[OBSERVED]` Older players (two men in their eighties, Batuan) "played parts which consisted mostly of eighth notes without passing notes. This was a slower, reduced playing, at half the density of the sixteenth note kotekan patterns." — [Morgan 2008](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)
- `[OBSERVED]` The Batuan Frog Dance ensemble (items 2 to 6 of Morgan's list; item 1 not captured in my extract) has: one small kendang "to carry the rhythm and give cues and accents"; "Several genggong, (usually four to eight [...]), playing an interlocking elaboration on the basic melody"; cengceng (small cymbals); two guntang (the larger as gong, the smaller, *klentit*, as time-keeper); a klenang "that interlocks with the small guntang, playing on the offbeat". Some groups use genggong only. *Enggung* (a breath-sounded free reed, named after a frog) gives "the sound of the big frog". — [Morgan 2008](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)
- `[OBSERVED]` Teaching (McPhee 1955, quoted by Morgan): pieces have a first section, "the polos or plain, sung in unison, and the more animated finale, the chandetan, a syncopated two-part affair, in which two separate but interlocking rhythms are combined to create an unbroken musical continuity"; children sang the parts for a month before touching the instruments. — [Morgan 2008](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)
- `[CLAIMED]` "Since the 1930s, however, genggong has often featured in gamelan genggong, a larger ensemble of several genggong together with suling and percussion instruments." — [Wikipedia: Genggong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genggong)

**Ainu (mukkuri)**
- `[CLAIMED]` Bamboo, "10 cm long and 1.5 cm wide"; "Sound is made by pulling the string"; "often accompanied by tonkori". Noted players: Umeko Andō, Shigiko Teshi, Daisuke Hare (who organised the first mukkuri competition in 2004 after visiting Sakha players in 2003). — [Wikipedia: Mukkuri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mukkuri)
- `[CLAIMED]` The player holds it to the mouth, pulls the string with the right hand, and changes the sound by moving the lips while breathing in and out; "Traditionally the mukkuri was played to attract the attention of someone one loves." — [MLIT multilingual database: Mukkuri (Mouth Harp)](https://www.mlit.go.jp/tagengo-db/en/R1-01909.html)
- `[CLAIMED]` "Traditionally played by women."; "quite difficult to sound due to their very thick tongue". — [ASZA instrument gallery: Mukkuri](https://asza.com/Instruments/ijmukkuri.shtml)
- `[CLAIMED]` The Japanese article says the pitch hardly changes and expression lies in changing the resonated overtones; it adds that the frequent overtone changes are sometimes likened to techno and that the instrument has gained young enthusiasts. — [Japanese Wikipedia: ムックリ](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ムックリ)

**China (kouxian)**
- `[CLAIMED]` Chinese jaw harps "may comprise multiple idiophones that are lashed together at one end and spread in a fan formation"; sets of three or more "might be tuned to the first few tones of the minor pentatonic scale"; especially popular among non-Han groups of Yunnan, Guangxi and Guizhou. Wang Li is the player named in the article's links. — [Wikipedia: Kouxian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kouxian)

### Inferences
(Mine.)
- The genggong gives a ready-made rhythm program for a pad instrument: two voices a minor third apart, each playing alternate sixteenths of one line (interlock), every note alternately "exhaled" (accented, open) and "inhaled" (passing, lighter), with a half-density eighth-note variant for the "old style". The croaking, guttural timbre should come from an open, large-cavity setting.
- Speech-surrogate playing (Hmong, Tuvan "speaking play", Sakha "talking khomus") is rhythmically speech-like: one pluck per syllable, phrase lengths and pauses of an utterance, no fixed metre. That is a recognisable "mode" for a sequencer: free, call-and-answer phrases between two voices.

### Gaps
- No tempo figure for genggong kotekan; Morgan gives note values only. No transcription beyond her Figure 5.5 was read (she mentions "preliminary musical transcription" as future work).
- Mukkuri sound-imitations (rain, wind, animals) could not be confirmed: the Hokkaido Ainu centre's mukkuri manual PDF did not download and the Dan Moi interview with Utae Ehara was only seen as a snippet (improvisation and feeling matter more than mastery).
- Kubing and kouxian: nothing beyond encyclopedia entries; no rhythm information.
- The LD&D article on jaw harp speech surrogacy (Hmong and Altai) sits behind bot protection.

## 6. Austrian / Alpine Maultrommel

### Takeaway
The Alpine style is melodic and harmonic: the player alternates quickly between two or more harps tuned to the tonic, dominant and subdominant so that dance tunes in major keys can be played over a changing drone; it was a courting instrument and a sociable one, and around 1770 it reached the concert hall in Albrechtsberger's concertos. Published text says breath shapes dynamics and rhythm, but I found no description of the dance rhythm itself.

### Cited Findings
- `[CLAIMED]` "Die österreichische Spielweise, bei der mehrere, auf die Hauptstufen (Tonika, Dominante und Subdominante) der Tonleiter gestimmte Maultrommeln verwendet werden, ermöglicht im Gegensatz zum reinen Bordunspiel die Verwendung der Maultrommel auch innerhalb der Dur-Moll-Harmonik". "Um den Tonumfang zu erweitern, wechseln Musiker im Spiel oft zwischen mehreren in den Händen gehaltenen Maultrommeln." — [German Wikipedia: Maultrommel](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maultrommel)
- `[CLAIMED]` Playing was highly developed in the Alpine countries, "unter raschem Wechsel zwischen unterschiedlich gestimmten Instrumenten" (with rapid alternation between differently tuned instruments); special techniques listed: "Zungenbewegung, Pharyngalverschluss, spezielle Zupftechniken". — [Födermayr, "Maultrommel", Österreichisches Musiklexikon online (2004)](https://www.musiklexikon.ac.at/ml/musik_M/Maultrommel.xml)
- `[OBSERVED]` Gerlinde Haid: by changing the mouth cavity the player favours partials and "kann also Melodien spielen, wobei jedoch der Grundton als Bordun immer mitklingt. Dynamik und Rhythmus werden durch den Atemstrom bestimmt, und mit Hilfe von Lippen und Zunge können außerdem noch viele klangliche Nuancen erzeugt werden." (melodies over an ever-sounding drone; dynamics and rhythm are determined by the breath stream). — [Haid, recommendation letter for the Austrian intangible heritage list, 7 Dec 2011](https://www.unesco.at/fileadmin/Redaktion/Kultur/IKE/IKE-DB/files/614.pdf)
- `[OBSERVED]` Courting and sociability: the instrument played "eine wichtige Rolle bei der Liebeswerbung am Kammerfenster zur Begleitung der Gaßlreime, was inzwischen abgekommen ist", and early became "einem Instrument geselligen Musizierens [...] mit dem sich sogar Virtuosen befasst haben"; its music "ist Volksmusik, in seltenen Fällen Kunstmusik und zunehmend auch experimentelle Musik." — [Haid 2011](https://www.unesco.at/fileadmin/Redaktion/Kultur/IKE/IKE-DB/files/614.pdf)
- `[CLAIMED]` In the past it was mostly used by boys visiting their sweethearts at night ("Fensterln"). — [Austria-Forum (AEIOU): Maultrommel](https://austria-forum.org/af/AEIOU/Maultrommel/Maultrommel_english)
- `[CLAIMED]` Scale from two harps: "The black notes on the stave show the scale which may be produced by using two harps, one tuned a fourth above the other." (Encyclopædia Britannica 1911, quoted in the article.) — [Wikipedia: Jew's harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew%27s_harp)
- `[CLAIMED]` Virtuosi: Franz Koch (1761–1831) "could play two Jew's harps at once"; Karl Eulenstein (1802–1890) "invented a system of playing four at once, connecting them by silken strings"; Johann Heinrich Scheibler mounted "up to ten mouth harps on a support disc" (the "Aura"). — [Wikipedia: Jew's harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew%27s_harp)
- `[CLAIMED]` Albrechtsberger "wrote seven concerti for Jew's harp, mandora, and orchestra between 1769 and 1771. Four of them have survived, in the keys of F major, E-flat major, E major, and D major. They are based on the special use of the Jew's harp in Austrian folk music." — [Wikipedia: Jew's harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew%27s_harp); contradicted on number and date by [German Wikipedia](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maultrommel) ("um 1765 sieben Konzerte [...] von denen sich drei in einer Bibliothek in Budapest erhalten haben"), by [Födermayr](https://www.musiklexikon.ac.at/ml/musik_M/Maultrommel.xml) ("vier Concertinos", inspired by the playing of the Melk Benedictine Bruno Glatzel) and by [Italian Wikipedia](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scacciapensieri) (two concertos, in E major and F major).
- `[CLAIMED]` In the concertos Albrechtsberger "interpretierte darin Melodien und Stilmittel der zeitgenössischen österreichischen Volksmusik" and fitted the instrument into Viennese Classical harmony by using different harps for the main degrees. — [German Wikipedia: Maultrommel](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maultrommel)
- `[CLAIMED]` The Ländler is "a European folk dance in 3/4 time", "a partner dance that strongly features hopping and stamping", sometimes with yodeling. — [Wikipedia: Ländler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A4ndler)
- `[CLAIMED]` Maultrommel playing has been on Austria's national intangible heritage list since 2012; Molln (Upper Austria) is the historic centre of manufacture; Albin Paulus was a laureate ("Preisträger") of the "World's virtuosos of khomus" competition in Yakutsk in 2011; an international festival was held in Molln in 1998. — [German Wikipedia: Maultrommel](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maultrommel); [Haid 2011](https://www.unesco.at/fileadmin/Redaktion/Kultur/IKE/IKE-DB/files/614.pdf)
- `[DESCRIBED]` A Sakha view of the Austrian instrument: Shishigin lists "an Austrian maultrommel" among harps "which play only melodies of its own nation". — [Varganist.ru: Shishigin interview](https://www.varganist.ru/53e.html)

### Inferences
(Mine.)
- What is rhythmic in the Alpine style is, by the evidence I have, carried by (a) the dance metre of the tunes (3/4 Ländler with stamping accents), (b) breath pulses on the beat, and (c) the audible switch of drone when the player changes harp at a chord change. For a pad instrument this suggests "kits" of two to four harps a fourth or fifth apart, with the bar's harmony chosen by which harp is struck.
- The 2-harp scale a fourth apart is the minimum for a diatonic tune; three (tonic, dominant, subdominant) match the cited description.

### Gaps
- No source read that describes the Alpine playing rhythm in detail (pluck per beat or per note, yodel-like figures, typical tempos), and no transcription of Maultrommel Ländler. Regina Plate's "Kulturgeschichte der Maultrommel", Klier's "Volkstümliche Musikinstrumente in den Alpen" (1956) and the Brüder Mayr method book were identified but not accessible.
- The count and dating of the Albrechtsberger concertos differ between four sources (seven, four, three surviving, two; c. 1765 vs 1769–1771); a catalogue or the Budapest sources would settle it.
- A festival workshop page on "the alpine traditions of maultrommel with Albin Paulus" appeared in search but the page fetched held other content.

## 7. Norwegian munnharpe

### Takeaway
The munnharpe plays the fiddle's dance repertoire (*slåtter*: gangar, halling, springar or springleik) as melody over the drone, with the player's foot stomp as part of the sound and "closed" (glottis-shut) tones as its hallmark. Tune types fix the metre (two-beat for gangar and halling, three-beat for springar), but the only tempo figure found is a general one for the halling dance (95–106 bpm), not a measurement of munnharpe playing.

### Cited Findings
- `[OBSERVED]` (snippet) Morgan argues that the munnharpe's hallmark sound is "a trifecta combining the glottal closing technique related to the seljefløyte, the melodic accents and ornamentation patterns related to the hardingfele, and the foot stomping rhythms related to folk dance choreography (Setesdal gangar)", and that the instrument's drone-and-melody texture led players to an idiomatic technique and repertoire. — [Morgan, abstract of "Archaic Fusion: An Analysis of Norwegian Munnharpe Style and Repertoire", AAWM 2018](https://deirdre-morgan.squarespace.com/upcomingevents/2018/6/26/analytical-approaches-to-world-music-conference-thessaloniki) (page returned 404 when fetched; text from the search excerpt)
- `[DESCRIBED]` A course by Tom Willy Rustad teaches "the stomp, the strokes, and how to get a clear beautiful sound" and "the mysterious 'closed' tones", with a lesson titled "Opening & Closing Technique"; the tunes are "Gangar from Setesdal and springliek from Gudbrandsdalen", plus a halling, a vals and others; "Most of the lessons are on a G-harp, one on D, and one on A." — [The Folk Music Academy: Norwegian Jaws Harp](https://www.thefolkmusicacademy.com/courses/21)
- `[CLAIMED]` (snippet) Two-part metre is found in halling, gangar and bridal march; three-part metre characterises pols and springar. — [Store norske leksikon: slått (musikk)](https://snl.no/slått_-_musikk)

### Inferences
(Mine.)
- A "munnharpe" program is a dance tune: a pluck on (nearly) every melody note, open and closed tones alternating as ornaments, and a foot stomp on the beats. A synthesizer aimed at rhythm could offer the stomp as a second, low thud voice alongside the harp.
- Applying the 95–106 bpm halling figure to munnharpe playing is an assumption; it describes the dance.

### Gaps
- No full-text read of Morgan's PhD thesis ("Speaking in tongues", SOAS 2017; the library host did not resolve), of her 2018 article, or of the USN thesis; these are the sources most likely to contain transcriptions and tempos.
- The asymmetric beat lengths of springar (which vary by district) are not documented for munnharpe in anything I read.
- "Closed tones" are described only by name here; the technique notes should explain them.

## 8. Central and Eastern Europe: Hungarian doromb, Czech brumle, Slovak drumbľa, Polish drumla, Hutsul / Ukrainian drymba

### Takeaway
Reference texts describe a drone-and-overtone-melody folk instrument with a breath-made rhythmic accompaniment: Hungarian sources name the pulsing diaphragm, Czech and Slovak sources name an off-beat breath technique called *kontrování*, and the Hutsul manner is characterised as markedly melodic with two overtone notes per stroke and a link to teaching dance. Ethnographic detail on historical playing in Bohemia, Moravia and Slovakia was not found.

### Cited Findings

**Hungary (doromb)**
- `[CLAIMED]` The fundamental acts as a continuously humming drone ("a folytonosan búgó burdon"); it "levegő beszívásával-kifújásával felerősíthető, illetve a rekeszizom lüktető mozgásával ritmuskíséretté alakítható" (can be strengthened by breathing in and out, and turned into a rhythm accompaniment by the pulsing movement of the diaphragm); range "akár két oktáv" (up to two octaves). Around 1900 it was a common children's toy, typically made by Roma smiths. — [Hungarian Wikipedia: Doromb](https://hu.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doromb)
- `[CLAIMED]` Áron Szilágyi (b. 1977) regularly plays in Moldavian Csángó dance houses; his band Zoord (from 2014) plays music based on Moldavian melodies, described as combining "virtuóz dorombjátékot, az energikus ethnót és az organikus trance-t". — [Hungarian Wikipedia: Szilágyi Áron (zenész)](https://hu.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szil%C3%A1gyi_%C3%81ron_(zen%C3%A9sz))

**Czech lands (brumle) and Slovakia (drumbľa)**
- `[CLAIMED]` "Brnkání je možné oběma směry, zejména při rychlých změnách tónu a tempa hraní." (Plucking is possible in both directions, especially for quick changes of note and tempo.) "Rytmickým prosáváním vzduchu štěrbinou při hraní se dosahuje efektu vlastního nepřízvučného a barvitého doprovodu, tak zvaného kontrování." (Rhythmically drawing air through the slit while playing gives an unaccented, colourful accompaniment of one's own, called *kontrování*.) Playing on the lips is "jemnější" (gentler) than on the teeth. The instrument is used solo, "až koncertního", or as accompaniment; recordings by J. Peško with the Slovak Radio Orchestra are mentioned. — [Czech Wikipedia: Brumle](https://cs.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brumle)
- `[CLAIMED]` Czech and Moravian names: brumle, brumla, mrumle, grumle, "židovská harfa"; Moravia "brumajzl"; Comenius (Orbis Pictus) "brumlačka". The claim that Roma metalworkers spread it is flagged in the article itself as constructed from a Moravian song. — [Czech Wikipedia: Brumle](https://cs.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brumle)
- `[CLAIMED]` The Slovak article repeats the same text (it is a translation), with names "dromľa, drmľa, drumlica, grumbla, bzučák, brnkáš" and the same *kontrovanie* passage. — [Slovak Wikipedia: Drumbľa](https://sk.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drumbľa)
- `[DESCRIBED]` Petr Jasinčuk: "Here in Czechia, it is mostly used in traditional folk music, especially in Moravia"; "Moravians and Slovaks also call it grumle"; when teaching, "I start with explaining the rhythm, because it is absolutely essential if you want to learn how to play the brumle. I developed four different patterns to help people in the learning process."; in duo "I create a sort of baseline with the jaw harp, while she adds the drum rhythm." — [Radio Prague International, 2 Nov 2023](https://english.radio.cz/node/8798977)
- `[CLAIMED]` (snippet) A Czech patent for a brumle says the tuning is set by mass and that the instrument is used for rhythmic accompaniment while playing the melody. — [Patent CS273105B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/CS273105B1/cs)
- `[CLAIMED]` Slovak maker Peter Sobota offers untuned drumbľa and ones "tuned C, D, E, F", and "two-sided" instruments in two keys; he has made 42 shape designs and runs playing courses. — [ÚĽUV: "Drumbľa", a humble and proud musical instrument… by Peter Sobota (RUD 4/2012)](https://uluv.sk/?p=124133)

**Poland (drumla)**
- `[CLAIMED]` Usually played against the teeth, sometimes held in the lips without tooth contact ("technika water drop"); the sound is modulated by tongue and lip movement and "poprzez wdychanie/wydychanie powietrza"; today used among other things in Roma music. — [Polish Wikipedia: Drumla](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drumla)

**Ukraine (Hutsul drymba)**
- `[CLAIMED]` Most used in Hutsul musical life. "Серед особливостей гуцульської методи гри на «дримбі» - яскраво виражена мелодійність (важлива не лише ритмічність, але й виразність нот обертонового звукоряду), а також перевага застосунку двох обертонових нот на один удар." (Features of the Hutsul manner: pronounced melodiousness, where not only rhythm but the clarity of the overtone notes matters, and a preference for two overtone notes per stroke.) — [Ukrainian Wikipedia: Дримба](https://uk.wikipedia.org/wiki/Дримба)
- `[CLAIMED]` The drymba was used in the Hutsul region for teaching ritual dances: "Переважно на дримбі грала мати, навчаючи дітей традиційним танцям" (mostly the mother played, teaching children the traditional dances), which the article links to the verb «дримбати». A 20th-century Transcarpathian folk choir had a drymba ensemble of 45 singers; Hutsul drymba is heard in Parajanov's 1964 film "Тіні забутих предків". — [Ukrainian Wikipedia: Дримба](https://uk.wikipedia.org/wiki/Дримба)
- `[CLAIMED]` In western Ukraine the drymba was the only women's (mostly girls') instrument; in the mid-20th century it was still popular among shepherds in Transcarpathia. Local names: *доромба* (Transcarpathia), *орган* (Lemko region). — [Russian Wikipedia: Варган](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Варган); [Ukrainian Wikipedia: Дримба](https://uk.wikipedia.org/wiki/Дримба)

### Inferences
(Mine.)
- *Kontrování* (the breath pulse between plucks, sounding the harp without a stroke) is the one named rhythm technique in the Czech and Slovak material and matches the Hungarian "pulsing diaphragm": for the synthesizer it is a second, softer event type placed on the off-beats between struck notes. The Wikipedia passage is unsourced, so the name's currency among players should be checked.
- "Two overtone notes per stroke" (Hutsul) means each hit carries a two-step formant movement, a useful per-hit articulation option.

### Gaps
- No Czech, Moravian or Slovak ethnographic source on historical brumle or drumbľa playing (who played, for which dances, with which instruments) was found; two candidate university PDFs (an ethno-organology text at Masaryk University and a bachelor thesis) were not downloadable, and the two Czech theses I did download do not mention the instrument. The brumle.cz portal is archived only.
- No tempo, metre or transcription for any of these traditions. Jasinčuk's "four different patterns" are not spelled out in the interview.
- Polish and Hungarian traditional (pre-revival) practice is described only at encyclopedia level.

## 9. Italian / Sicilian marranzano (scacciapensieri)

### Takeaway
In Sicily the marranzanu accompanies songs and tarantellas and has a solo repertoire; teachers describe its technique as a coordination of diaphragm breath, glottis and finger stroke. The research on Sicilian repertoire is said to be largely unpublished, and I found no description of its rhythm patterns.

## 10. British, Irish and American Jew's harp / trump

### Takeaway
In Scotland and Ireland the trump plays dance tunes (hornpipes, jigs, reels, marches, strathspeys) melodically in the key of the harp; a leading player says the plucking pattern, not breath, should carry the rhythm, and that heavy breathing turns the playing into "a rhythmic drone". American traditional practice is not covered by anything I could read.

### Cited Findings
- `[DESCRIBED]` Michael Wright: "The first thing to realise is that you need Jew's harps in different keys."; "If you play a Jew's harp in a non-harmonious key it sounds awful."; "most musicians in sessions play in D, G, A or E flat." — [Wright, "The Jew's Harp, the fool of instruments – a personal view", The Living Tradition](https://www.livingtradition.co.uk/htmfiles/inart545.htm)
- `[DESCRIBED]` Repertoire by harp: on an F harp hornpipes, jigs and reels ("Murphy's Hornpipe", "Banish Misfortune", "The Maid Behind the Bar"); "the G is good for quick, sharp tunes"; "The D has opened up the possibility of set dances, airs and Scottish MSF's (march, Strathspey and reels)." — [Wright, The Living Tradition](https://www.livingtradition.co.uk/htmfiles/inart545.htm)
- `[DESCRIBED]` Rhythm by plucking: "I usually start off by only playing the drone for the first few seconds."; on jigs "you can pluck on beats one and two, leaving three, keeps the energy of the tune moving."; "Another technique is to pluck the tongue for every note."; "Some musician's push the metal tongue forward", a technique he has "never favoured". — [Wright, The Living Tradition](https://www.livingtradition.co.uk/htmfiles/inart545.htm)
- `[DESCRIBED]` Breath and loudness: "it is a delicate and subtle instrument"; in sessions players "compensate for the quiet nature of the harp by breathing heavily through the tongue"; "Too heavy breathing accentuates the drone (not desirable)"; "The result is a rhythmic drone that kills any idea of subtlety."; "some people simply cannot hear beyond the drone." — [Wright, The Living Tradition](https://www.livingtradition.co.uk/htmfiles/inart545.htm)
- `[DESCRIBED]` Players: John Wright, "one of the great living players", called Angus Lawrie of Oban "The greatest Jew's harp player I have ever heard."; John Campbell is "one of the few melodic players I know combining [...] the Jew's harp with the 'diddling' of Len Graham." "Scotland and Ireland still use the original name of 'trump'." — [Wright, The Living Tradition](https://www.livingtradition.co.uk/htmfiles/inart545.htm)
- `[CLAIMED]` An early-modern description quoted in the article: "one may strike the tongue with the index finger in two ways, i.e., by lifting it or lowering it: but it is easier to strike it by raising it"; the buzzing "imitates that of bees, wasps, and flies". The name "Jue harpes" appears in 1481. — [Wikipedia: Jew's harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew%27s_harp)
- `[CLAIMED]` Popular-music uses: Canned Heat "Parthenogenesis", Black Sabbath "Sleeping Village", The Who "Join Together"; Charles Ives wrote a part in "Washington's Birthday". In German-speaking countries the sound is widely known from the Sesame Street theme and from Medicine Head's 1970s hits. — [Wikipedia: Jew's harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew%27s_harp); [German Wikipedia: Maultrommel](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maultrommel)
- `[CLAIMED]` (snippet) A review of Wright's book reports his view that the Scots and Irish integrated the jaw harp into their music culture and the English did not. — [Dan Moi: on Michael Wright's book](https://www.danmoi.com/how-the-jaw-harp-became-a-commodity-in-great-britain-and-ireland-michael-wrights-book-about-the-jaw-harp)

### Inferences
(Mine.)
- Two ready rhythm behaviours follow from Wright: "pluck every note" (the stroke rate equals the tune's note rate, so reels at session speed mean fast continuous strokes) and "pluck beats one and two of three" in jig time, letting the ring carry the third. Both are directly programmable.
- The old-time and country "boing" of American usage is presumably the same drone-rhythm playing Wright criticises, but I have no source for that.

### Gaps
- No source read on American (old-time, minstrel, country) jaw harp practice: Fox's anthology, Crane's writings and the Jew's Harp Guild material were not accessed. Wright's book (The Jews-Harp in Britain and Ireland, 2015) was not read; a reviewer notes its coverage of the music itself is limited. No tempo or transcription found; the Living Tradition page carries no date.

### Takeaway
Since the 1990s revival a "trance" idiom has grown around fast, breath-driven, filter-sweep-like playing, often with didgeridoo, drums and beatbox; journalists and listeners compare it to an acid bass line. Hard descriptions of the playing (tempo, patterns) are almost absent from the readable sources.

### Cited Findings
- `[CLAIMED]` Airtist (founded 2006) was a jaw harp, beatbox and didgeridoo trio: "Markus Meurer – didzseridu, Dömötör Balázs – beatbox, Szilágyi Áron – doromb"; Szilágyi led it until 2017 and it played in more than 30 countries. ### Inferences
(Mine.)
- The "acoustic TB-303" comparison fits what the instrument physically offers: a fixed-pitch buzz with a sweeping resonance and accent by breath. A trance-style program is a continuous sixteenth-note pluck stream at dance tempo with a slowly moving vowel sweep and breath accents; the tempo range of the electronic genres themselves (roughly 120–150 bpm) is general knowledge, not a jaw harp source.

### Gaps
- No measured tempo, no pattern description and no first-hand technique account for trance or techno jaw harp or for beatbox with jaw harp; the Dan Moi articles returned HTTP 403 and the web-search allowance ran out before named players (for example the Russian and German scenes) could be researched.

## 12. Transcription, notation and measured tempo across traditions

### Takeaway
Published notation of jaw harp rhythm is scarce: the only analysed rhythm I could read is Morgan's breath-and-accent scheme for genggong, and the only numeric rate is the Carnatic percussion stroke density. Notation exists for Kyrgyz pieces and is discussed for the Yakut khomus, but none was readable.

### Cited Findings
- `[MEASURED]` Carnatic percussion solos: mean 8.6 strokes per second, standard deviation 3.8, over about 12 hours (mridangam, ghatam, khanjira; no morsing). — [Gowriprasad et al. 2023](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2023/paper/000005.pdf)
- `[OBSERVED]` Genggong: sixteenth-note kotekan with alternate exhaled (accented) and inhaled (passing) notes; an older style in eighth notes at half the density (Morgan's Figure 5.5 and text). — [Morgan 2008](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)
- `[OBSERVED]` Kyrgyz: "Many jaw harp melodies were recorded and notated"; new ensemble pieces are "notated in Western staff notation". — [Nyshanov 2016](https://musc102.blogs.wesleyan.edu/files/2022/02/The-Music-of-Central-Asia_Chapters-15-16.pdf)
- `[CLAIMED]` Temir komuz pieces "have been notated by Aleksandr Zataevich in two or three parts". — [Wikipedia: Temir komuz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temir_komuz)
- `[OBSERVED]` "Despite the JH's tuning to a certain fundamental frequency (FF), indigenous techniques obstruct standard Western notation (frequency-based), since the production of discrete pitches constitutes only a fraction of the possibilities". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)
- `[CLAIMED]` The modern Yakut concert style "raised the question of notation". — [Ivanova 2015](https://conf.msu.ru/archive/Lomonosov_2015/data/6975/uid82785_report.pdf)
- `[CLAIMED]` The German article links a "Notationssystem für Maultrommel" (archived) and Robert Vandré's "Virtuose Spieltechnik für Maultrommel: Eine Anleitung mit Klangbeispielen"; the Russian article describes varganist.ru as carrying "Техника игры на варгане по нотам" (playing technique from notation). — [German Wikipedia: Maultrommel](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maultrommel); [Russian Wikipedia: Варган](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Варган)
- `[CLAIMED]` Dance tempos near jaw harp repertoires: halling 95–106 bpm (6/8 or 2/4); Carnatic speeds defined as 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 strokes per beat. — [Wikipedia: Halling (dance)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halling_%28dance%29); [Wikipedia: Tala (music)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tala_(music))

### Inferences
(Mine.)
- Because the pluck is the only attack, published "rhythm" in most traditions is really two layers: the pluck stream and the articulation inside each ring (breath in and out, glottis, tongue). Any notation for the synthesizer's programs needs both layers; Morgan's three-row scheme (pitch vowel, breath direction, accent) is the nearest published model.

### Gaps
- None of the notation systems named above was read (Vandré, the archived Maultrommel notation page, varganist.ru's notation lessons, Zataevich). No measured tempo of any jaw harp performance was found in print; measuring recordings is the other researcher's task and would be the only way to fill this.

## 13. What the traditions imply for a rhythm-first synthesized jaw harp (cross-tradition summary)

### Takeaway
Only two traditions treat the jaw harp as a rhythm instrument in the strict sense (Carnatic morsing, Balinese genggong); the rest make rhythm inside a drone by breath, tongue and throat, or simply follow the metre of a dance tune. The sound descriptions split into a short, bright, loud staccato type (morsing) and a long-ringing, low, vowel-rich type (khomus), with Alpine, Norwegian and Celtic playing in between as melody over a drone.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
(Mine; a proposal for the report writer, not sourced facts.)
- Default sound for a rhythm-first instrument: the documented percussive type is the morsing (short, bright, loud). If the default must also serve melodic or "trance" playing, a medium sustain with a controllable decay is the compromise; a separate long-ringing low "khomus" preset covers the other pole. Shishigin's rule gives the mapping: low instruments ring longer and suit moderate playing, high ones suit fast playing.
- Event types worth having as separate pads or note ranges, each attested above: outward and inward pluck (Czech "both directions", Wright, Mersenne-style description); exhaled accent and inhaled passing note (genggong, Hmong, morsing); unplucked breath pulse (*kontrování*, gumki-like effect); damped stroke (Sakha "drops", horse); stopped reed with pause (Gogoleva's "reed pause"); nasal or tongue-gated repeat inside one ring (morsing, lark); foot stomp (munnharpe).
- Program families with documentary support: tani-style Carnatic sequences in 4, 3, 5 and 7 subdivisions; genggong interlock for two voices a minor third apart; one-per-syllable "talking" pulse; Kyrgyz fifth–octave ostinato with accelerando; jig pattern "pluck 1 and 2, leave 3"; reel "every note"; Ländler in 3 with harp changes on the harmony; halling in 2 at about 95–106 bpm; tarantella-type running triplets (weakly supported); trance sixteenths with vowel sweep (weakly supported).
- Where the sources give syllables, they can label pads: Carnatic "Tha Ka Dhi Mi", "Tha Ki Ta", "Tha Dhi Gi Na Thom"; Balinese "ning nong neng nung nang"; Sakha "Ka-Ku", "iy ia io iu", "hyttya".
