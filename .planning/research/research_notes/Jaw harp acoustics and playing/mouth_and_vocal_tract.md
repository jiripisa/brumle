# Mouth, vocal tract, breath and voice: how the player shapes the jaw harp sound

Scope: the mouth and throat as the resonator that picks harmonics out of the lamella's sound, the breath, and the voice and glottis. The lamella as a sound source, the instrument types and the musical traditions are left to other notes.

Evidence classes used on every finding: **MEASURED** (a published measurement; the quality is stated where it is weak), **MODEL** (authors' model or interpretation), **DESCRIBED** (a player's or teacher's own account), **CLAIMED** (maker, vendor, encyclopedia, journalist, student page). "(snippet)" marks text seen only in a search-result excerpt.

Numbering used in these notes: harmonic n = n × the lamella's fundamental (harmonic 1 is the fundamental). Two sources count differently and are converted where quoted: Robert Vandré counts "overtones" (his overtone k is harmonic k + 1; verified on his figure, where the "10th overtone" of a 98 Hz harp sits at about 1078 Hz = 11 × 98 Hz); Nikolsky et al. write f5, f13 for harmonics 5, 13, and use "F1 / F2" for their own "instrumental / vocal formant", which is not the phonetician's F1 / F2.

## 1. The mechanism of harmonic selection: which resonance, what range, how sharp, how many dB

### Takeaway
The lamella passing through the frame fires short pulses into the mouth, and the mouth (with the throat behind it) rings in answer; the harmonic of the lamella nearest the mouth resonance is the one that is heard as the melody. Published numbers put the main, melody-carrying resonance at about 0.5 to 2.0–2.3 kHz and a second, lower one at about 0.22–1.0 kHz; the selected harmonic stands 9 dB (open throat) to 13–17.5 dB (closed throat) above the mean of its two neighbours in the one measured series that exists, which is a hobbyist's. No calibrated measurement of the mouth's resonance frequency, bandwidth or Q during jaw harp playing was found.

### Cited Findings
- MODEL (abstract only): "the jew's harp excites the mouth cavity by producing short pulses when the lamella passes through the frame." Two regimes follow: "When the mouth cavity is tuned to low frequencies, its natural resonance time is longer than the period between successive pulses and the harmonic series of the fundamental frequency of the lamella dominates the sound as the mouth tuning is changed. At high frequencies, the mouth cavity and ear respond to each successive pulse independently so that the dominant sensation of pitch coincides with the natural resonance frequency of the mouth and may be tuned continuously." — [Adkins 1974, JASA 55(3), 667–670](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1914580)
- MEASURED / MODEL (as reported by Morgan; Ledang's own text not obtained): Ledang's spectral analysis "observed that a lamella ... vibrating on its own does not constitute a harmonic series; it is the frame surrounding the lamella ... through which the lamella passes when plucked, that creates a spectrum of harmonics"; "the smaller the distance between lamella and frame, the richer the harmonic spectrum and the longer it is sustained". Morgan adds that Crane reached the same conclusions independently. — [Morgan 2008, p. 23–24](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf); opening extract of the paper at [Ledang 1972](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/yearbook-of-the-international-folk-music-council/article/on-the-acoustics-and-the-systematic-classification-of-the-jaws-harp/FFA2A840F93D1BFC82157BC4BB81730B)
- MODEL: the lamella is the "carrier signal" and the vocal apparatus the "modulator filter"; "the JH makes an effective vocoder (Leipp, 1963)". The instrument "determines the frequency, while the vocal component—its amplification/attenuation (Dournon-Taurelle and Wright, 1978, p. 21)". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051/pdf)
- MEASURED (hobbyist; Audacity spectra, one player, levels not calibrated between recordings): the same G harp (fundamental 98 Hz) recorded clamped in a vice and at the mouth shows peaks at identical frequencies; only their heights differ. "the resonances of the mouth cavity are a filter that amplifies or reduces the volume of the jew's harp reed at certain frequencies - just like an equalizer". — [Vandré, Tones and Pitches](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html)
- MEASURED (hobbyist): the spectrum played at the mouth minus the spectrum in the vice, for the harp's harmonic 12 (about 1176 Hz): "In the region of 1200 Hz the formant is displayed as a mountain." Knocking on the corner of the mouth with the same mouth position and no harp gives a spectrum with the same hump "around 1200 Hz". Read by me off the figure: the difference curve peaks at about +30 dB near 1.2 kHz, stays above +10 dB from about 1.1 to 1.45 kHz, and is 0 to −30 dB below about 1 kHz; the knock response peaks about 18–20 dB above its floor at about 1.22 kHz. — [Vandré, figure "resonance-filter"](http://www.rvandre.de/grafiken/resonance-filter.gif), text at [Tones and Pitches](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html)
- DESCRIBED / MODEL (player's assignment of formants, no measurement behind it): "The most pronounced resonance region is the second formant of the vocal tract, being somewhere in the range from 500 to 2000 Hz." "The first formant can be anywhere in the range between 250 and 1000 Hz" and gives "accompanying notes". "Several formants can be active at the same time." — [Vandré, Tones and Pitches](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html)
- CLAIMED / DESCRIBED: "Regina Plate states that the pitches that can be played range from 500 to 2000 Hz, corresponding to two octaves. In my experience tones with a frequency of up to about. 2300 Hz can be played. The notes of the first formant can be as deep as about 250 Hz." - MEASURED (Russian version, my translation): on the Yakut khomus (fundamental 72 Hz) "F1 occupies the zone 220–365 Hz, which corresponds to 876 cents, whereas F2 varies within 950–1700 Hz, making 1007 cents"; a Cambodian angkuoch (fundamental 85 Hz) was analysed the same way. - MEASURED (audio example analysed by the authors, one player, Ivan Alekseyev): the four "poles" of khomus articulation each stress one register of the "JH formant": "2.4–3.1 kHz for 'front,' 0.3–1.6 kHz for 'back,' 1.2–2.5 kHz for 'high,' and 0.6–0.9 kHz for 'low' vowels." — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051/pdf); same figures in [Nikolsky et al. 2019 (Part 1)](https://lang-folk.ru/sites/default/files/2026-09/01_96.pdf)
- MEASURED: compared with singing and speech of the same vowel, "F1 in the JH is much softer", "The JH's F2 is much stronger", the harmonics "display a much greater odd/even contrast", and are "much narrower in bandwidth and are compromised by less noise". - MODEL (anatomist's reading of his own spectrograms; newsletter article, no table): in a narrowband spectrogram of a D harp plucked "4 to 5 times a second", "The lines of harmonics are kept constant in positions and ranges during the play"; changing pitches "keep a constant harmonics pattern but follow moving of formant frequencies"; "The higher pitches represent higher formant frequencies. ### Inferences
- (Mine.) All the numeric sources agree on one picture with two player-controlled regions: a strong, movable resonance somewhere in 0.5–2.3 kHz that carries the melody, and a weaker low region around 0.22–1.0 kHz (Vandré's "first formant" at 250–1000 Hz, Nikolsky's "instrumental formant" at 220–365 Hz, Trias's line near 500 Hz). A third region at 2.4–3.1 kHz appears for front ("i"-like) shapes. A sound engine with one strong movable band-pass, one weaker low band-pass and an optional high one covers every published number.
- (Mine.) Nikolsky's "instrumental formant" at harmonics 3–5 (216–360 Hz on a 72 Hz khomus) coincides with the first formant of a vocal tract whose mouth opening is very small (speech F1 of [i] and [u] is 270–310 Hz for men, see section 8). The harp frame held at the teeth keeps the mouth opening small for every articulation, which would hold the tract's own F1 low and nearly constant. This would explain why the authors find it "instrumental" (the same for all vowels) while Vandré and Trias find the low region playable. It is a hypothesis; no source tests it.
- (Mine.) Size of the mouth resonance implied by Vandré's bars, if the mouth is modelled as one two-pole resonance centred on the played harmonic (neighbours at ±98 Hz attenuated by 10·log10(1 + (2Δf/B)²)) and the lamella's own odd/even contrast (the "vice" row) is subtracted: glottis closed, odd harmonics, 17.5 − 5.7 = 11.8 dB gives B ≈ 52 Hz, that is Q ≈ 20–30 at 0.9–1.5 kHz; open tract, even harmonics, 4.3 + 5.9 = 10.2 dB gives B ≈ 64 Hz; open tract, odd harmonics, 9.0 − 5.7 = 3.3 dB gives B ≈ 180 Hz. The three values do not agree, so the mouth does more than a single resonance does (see the odd/even effect in section 3), but 50–65 Hz matches the closed-glottis bandwidths measured in speech research (45–80 Hz below 2.1 kHz, section 8).
- (Mine.) My reading of Vandré's knock response: a peak about 18–20 dB above the floor with a −3 dB width of roughly 80–120 Hz at 1.22 kHz, Q roughly 10–15. A hand knock is a crude excitation and the figure is small; treat it as an order of magnitude.
- (Mine.) Adkins's two regimes in numbers: a resonance of bandwidth B rings with a time constant 1/(πB), that is 7 ms for 45 Hz, 5.3 ms for 60 Hz, 3.2 ms for 100 Hz. A 72 Hz lamella has a period of 13.9 ms and a 98 Hz one 10.2 ms; if the lamella fires a pulse on each pass through the frame the pulse spacing is half that. Ring time and pulse spacing are therefore of the same order across the playing range, so both regimes are in play at once. A resonator excited by the pulse train (rather than a filter applied to a steady harmonic spectrum) reproduces both without a switch, including the continuous glide of perceived pitch at high mouth tunings.
- (Mine.) The Nikolsky group's ">10 dB above the base level" is their detection criterion for calling a peak a formant, not a measured prominence, and should not be quoted as "the formant stands 10 dB above its neighbours".

### Gaps
- No measurement of the mouth's impedance or transfer function during jaw harp playing exists in what I found (nothing like the UNSW impedance-probe work on the didgeridoo). The resonance frequency, bandwidth and Q in this section come from a hobbyist's spectra, from spectra of the radiated sound, or from my arithmetic.
- Which vocal-tract resonance carries the melody is asserted, not demonstrated: Vandré says the second formant, Adkins "the natural resonance frequency of the mouth", Trias (second hand) a Helmholtz front cavity for the low line. No source separates a front-cavity resonance from F2.
- Ledang 1972, Crane 1968, Leipp 1963/1967 and Trias 2010 could not be read; Adkins 1974 only as an abstract. Leipp's bulletin is the source Nikolsky et al. cite for "JH formants differ from speech/singing formants for the same vowels" and may hold the only formant table.
- Spectrogram figures in Nikolsky et al. (Fig. 3, Fig. 6) carry dB values per harmonic that I could not read from the text extraction.

## 2. Which harmonics are usable, the resulting scale, why low harps give more, and the drone

### Takeaway
The usable harmonics are whichever fall inside the player's resonance range of roughly 0.5–2 kHz, so the numbers depend on the fundamental: about 4–12 or 5–10 on high-pitched harps (the classic textbook and Balinese figures), about 8–32 on medium ones, and 13–23 measured for the melody formant on a 72 Hz khomus. The scale is the natural harmonic series; a lower fundamental puts more harmonics, more closely spaced, into the same frequency window. The fundamental pitch is always perceived, but in the sound radiated from the mouth the fundamental component itself is weak; the drone is carried by the low harmonics and by the periodicity.

### Cited Findings
- CLAIMED (encyclopedia, quoting Wheatstone 1828): with the mouth cavity altered, "the series of reciprocated sounds would be C, E, G, B♭, C, D, E, F, G, etc."; "by using two or more instruments in different keys, a complete scale may by obtained"; "The vibration of the tongue itself corresponds with a very low sound"; Charles Eulenstein "by using 16 jew's harps produced extraordinary effects" in London in 1827–28. — [de Pontigny, Grove's Dictionary 1880, "Jew's Harp"](https://musikwissenschaften.de/lexikon/m/maultrommel/jews-harp-1880/)
- CLAIMED (encyclopedia): "The lower harmonics of the series cannot be obtained, owing to the limited capacity of the resonating cavity"; a full scale is shown for "two harps, one tuned a fourth above the other". — Schlesinger, Encyclopædia Britannica 1911, as quoted in [Wikipedia, Jew's harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew%27s_harp)
- DESCRIBED (ethnography, Balinese genggong, bamboo, string-pulled): "only a handful of these overtones can be resonated within the mouth cavity's possible range of movement. As a result, only the overtones five through ten are actually playable". "Genggong wadon typically has a range of harmonics from the 5th (low ning) to the 9th (high nang)"; genggong lanang "usually starts on the 7th harmonic ... and goes to the 10th"; a higher note "is often difficult to sound as genggong lanang's fundamental is usually too high to make that harmonic reachable". Example: harmonics 5–9 are "E, G, Bb, C, D on a Jew's harp with a fundamental of C". — [Morgan 2008, p. 49–55](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)
- DESCRIBED (player's chart; the octave spans read by me off the figure and converted to harmonic numbers): the main resonance covers harmonics 4–16 on "high" harps, 8–32 on "medium" and 16–64 on "low" ones; the accompanying lower resonance covers the octave below each (2–4, 4–8, 8–16). — [Vandré, figure "tonescale"](http://www.rvandre.de/grafiken/tonescale.gif), explained at [Tones and Pitches](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html)
- DESCRIBED: "On jew's harps with a lower fundamental note more different pitches can be played than on those with a higher fundamental note. E.g., a harp tuned A1 plays the multiplication tables from 55 Hz, wheras a harp tuned G plays that from 98 Hz. With the multiplication tables from 55 you will get more steps in the resonance range between 250 and 2000 Hz than with those from 98 Hz." Example fundamentals: 98 Hz (G) and 58.3 Hz (B1). — [Vandré, Tones and Pitches](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html)
- DESCRIBED: "No tone inside this octave can be played" between harmonics 1 and 2; one tone between 2 and 4, three between 4 and 8; "At the higher part of the row the overtones are so near each other that practically every melody can be played irrespective of the fundamental note." Harmonic 11 "will diverge by 49 cent" from the tempered scale (his "10th overtone"). — [Vandré, Tones and Pitches](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html) and [Playing Techniques](http://www.rvandre.de/playtech.html)
- MEASURED: on a 72 Hz khomus the melody ("vocal") formant sits on harmonics 13 to 23 depending on the vowel, and the low "instrumental" formant on harmonics 3–5 (see section 1). — [Nikolsky et al. 2020, Fig. 3B](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051/pdf)
- DESCRIBED (Yakut convention): the eight Yakut vowels are numbered "according to their perceived 'pitch values' in an ascending scale (Ogotoyev, 1988)", an "articulatory scale"; in the Russian figure the order is 1 [O], 2 [A], 3 [Ö], 4 [U], 5 [Y], 6 [Ä], 7 [Ü], 8 [I], "with the correction by Shishigin (2015) that places 'o' rather than 'a' at the bottom". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051/pdf); order of the vowels from [Part 2, Fig. 1](https://lang-folk.ru/sites/default/files/2026-09/2020_9_24_Alekseyev_Part_2.pdf)
- DESCRIBED: Yakuts "do not consider the khomus to be frequency-based. They evaluate its sound not as 'pitch,' but as 'coloration.'" Playing tunes such as "Mary had a little lamb" is called an "inauthentic, Western-style treatment". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051/pdf)
- DESCRIBED / MODEL, the drone: "A drone-like FF turns into 'background,' while changes in residue comprise 'foreground'"; the harp "produces the fundamental note ... that is heard continuously while playing"; "the fundamental pitch can be heard droning underneath the harmonics played above it. To the untrained ear, the fundamental frequency can sometimes seem to dominate". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051/pdf); [Vandré, Playing Techniques](http://www.rvandre.de/playtech.html); [Morgan 2008, p. 48](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)
- MEASURED / MODEL, what the drone is made of: the khomus spectrum peaks at harmonic 9, not at the fundamental; "A JH can also omit a harmonic (usually f2), or generate a non-serial partial or a subharmonic"; the "sum of the unresolved partials ... is perceived as a pitch identical to FF, but harsher in timbre". Harps "made of organic materials usually deviate from the harmonic series in the tuning of partials". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051/pdf)
- MEASURED (hobbyist, read by me): at the mouth the 98 Hz line is near the noise floor while the selected harmonic 11 is about 50 dB stronger; in the vice the fundamental is the strongest line. — [Vandré, figure "open-closed"](http://www.rvandre.de/grafiken/open-closed.gif)
- DESCRIBED (ethnomusicological transcription, Chuvan frame harps, via Nikolsky): four textural layers: "(1) the FF; (2) its subharmonic chest resonance an octave below; (3) the middle melodic layer above FF controlled by the player's breathing and tongue position, exhibiting 'talking' qualities; and (4) a high melodic layer that changes the sustained phonemic formants which resembles singing." — Dobzhanskaya 1991, as reported in [Nikolsky et al. 2020, note 62](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051/pdf)
- MEASURED (spectral texture analysis of five recordings): "In most cases, the texture spreads from about 70 Hz up to 3–5 kHz"; a bronze bow-shaped harp gives "a single melody with accompaniment" (pedal bass, melody in the "tenor", ostinato chords above), a grass harp five simultaneous, weakly controlled parts. Players can "consistently and reliably control no more than 2–3 melodic parts". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020, Fig. 6 and note 63](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051/pdf)
- MEASURED (overtone singing, for comparison): with a 90 Hz fundamental and the one-cavity technique "les harmoniques exploitables sont H4 (360 Hz), 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 (1080 Hz)"; with the two-cavity technique the melody harmonics lie "surtout dans la zone de 1 à 2 KHz"; a female voice at 240 Hz has only H3 to H5 usable with one cavity. — [Zemp and Trân Quang Hai 1991](https://journals.openedition.org/ethnomusicologie/1572)

### Inferences
- (Mine.) Number of harmonics inside 500–2000 Hz: fundamental 55 Hz gives harmonics 10–36 (27 of them), 72 Hz gives 7–27 (21), 98 Hz gives 6–20 (15), 110 Hz gives 5–18 (14), 147 Hz gives 4–13 (10), 196 Hz gives 3–10 (8). This is the whole reason "low harps give more notes".
- (Mine.) Step size between neighbouring harmonics n and n + 1: 386 cents at n = 4, 204 at 8, 139 at 12, 105 at 16, 71 at 24, 53 at 32. A diatonic-looking scale needs harmonics 8–16; above 16 the steps are a semitone or less, so a low harp can approximate any tune and a high one cannot.
- (Mine.) The classic "4 to 12" (Wheatstone's C, E, G, B♭, C, D, E, F, G is harmonics 4–12 of a C two octaves lower) and the Balinese "5 to 10" both fit the 0.5–2 kHz window if the fundamentals are around 130–170 Hz (5 × 131 Hz = 655 Hz, 10 × 131 Hz = 1310 Hz). The fundamentals of those instruments are not stated in the sources; this is a consistency check, not a finding.
- (Mine.) For MIDI pitch: the lowest reachable melody harmonic is ceil(500 Hz / f0) with a softer floor near 250 Hz for the accompaniment resonance, and the highest is floor(2000–2300 Hz / f0). Outside that window a selected harmonic should fade, not jump.
- (Mine.) The synthesizer's "drone" should not be a loud sine at the fundamental. The published spectra have a weak fundamental (and sometimes a missing second harmonic) at the mouth, a persistent group at harmonics 3–5, and a melody group higher up; the low pitch is a residue pitch.

### Gaps
- No source measures the highest harmonic number a player can isolate cleanly, nor how the prominence falls as the harmonics crowd together at high numbers on low harps.
- "Up to the 16th" as a general limit was not found stated anywhere; the numbers found are instrument-specific.
- The fundamental's absolute level in the radiated sound is known only from one hobbyist recording (possibly affected by the microphone's low-frequency response).

## 3. What each part of the vocal tract does: tongue, lips, jaw, cheeks, larynx, soft palate, pharynx, glottis; two resonances at once; comparisons

### Takeaway
The tongue does the tuning (as in the vowel series u–o–a–e–i, and as in whistling), the lips and jaw are largely fixed by the instrument, and the throat end of the tract is a switch with three positions: glottis open (lungs coupled, louder and lower-sounding, all harmonics available), glottis closed (sharper, and it favours the odd harmonics), and tongue against the soft palate (only the front of the mouth resonates, formants about twice as high). The closed-glottis claim is supported by one hobbyist measurement series and by the speech literature on bandwidths; nobody has measured it properly on a jaw harp.

### Cited Findings
- DESCRIBED, tongue and vowels: "On the jew's harp different pitches are produced by the same movements of the tongue used in speech when pronouncing the different vowels"; the exercise is the vowel u ("boot") gliding to i ("reed") and back. "The only difference between whistling tunes and playing them on the jew's harp is the way the sound is generated. The movements of the tongue ... are equal and produce the same pitches." — [Vandré, Playing Techniques](http://www.rvandre.de/playtech.html)
- CLAIMED (encyclopedias): the player "frames their mouth as though intending to pronounce the various vowels" (Britannica 1911); the German article gives the vowel series "a-o-u-ö-e-i" and "Durch Verändern des Mundhohlraumvolumens und seiner Geometrie". — [Wikipedia, Jew's harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew%27s_harp); [Wikipedia (de), Maultrommel](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maultrommel)
- DESCRIBED (Sakha players, with measured registers in section 1): "front" syllables "require tension in throat, face, lips, jaw, cheeks and tongue—isolating the mouth chamber"; "back" syllables "require relaxation in vocal apparatus"; "high" syllables "resemble 'front' articulation, except that facial muscles remain relaxed"; "low" syllables "strongly activate the soft palate, configuring it into a 'cupola'" and are associated with "the sound of a big church-bell". How strained the larynx is colours the high and low syllables. "reconfigurations of larynx and pharynx are usually done automatically—based on the reflexes established by speech." — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051/pdf)
- MEASURED / MODEL, ü and u: "U" uses the same harmonics as "O" but with "a smoother shape and consistent dynamic prevalence of odd over even harmonics (>f13)"; "Ü" uses the same as "Ö" with "an even greater contrast of odd/even harmonics and louder high harmonics >f47". Vowels close in formant position are told apart by "an odd/even harmonic ratio" and by the width of the band the formant occupies. — [Nikolsky et al. 2020, Fig. 3B](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051/pdf)
- DESCRIBED, lips and jaw: for the low resonance "the lips are not moved, they stay on the jew's harp frame"; bow-shaped harps "have to touch the opened incisors". With a 200–300 g weight clamped to the harp it can be played against the lips alone, and then "the lips can be opend and closed while playing"; the sound "is the same as normal (only the player himself/herself will hear the sound differently, as it is transported to the ears via the skull when using the teeth)". Against pursed lips "The sound is muted. If the lips are closed, the reed claps against them." — [Vandré, Playing Techniques](http://www.rvandre.de/playtech.html)
- MODEL (anatomist; cross-section drawings and spectrograms, no numbers): three basic ways of sounding. "[A] Laryngeal sounds: respiratory position. The glottis is kept open ... The sounds resonate to oral, pharyngeal, laryngeal cavities, and trachea." The soft palate may hang down (nose open) or be lifted. "[B] Pharyngeal sounds: vowel position. Glottis is closed and the soft palate is lifted up to close the nasal cavity ... The sounds resonate to oral and pharyngeal cavities." "[C] Oral sounds: nasal sound position. Soft palate is pulled down to close the oral cavity ... The sounds resonate to anterior oral cavity." — [Abe 2010, IJHS Newsletter 11, p. 7–8](https://jewsharpsociety.org/storage/app/media/IJHS%20Publications/NEWSLETTERS/2010.12.24%20IJHS%20Newsletter%2011.pdf)
- MODEL (same source): "The sounds of [A] and [B] show similar formants, F1, F2, and F3, and the sounds of [C] show F1 of two times higher frequencies"; "A half shorter resonance space of [C] than [B] produces two times higher formants." The laryngeal sounds [A] "have the longest resonance space to emphasize low frequency". "The [B][C] sounds are just a two times wide harmonics pattern. The difference reflects effects of open-end air column in [A] and closed-end air columns in [B][C]". — [Abe 2010, IJHS Newsletter 11, p. 8](https://jewsharpsociety.org/storage/app/media/IJHS%20Publications/NEWSLETTERS/2010.12.24%20IJHS%20Newsletter%2011.pdf)
- MEASURED (hobbyist), closed against open: with the glottis closed or the tongue on the soft palate the odd harmonics 9–15 stand 13–17.5 dB above their neighbours and the even harmonics cannot be brought above theirs; with an open tract and gentle breathing both odd (+9 dB) and even (+4 dB) harmonics can be selected (table in section 1). In the author's words (his numbering reversed): "Odd-numbered overtones can only be heared clearly with open playing. The closed play modes 'palate' and 'glottis' amplify the even overtones very clearly, but not the odd ones". "The reason for this is not clear to me so far." — [Vandré, Tones and Pitches](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html)
- DESCRIBED (Norwegian tradition, via Vandré): "in traditional Norwegian jew's harp playing, musicians most often open and close the vocal tract for the respective tones of the scale"; a Norsk Munnharpeforum workshop chart marks each scale note "Åpen" or "Lukket". — [Vandré, Tones and Pitches](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html)
- DESCRIBED (Balinese genggong against other traditions): "genggong uses an open glottis, using the oral cavity at its maximum resonant capacity. That is what gives genggong its characteristically deep and guttural sound." The same harmonics "can be sounded when the glottis is closed—but can be arrived at with different phonemes. However, the resonance is totally different with a closed glottis, and open glottis playing is much louder because of the extra resonating cavity." — [Morgan 2008, p. 55](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)
- DESCRIBED, two resonances at once: with the glottis closed "in addition to the 'normal' overtones deeper overtones are amplified. Two overtones can be heard simultaneously and influenced independent of each other." Demonstration on a C harp: melody note e3 with accompanying g1, then g3 with e1. The low notes are formed by going through "u - o - a - o - u" without voice while holding the melody note, and "are especially clear when you breath softly while playing, or when you close the voice chink". — [Vandré, Playing Techniques](http://www.rvandre.de/playtech.html) and [Tones and Pitches](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html)
- CLAIMED (encyclopedia, citing Mamcheva 2005, my translation): "The oral cavity and pharynx, and also the nasal cavity and the lower airways, serve as the resonator"; the timbre is changed "by controlling the articulatory apparatus and the breath", "while the fundamental sounds as a constant drone". — [Wikipedia (ru), Варган](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Варган)
- CLAIMED (encyclopedia, unsourced sentence): the mouth is used "plus the throat and lungs when breathing freely" as a resonator; the note is changed by the mouth shape "and in some traditions closing the glottis". — [Wikipedia, Jew's harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew%27s_harp)
- CLAIMED (second hand): Trias's thesis is reported to place the larynx in an "extremely high position" for the Helmholtz-cavity notes. — [noseflute.org blog, 2014](https://nose-flute.blogspot.com/2014/09/nose-flute-physics-ii.html)
- MEASURED, overtone singing (the nearest well-measured analogue; a voiced source, not a jaw harp): three Tuvan singers, fundamentals about 140–164 Hz, focus "a narrow spectral band around 1.5–2 kHz"; "±1 overtones are as much 15–35 dB and ±2 overtones are 35–65 dB below the central overtone"; the focus is made "by merging two broader formants together (F2 and F3...)"; transitions into and within the focused state take "∼40–60 ms"; one singer held a second, independent focus at "approximately 3–3.5 kHz". Dynamic MRI: a tight constriction near the alveolar ridge brings F2 and F3 together, and the constriction near the uvula sets the frequency. — [Bergevin et al. 2020, eLife 9:e50476](https://elifesciences.org/articles/50476)
- MEASURED, overtone singing (one singer, fundamental 138 Hz; OCR text with interleaved columns, the first quotation joined by me across a column break): "The bandwidth of the second formant is very narrow and, especially for the lower overtones, seldom exceeds 40 Hz. This indicates little acoustic damping in production: firm glottal closure and small losses in the vocal tract." Above 800 Hz the peak is "a combination of the second and third formant"; strong nasalisation separates the overtone formant from the first formant for the lower overtones. — [Bloothooft et al. 1991, ICPhS](https://www.coli.uni-saarland.de/Phonetics/icphs/ICPhS1991/12_ICPhS_1991_Vol_5/p12.5_014.pdf); journal version (abstract): [Bloothooft et al. 1992, JASA 92, 1827–1836](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.403839)
- MEASURED, overtone singing (abstract; inverse filtering and dynamic MRI, one singer): "the overtone enhancement resulted from a close clustering of formants 2 and 3"; "the second formant frequency was strongly dependent on the back cavity, and the third on the front cavity, which acted like a Helmholtz resonator, tuned by the tongue tip position and lip opening." — [Sundberg, Lindblom and Hefele 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34860148)
- MEASURED / DESCRIBED, overtone singing (French study with Trân Quang Hai as singer): one-cavity technique (tongue tip down, back of the tongue against the soft palate) and two-cavity technique (tongue tip against the palate); with less abdominal and pharyngeal contraction "la puissance de la mélodie d'harmoniques est nettement moindre et la sonorité plus matte", with strong pharyngeal blocking the melody is more "détachée"; in "khöömii de nez" (mouth closed) the melody is "noyée". Léothaud is quoted: "le pharynx et la bouche se comportent donc comme des résonateurs de Helmholtz". A Rajasthani singer's rhythmic accents are noted as "sans doute pour imiter le jeu de la guimbarde", and a Xhosa style as imitating the musical bow umrhubhe. — [Zemp and Trân Quang Hai 1991](https://journals.openedition.org/ethnomusicologie/1572)
- CLAIMED (second hand): Klingholz 1993 is cited for overtone-singing "resonances narrowing to approximately 20 Hz under pharyngeal constriction". — [Cámara et al. 2026, arXiv:2606.04943](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.04943v1)
- CLAIMED (university web page): in harmonic singing "one of the vocal tract resonances is made much stronger, while all the others are weakened", with "a small mouth opening and also a relatively tight constriction between the tongue and the roof of the mouth"; in the example with the eighth harmonic emphasised the fundamental is "only 8 dB lower". — [UNSW, Harmonic singing](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/xoomi.html)
- MEASURED, didgeridoo (summary page of the UNSW work): "positioning the tongue close to the hard palate" "produces a strong formant between 1 and 2 kHz"; with the tongue low there are no strong formants; with the vocal folds nearly closed "the reflection coefficient for sound waves travelling down the vocal tract is high for all but very low frequencies", when they are open "the reflection coefficient for frequencies near 1 kHz is much lower"; "Measurements on such systems show weaker resonances and weaker formants when the glottis is open." — [UNSW, yidaki page](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/yidakididjeridu.html)
- MEASURED, speech research, glottis closed against phonating (ten subjects, impedance measured through the lips): bandwidths of the first three resonances are 45–65 Hz (men) with the glottis closed and 85–90 Hz during phonation; a model experiment "show[s] that BR1 increases by 15% with open glottis, and by 35% with a rapid airflow of 6 L s⁻¹ through the glottis, compared to a closed glottis condition". Full table in section 8. — [Hanna, Smith and Wolfe 2016, JASA 139, 2924–2936](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/HannaBandwidth.pdf)
- MEASURED, speech research, the open glottis shifts the resonances upward: whispering raises R1–R4 by "255 Hz with standard deviation 90 Hz, 115 ± 105, 125 ± 125, and 75 ± 120 Hz"; "the effective glottal area ... increases to 40 ± 30 mm² during whispering". — [Swerdlin, Smith and Wolfe 2010, JASA 127, 2590–2598](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/whisper.pdf)

### Inferences
- (Mine.) "Closed glottis gives clearer overtones" is supported by three independent lines: Vandré's bars (17.5 dB closed against 9 dB open), the speech measurements (closed-glottis bandwidths roughly half of those in phonation), and the overtone-singing and didgeridoo results that tie narrow resonances to firm glottal closure. "Open glottis is louder" (Morgan) is a player's statement with no measurement; it does not contradict the first claim, since a broader, lower resonance can pass more total energy while isolating a single harmonic less well.
- (Mine.) The odd/even effect. In harmonic numbers, Vandré's finding is that a tract closed at the back cannot bring an even harmonic above its odd neighbours, while an open tract can. Abe describes the same thing as a "two times wide harmonics pattern" for closed sounds. The free lamella already favours odd harmonics by about ±6 dB (the "vice" row), so the closed tract may simply preserve the source's pattern while the open tract adds something that fills in the even harmonics. Neither author explains it, and I found no measurement that does. For a model, "throat open" should raise the even harmonics by several dB as well as broaden and lower the resonance.
- (Mine.) Abe's "[C] formants twice as high" gives a usable rule: closing the tract at the soft palate (the "ng" position) halves the resonating length, so the lowest resonance of the front cavity lands roughly where the second formant of the whole tract would be. It is an anatomist's inference from spectrograms; no frequencies are given.
- (Mine.) Vandré's two pages disagree on where each resonance is made: the "tones" page assigns the main (second-formant) resonance to "movements in the throat" and the lower one to "movements in the oral cavity", the "techniques" page says the main one is the whistling resonance of the mouth and the deep one is "formed in the throat". The second version agrees with phonetics (front cavity and tongue position for F2, pharynx and jaw for F1) and with Sundberg's and Bergevin's MRI results.
- (Mine.) Vandré's two-resonance demonstration in numbers, assuming Helmholtz pitch names and a C harp at 65.4 Hz: melody e3 is harmonic 20 (about 1308 Hz) with accompaniment g1 at harmonic 6 (about 392 Hz); swapped, melody g3 is harmonic 24 (about 1570 Hz) with e1 at harmonic 5 (about 327 Hz). The two resonances are thus about two octaves apart and are moved in opposite directions, which a fixed low formant could not do.
- (Mine.) How fast the tract can retune: the overtone singers' 40–60 ms transitions are an upper bound on speed for a skilled vocal tract and a sensible smoothing time for the resonance-frequency control; Abe's example was plucked 4–5 times per second.
- (Mine.) Jaw harp isolation (9–17.5 dB over neighbours spaced 98 Hz) is in the class of the weaker sygyt figures (15–35 dB over neighbours spaced about 150 Hz). A single resonance cannot give 35 dB at that spacing (it would need a bandwidth of about 5 Hz), which is why the singers need two merged formants; nothing published shows jaw harp players doing the same.

### Gaps
- No measurement on a jaw harp of bandwidth, level or resonance frequency for glottis open against closed, beyond Vandré's spectra. Nasal coupling, cheeks, jaw opening and larynx height are described only in words.
- No published acoustic comparison of the jaw harp with the mouth bow was found (the search allowance ran out before the mouth bow could be searched in depth). The comparisons with Tuvan or Mongolian overtone singing found are in passing only; a Russian encyclopedia sentence that the jaw harp's "playing technique is close to throat singing" was seen only as a search excerpt of [bigenc.ru](https://bigenc.ru/c/gorlovoe-penie-f1c3ba) (snippet), and the French term "voix guimbarde" for diphonic singing only in a search excerpt, not in the part of Zemp and Trân's text that I read (snippet).
- Kob 2004 and Levin and Edgerton 1999 were not obtained; two figures attributed to Kob's and Tsai's work in a search excerpt (about 15 dB gained by matching two resonances; a 10th harmonic more than 25 dB above its neighbours) could not be traced to a readable page and are not used.

## 4. Coupling back onto the lamella: does the mouth change its pitch, loudness or decay?

### Takeaway
Thin evidence. The sources treat the lamella's frequencies as fixed and the mouth as a filter; the only measured statement, from a student page, is that the decay time changes little with or without the mouth. Things that touch the lamella or come close to it (a finger, the lips) do change pitch and loudness.

### Cited Findings
- MEASURED (hobbyist): played at the mouth and in a vice "the peaks of both curves are at the same frequencies"; "The tones of a certain jew's harp are unalterable, yet the sounds are many and diverse". — [Vandré, Tones and Pitches](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html)
- MEASURED (spectrogram, anatomist): "The lines of harmonics are kept constant in positions and ranges during the play except for the laryngeal sounds [A]". — [Abe 2010, IJHS Newsletter 11, p. 8](https://jewsharpsociety.org/storage/app/media/IJHS%20Publications/NEWSLETTERS/2010.12.24%20IJHS%20Newsletter%2011.pdf)
- CLAIMED (student course wiki, bamboo kumbing, method not described): decay times of about 0.720 s (loud pluck), 0.569 s (medium) and 0.406 s (soft); the decay "changes little with or without the oral cavity". — [UBC Phys341 wiki, Kumbing](https://wiki.ubc.ca/Course:Phys341_2020/Kumbing)
- MEASURED / MODEL (via Morgan): the sustain depends on the instrument's geometry: "the smaller the distance between lamella and frame, the richer the harmonic spectrum and the longer it is sustained"; the genggong lamella "has a relatively short sustain, and must be constantly kept in motion by pulling the attached string". — [Morgan 2008, p. 24 and 57](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)
- DESCRIBED, things that do shift the lamella: a finger knocking on the lips after the pluck: "At each knock the amplitude and pitch of the jew's harp sound is slightly raised" (a vibrato attributed to Vladiswar Nadishana); a fingernail touching the vibrating reed raises the fundamental "for an instant"; pinching the reed mutes it and raises its pitch. — [Vandré, Playing Techniques](http://www.rvandre.de/playtech.html)
- CLAIMED (encyclopedia): "The note or tone thus produced is constant in pitch". — [Wikipedia, Jew's harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew%27s_harp)
- DESCRIBED: breath does act on the lamella: it can set it in motion and make the sound louder (section 5).

### Inferences
- (Mine.) Treating the lamella as an independent source whose frequency does not depend on the mouth shape is consistent with everything found. The measured exceptions are contact effects (finger, lips) and airflow (section 5), which a model can add as separate small pitch and level modulations.
- (Mine.) Whether a mouth resonance tuned onto a strong low harmonic takes enough energy from the lamella to shorten its decay is an open physical question; Adkins's and Ledang's "aerophone" view (the sound is made by air driven through the gap) would allow it.

### Gaps
- No measurement of lamella frequency, amplitude or decay time against mouth shape, glottis state or breath was found. Adkins 1974 (full text) may contain one.

## 5. Breath

### Takeaway
Players and encyclopedias agree that breathing in or out through the harp makes it louder, strengthens the low end, adds rasping noise, links rhythmically to the plucks (one breath direction per stroke in several traditions), and can even drive the lamella with no pluck. Numbers are almost absent, and no source says how inhaling differs in sound from exhaling, apart from one Balinese rule that ties particular notes to each direction.

### Cited Findings
- DESCRIBED: "Separated, articulated tones can be produced by short breaths. When breathing in and out, the air stream amplifies the fundamental note." With regular plucking, "By breathing rasping sounds are produced between the plucks." Breathing is "done by the diaphragm". — [Vandré, Playing Techniques](http://www.rvandre.de/playtech.html)
- CLAIMED (encyclopedia): "Durch das gleichzeitige Hindurchblasen bzw. -ziehen von Luft wird dieser Klang verstärkt"; sound and pitch are influenced by the mouth cavity "und durch Atmung". — [Wikipedia (de), Maultrommel](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maultrommel)
- DESCRIBED, sounding the lamella by breath alone: harps held to the lips or teeth "begin to sound without being plucked, just by breathing in or out"; "The reed has to be shifted a little from the level of the frame, and the air flow has to pull it back towards the frame level"; on bow-shaped harps the thumb pushes the reed's base slightly. "In this way the jew's harp works just like a harmonica." (Explanation credited to Clemens Voigt of Dan Moi; a sound example plays one note on a harmonica, plucked on the harp, and "stimulated by breathing in".) — [Vandré, Tones and Pitches](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html)
- DESCRIBED (my translation): the jaw harp "can be excited without the hands, by breath alone or by a movement of the tongue". — [Nikolsky et al. 2019 (Part 1), note 4](https://lang-folk.ru/sites/default/files/2026-09/01_96.pdf)
- DESCRIBED (ethnomusicologist, Southeast Asian harps): the harp "must be held at the mouth (jaw) while inhaling or exhaling at each rhythmic stroke to produce music." — [Catlin 1980, Smithsonian Festival of American Folklife program book](https://folklife-media.si.edu/docs/festival/program-book-articles/FESTBK1980_09.pdf)
- DESCRIBED (Balinese genggong): "Many Jew's harp traditions employ the use of breathing in and out in rapid succession, as it keeps the momentum going on the instrument and allows for faster playing." In sixteenth-note interlocking patterns "half the notes are played while exhaling and the other half are played by inhaling"; in slower melodic playing "the notes are all generally sounded by exhaling". Accents coincide with the alternation (exhale accented, inhale a passing note in the author's figure). — [Morgan 2008, p. 56–57](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)
- DESCRIBED (Balinese teacher, quoted by Morgan): "Two of the aforementioned tones, that is, dang and deng, are made with the technique of ngengkahin (exhaling), and dung and ding are not (inhaling)." Also: "the adjustments of the mouth cavity and the blowing or inhaling, can dramatically change the sound quality". — Rai 1998 and 2004, as quoted in [Morgan 2008, p. 55–57](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)
- DESCRIBED (video lesson page, teacher named only as "Sanshi"): "making combinations such as breathe in-pluck, breath out-pluck, or breath in breath out then pluck"; "Create a vibrating sound while playing the Jews Harp and breathing in and out." — [Didgeridoo Dojo, lesson 9 of 9](https://www.didgeridoodojo.com/?p=13388)
- DESCRIBED (Chuvan frame harps): the "middle melodic layer above FF" is "controlled by the player's breathing and tongue position". — Dobzhanskaya 1991, in [Nikolsky et al. 2020, note 62](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051/pdf)
- CLAIMED (student course wiki, method not described): breathing in and out while playing a bamboo kumbing boosts the amplitude of the lower frequencies, from 280 Hz to 1 kHz. — [UBC Phys341 wiki, Kumbing](https://wiki.ubc.ca/Course:Phys341_2020/Kumbing)
- MEASURED (student project, weak): with breathing out, "the general spectrum has not changed"; breath "should not change the general spectrum except for the amplitude of some harmonics"; with the breath held after a deep inhalation a peak near 3000 Hz disappeared. — [Li 2015, UIUC Physics 406 report](https://courses.physics.illinois.edu/phys406/sp2017/Student_Projects/Spring15/Simon_Li_Physics_406_Final_Report_Sp15.pdf)
- DESCRIBED: the deep accompanying notes "are especially clear when you breath softly while playing". — [Vandré, Playing Techniques](http://www.rvandre.de/playtech.html)
- CLAIMED (society newsletters): breathing is listed among the "techniques that are easier to master, such as plucking, breathing"; a festival ran "a very intensive breath playing techniques workshop led by Nikolaj Sobolev". — [Wright, BOING 1-2023](https://jewsharpsociety.org/storage/app/media/IJHS%20Publications/NEWSLETTERS/2023%20IJHS%20Newsletter_BOING_2023_01_sr.pdf); [BOING 3](https://jewsharpsociety.org/storage/app/media/BLOG/NEWSLETTER%20BOING/BOING003-online.pdf)
- CLAIMED (a free-reed specialist's summary; the two papers themselves were not read): "Frederick Crane and Ole Kai Ledang have returned to Mersenne's original view and have classified the Jew's harp as an aerophone, arguing that full functioning of the instrument occurs only when a stream of air moves past its tongue." The same page reports Mersenne's second view (1646), that the harp is a percussion instrument "because breath alone, without striking, does not make it yield any sound", which contradicts the breath-only sounding described by Vandré above. Adkins's pulse model (section 1) is the acoustic form of the aerophone idea. — [Doktorski, Taxonomy of Musical Instruments](https://henrydoktorski.com/free-reed/description/taxonomy.html)

### Inferences
- (Mine.) Breath does three separable things in the accounts: it opens the glottis and couples the airways (lower, broader resonance, all harmonics available; sections 1 and 3); the moving air feeds energy to the lamella (louder, longer, re-excitation without a pluck, with the lamella offset from the frame plane as in a free reed); and the flow makes noise (the "rasping" between plucks). A synthesizer can expose these as throat-open amount, a breath drive that slows or reverses the lamella's decay, and a breath-noise level shaped by the same mouth resonances.
- (Mine.) Since a free reed speaks in one flow direction only for a given offset, breath-driven sounding should favour one direction per instrument set-up; this follows from Vandré's harmonica comparison and is not stated by any source.
- (Mine.) Rhythmic use: the documented patterns are one breath direction per pluck, strict in/out alternation at sixteenth-note speed with the accent on the exhale (Bali), and free combinations of in, out and pluck. That is enough to justify a per-step breath direction and accent in a rhythm-first engine.

### Gaps
- No source states how the timbre differs between inhaling and exhaling, and no measurement of loudness gain from breath (in dB), of breath noise level or spectrum, or of breath-sustained decay time was found.
- German, Russian and French tutorials on breathing technique (Atemtechnik, дыхание, respiration) were searched once each without useful hits before the search allowance ran out; the Dan Moi tutorial pages refused automated access. Diaphragm pulses are mentioned only in passing.

## 6. Voice and glottis: singing with the harp, glottal gating, throat sounds, whispered and "talking" playing, clicks

### Takeaway
The voice is not needed (people without a voice can play), and when it is used with the harp the two interfere. What traditions use instead is unvoiced articulation: whispered phonemes, tongue strokes that open and shut the resonance, and opening and closing the glottis, which is audible as an articulation of its own. The "talking" harp works because the mouth filters the lamella exactly as it filters the voice; in Hmong it also carries lexical tone. No measurements of any of these effects were found.

### Cited Findings
- DESCRIBED, glottal closing as an effect and an articulation: after a melody "the chink is closed and opened repeatedly and the additional tones of the altered resonance are heard as well as the opening and closure of the glottis." The action is the one that starts a vowel in speech or holds the breath before a cough. — [Vandré, Playing Techniques](http://www.rvandre.de/playtech.html)
- DESCRIBED, tongue as a gate: "the tongue opens and closes the oral cavity, allowing and interrupting resonance"; slow notes on "de de de", fast ones on "doodle doodle doodle"; staccato by rapid plucking with "det det dedledet"; silently saying "ing ing ing ing" (tongue to soft palate) and "ne ne ne" give further effects. "This technique is easy and can be played very fast. Nevertheless it is rarely heard on jew's harp recordings." — [Vandré, Playing Techniques](http://www.rvandre.de/playtech.html)
- DESCRIBED (MRI project report by a professional player): the positions scanned were "five tones: highest, middle, low and two throat tones", and "a complete intonation cycle (from highest to lowest voice, open and closed epiglottis with throat sounds)". — [Szilágyi, BOING 1-2023, p. 24–27](https://jewsharpsociety.org/storage/app/media/IJHS%20Publications/NEWSLETTERS/2023%20IJHS%20Newsletter_BOING_2023_01_sr.pdf)
- DESCRIBED, voice not required: the harp "even allows aphoniacs to emit sounds (Shimomura, 2016)"; its lamella "makes women, men, the old, and the young sound the same". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051/pdf)
- DESCRIBED, singing while playing (Sakha): the closeness of the playing style syyia tardy to the song style toyuk "enables the simultaneous singing and playing"; in a recorded improvisation "Instrumental and vocal parts interfere with each other: khomus as though obstructs attempts of the voice to carry out a coherent song". Yakuts treat it as "'coloring' the same song". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051/pdf)
- DESCRIBED, the talking khomus: syyia tardy ("moderate playing") is "nicknamed 'talking khomus'"; "The bulk of the JH's autochthonous repertory consists of speech-like articulations and special sound effects"; tabygyr is staccato; Yakut players "frequently engage the timbral vocable 'hyttya'". Ensemble playing "would mess up the clarity of articulation". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051/pdf)
- MODEL, why it can talk and sing at once: the low part of the spectrum is the same for all articulations and the upper part changes with each phoneme, "Hence, the JH can 'talk' AND 'sing' sequentially as well as simultaneously." The harp's "capacity ... to faithfully reproduce vowels and some consonants of a language is often employed to deliver words and phrases at the closest distance". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020, text and note 45](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051/pdf)
- DESCRIBED, syllables played on the harp: Nivkh patterns "khai t'ok khai-nak-nak" or "khavr-khavr khaf-khaf-khaf" (imitating barking) and sustained "ko lyo-lyo-lyo, with vibrato of the lower lip or tip of the tongue"; a Mansi girl repeating "Gav-ri-ke, gav-ri-ke, vas pyrish, vas pyrish!" on the tumran (Tchernetsov's diary, 1926–27). — [Nikolsky et al. 2020, notes 54 and 56](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051/pdf)
- DESCRIBED, whispering (Bali): "The key to genggong technique is to whisper the phoneme, or the most minimal defining unit of the note's name, into the instrument"; the vowels of the solfège names ning, nong, neng, nung, nang ("i o e u a") each shape one resonance. — [Morgan 2008, p. 54–55](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)
- DESCRIBED, Hmong courting speech: "The Hmong use the jaw harp to convey messages in courtship. A young man serenades the one he has chosen by composing his thoughts into words and then expressing them through the jaw harp. The young woman replies with her own instrument. Hmong is a tonal language with eight tones, so it is possible to produce the rising, falling, or straight tones at the appropriate high, middle, or low pitch levels on any melodic instrument." (Of the Vietnamese monochord the same article says "All the tones produced are harmonics, as in the jaw harp. Thus, it must be played in quiet places and in close company.") — [Catlin 1980](https://folklife-media.si.edu/docs/festival/program-book-articles/FESTBK1980_09.pdf)
- DESCRIBED: "in Hmong tradition JH not only imitates linguistic phonemes, but also engages tone intonations (Poss, 2012)"; Li girls on Hainan "use JH to camouflage their voices in their confession of love". — [Nikolsky et al. 2020, notes 44–45](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051/pdf)
- CLAIMED (snippet; the article refused automated access): "In Hmong, the backbone of the jaw harp's speech surrogacy is the language's tone system reproduced not by the larynx but through oral harmonics, with a secondary consonantal contrast reproduced in the plucking pattern of the instrument", contrasted with an Altai tradition. — [Language Documentation and Description, article 2939](https://www.lddjournal.org/article/id/2939/)
- MEASURED (abstract; on Hmong free-reed pipes, not the jaw harp): skilled listeners identified played phrases with high success and "relied mainly on the links between musical pitch and lexical tone, not broad differences in syllable onsets". — [Poss 2012, Ohio State dissertation, catalogue record](https://hmongstudies.library.wisc.edu/catalog/HmongStudies2591)
- DESCRIBED (Huli, Papua New Guinea, via Morgan): words are articulated on both the jaw harp and the mouth bow, "a unique instrumental/musical/poetic/linguistic phenomenon" (Pugh-Kitingan 1977); the harp's "ability to convey some consonants as well as vowels means that it can be used to convey speech in non-tonal languages as well". Ledang is quoted: "the ultimate sound produced by a jaw's harp player cannot be adequately described without reference to phonetics". — [Morgan 2008, p. 25–26](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)
- CLAIMED (snippet): in Tuvan music the jaw harp is used together with overtone and undertone singing. — [Austria-Forum, Maultrommel](https://austria-forum.org/af/AustriaWiki/Maultrommel)

### Inferences
- (Mine.) For a synthesizer the "consonants" of jaw harp speech reduce to four gates on an otherwise continuous lamella sound: the pluck pattern, tongue closures that cut the resonance (d, t, n, l, ng shapes), glottal closures that switch the tract between its open and closed states (with an audible change of harmonic balance, section 3), and breath bursts. None needs a voice source.
- (Mine.) Sung or hummed voice together with the harp is documented for the Sakha and Tuvan traditions but described as interfering; as a feature it is an optional second source through the same mouth filter, not part of the core sound.
- (Mine.) Hmong tone-carrying means the selected harmonic glides or steps within a syllable; with harmonics a semitone or more apart this can only be a stepped contour unless the fundamental is low or Adkins's high-frequency continuous regime applies.

### Gaps
- No measurements of glottal-stop gating (attack time, level change), laryngeal pulsing, growling or humming with the harp were found; no source mentions tongue clicks.
- The Sakha technique literature (Shishigin, Alekseyev, Ogotoyev), Catlin's 1982 paper on Hmong speech surrogates (catalogue record only, no abstract) and the Dan Moi article on "speaking jew's harps" (access refused) were not read. The Hmong claim about consonants in the plucking pattern rests on a search excerpt.

## 7. Articulatory measurements of jaw harp playing (MRI, ultrasound, X-ray, palatography, endoscopy) and formant tables

### Takeaway
Two MRI efforts exist and neither has published numbers: Trias's 2010 master's thesis (not obtained) and Áron Szilágyi's 2023 static MRI project at the University of Malta, made for teaching. No ultrasound, X-ray, electropalatography or endoscopy study and no formant table for jaw harp articulations were found; the closest thing to a table is the Nikolsky group's list of harmonic numbers per Yakut vowel.

### Cited Findings
- DESCRIBED (project report): the University of Malta "has carried out what is believed to be the world's first project to examine a jaw harp player's mouth cavity in an MRI scanner"; partner Dr Claude J. Bajada (Boundaries of the Brain Lab); scanner booked for 30 September 2023. - (Mine.) The likely reading is that the strongest player-controlled peak stops near 1.7 kHz on this instrument and the bright "front" colour comes from an additional higher resonance.
- (Mine.) Szilágyi's MRI images, if published as planned, would allow area functions and computed resonances for a handful of held positions; as of the report they are teaching material.

### Gaps
- No ultrasound, X-ray, electropalatography or endoscopy of jaw harp playing was found; the search for them was cut short by the search allowance. Trias's thesis (said to contain MRI) and Abe's announced full-length article were not obtained.
- No table of formant frequencies in Hz with bandwidths exists for jaw harp articulations in anything I could read.
