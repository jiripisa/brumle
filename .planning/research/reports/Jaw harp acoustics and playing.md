# The reed keeps time, the slot makes the buzz, the mouth makes the tune

A jaw harp's steel tongue does not radiate the sound that is heard. It swings at one low pitch, typically 50–100 Hz on European forged instruments and 64–146 Hz on the eight instruments measured for this report, and each time it passes through the slot of its frame, twice per cycle, it fires a short pulse into the mouth ([Adkins 1974, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1914580); [Leipp 1967](https://www.lam.jussieu.fr/Publications/BulletinsGAM/GAM_25-La-Guimbarde.pdf); [recordings notes](../research_notes/Jaw%20harp%20acoustics%20and%20playing/recordings_measured.md)). The result is a strictly harmonic buzz of up to 145 partials with a weak fundamental, and the mouth, a resonance some 50–120 Hz wide that the tongue moves between about 0.5 and 3 kHz above a second one that stays near 400 Hz, lifts one harmonic or a small cluster 5–17 dB above its neighbours ([Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051); [Vandré](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html)). The literature gives almost none of the numbers a pluck synthesizer needs most, so this research measured eleven public recordings: a stroke is at full level within two periods of the reed, the brightness dies two to six times faster than the low harmonics, a low harp left alone falls 15–18 dB per second, a finger stops the reed for 25–50 ms before every re-pluck, and fast playing runs at 8–10 strokes per second. A decaying sine pushed through a "slot" law, in which each burst grows with the square of the reed's speed and shortens with its inverse, reproduces the five measured decay slopes of one instrument with two numbers; that is the source this report recommends, followed by parallel mouth resonances that the bursts strike. No published synthesis model of the jaw harp exists, the one open-source synthesizer is 16 sine partials through speech-vowel band-passes and its author calls that "the lower third" of the instrument, and the commercial imitations are sample libraries whose mouths cannot glide ([synthesis notes](../research_notes/Jaw%20harp%20acoustics%20and%20playing/synthesis_and_prior_art.md)). Only two traditions use the instrument as percussion in the strict sense, the South Indian morsing (short, loud, high) and the Balinese genggong (interlocking sixteenths, exhaled accents); everywhere else rhythm is made inside the ring of one pluck by tongue, throat and breath. That split decides the engine: almost everything that gives the instrument its words has to happen inside one hit. Nobody has published sweep times, tap rates, loudness in decibels, a decay time for a metal harp or the sign of the pulses, and the published claim that odd harmonics dominate did not hold in the measured recordings, so those parts of the instrument will be settled by the author's ear.

*Evidence labels used throughout.* **Measured** is a published measurement (student and hobby measurements are named as such). **Measured here** is this research's own analysis of eleven recordings from Wikimedia Commons, made on 2026-11-15 with scripts written for the purpose, without listening: unpublished, unreviewed, one family of players and one take per instrument. **Authors' interpretation** is a published researcher's own model or explanation. **Observed** is an ethnomusicologist's field description. **Described** is a player's or teacher's own account; **claimed** is a statement by a maker, vendor, journalist or encyclopedia; both are unmeasured. **Derived in the notes** is arithmetic or reasoning done by this project's research notes from published numbers. **Proposal** is this report's own design suggestion; none of it has been heard. "Snippet" marks a claim taken from a search-result excerpt, "abstract" a paper of which only the abstract was read. Checked against the source for this report: the Adkins abstract, pages 12–13 of Leipp 1967, the quotations from Nikolsky et al., Morgan's morsing sentence, the ISMIR stroke density and two source files of the one published synthesizer (`voice.rs`, `kubyz_f2.rs`). Not checked again: the other quotations, the vendor table's statistics and the readings of printed figures.

## The reed: a steel tongue at 50–150 Hz in a slot a tenth of a millimetre wide

### Two families, and the difference that matters is material and gap

**Claimed and measured (catalogues, museum records).** Bow-shaped harps have a steel reed fixed to a forged, bent or cast frame, are pressed against the teeth and are plucked on the reed's bent tip; in current production the frames are about 50–145 mm long, the reeds 45–105 mm, the mass 7–115 g. Lamellate harps have the reed cut from one strip of bamboo, palm or sheet brass, are held at the lips, weigh 2–12 g in brass, and are sounded by plucking the frame end beyond the reed's hinge or by jerking a string (Balinese genggong, Ainu mukkuri) ([instruments notes](../research_notes/Jaw%20harp%20acoustics%20and%20playing/instruments_and_tunings.md), from the [Oberton Pro table of 130 models](https://www.oberton.pro/en/lab.htm?sort=frequency_avg), museum catalogues and [Kolltveit 2016](https://musark.no/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/J-Harps-of-Bone-Wood-and-Metal.Kolltveit-2016.pdf)). The forged frame is square bar turned edge-on, so the reed is closely enclosed over only a short stretch of its travel ([reed notes](../research_notes/Jaw%20harp%20acoustics%20and%20playing/reed_and_sound_production.md)).

**Measured and claimed.** Organic harps are more damped and radiate fewer partials (the wooden guimbarde: "le système est plus amorti ... moins de partiels", [Leipp 1967](https://www.lam.jussieu.fr/Publications/BulletinsGAM/GAM_25-La-Guimbarde.pdf)); they "usually deviate from the harmonic series in the tuning of partials", may "omit a harmonic (usually f2)", and by material give "hollow" clicking (grass, bark), rattling with tremolo at high intensity (bamboo), a "clang" on the onset (wood) or, for metal, a sound "ringing or buzzing, resonant, very full and rich, the brightest ... with the longest decay" ([Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)). Thin brass leaf harps (đàn môi, kouxian) are a separate case: a very small gap gives them a full, stable spectrum, and they need no teeth ([reed notes](../research_notes/Jaw%20harp%20acoustics%20and%20playing/reed_and_sound_production.md)).

**Derived in the notes.** The split that matters for sound is not how the reed is attached but material and gap: bamboo, wood and bone damp heavily and rattle; steel and thin brass ring; a tight gap gives either a rich spectrum.

### Catalogues run from 27 to 165 Hz, most harps sit at 60–85 Hz, and makers name the reed's fundamental

**Measured.** "La guimbarde produit une large série de partiels quasi harmoniques du fondamental du système, qui varie généralement entre 50 et 100 Hz"; Leipp's three Austrian instruments are labelled about 80, 60 and 50 Hz ([Leipp 1967](https://www.lam.jussieu.fr/Publications/BulletinsGAM/GAM_25-La-Guimbarde.pdf), pp. 12–13, read for this report). A shop's table of 130 models gives fundamentals from 33 to 130 Hz with a median of 70 Hz and quartiles of 62.5 and 82.5 Hz (the statistics are the notes' arithmetic on the [Oberton Pro table](https://www.oberton.pro/en/lab.htm?sort=frequency_avg); the shop does not document its method).

**Measured here.** The eight single instruments recorded in Molln have fundamentals of 63.8 Hz (Sicilian), 66.0 Hz (Molln harp called "c2"), 73.1 Hz (Yakut khomus), 78.9 Hz (đàn môi "of iron"), 99.3 Hz (Mongolian), 109.4 Hz (Nepalese), 129.4 Hz (Indian, cast brass) and 146.1 Hz (Molln harp called "d3", "very high tone"). So the makers' note names are scientific-pitch names of the reed's own fundamental, C2 = 65.4 Hz and D3 = 146.8 Hz, and D3 already counts as very high ([recordings notes](../research_notes/Jaw%20harp%20acoustics%20and%20playing/recordings_measured.md)).

**Claimed (makers' lists).** The Szilágyi workshop's chart runs from A0 (27.5 Hz) to E3 (164.8 Hz), calling A0–B0 "very, very low, quiet, hard to sound; for meditation" and B2–E3 "ideal for fast playing; generally a tight spring" ([doromb.com tuning chart](https://www.doromb.com/shop_ordered/27095/pic/doromb-hangolas-chart-HU.pdf); translations by the notes). The Molln workshop Wimmer-Bades lists "A tief" to "D3 hoch", with pairs A+E, G+D, C+F and trios A+E+D, G+C+F ([Wimmer-Bades](https://www.maultrommel.at/produkte/gestimmte-maultrommel/)). In one shop's stock of tuned harps 75 of 102 specimens were in octave 2, 21 in octave 1 and 6 in octave 3, and C2, D2, E2, F2 and G2 were the commonest notes ([instruments notes](../research_notes/Jaw%20harp%20acoustics%20and%20playing/instruments_and_tunings.md)). Brass kouxian reeds are sold higher, C3 to D#4 (snippet).

**Thin.** The South Indian morsing is the one type conceived as high-pitched and tuned to the tonic of the music, but no source gives its pitch in hertz; a museum record's "approximately F3" (175 Hz) is a snippet from a page that could not be read ([Grinnell College](https://omeka-s.grinnell.edu/s/MusicalInstruments/item/2687)). Players name srutis "2 to 5" as optimal ([Lakshmi Anand, on Bhagyalakshmi M. Krishna](https://lakshmianand.com/?p=3384)), which the notes read as about D to G without a source for the octave.

**Claimed.** Pitch is lowered by mass at the tip (tin, solder, beeswax on the morsing, a clip-on magnet) or by thinning the reed near its root, and raised by filing or by changing the angle of the bent end; "tuned" means within ±10 cents ([Oberton Pro FAQ](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/faq.htm)). The measured instruments stand −43 to +24 cents from the nearest note.

**Derived in the notes.** A keyboard covers real steel harps with MIDI notes 21 to 52 (A0 to E3) and brass leaf reeds up to 63 (D#4). The sixteen pad notes 36–51 are C2 to D#3, 65–156 Hz: by coincidence exactly the span of the eight measured instruments and of the commonest catalogue notes.

### What is heard is not the bar: its own modes are inharmonic, the sound is not

**Textbook, computed in the notes.** A uniform clamped-free bar has modes at 1 : 6.27 : 17.55 : 34.39. A point mass at the tip (the bent trigger, a drop of solder) lowers the first mode and pushes the others away from it: at a tip mass of half the bar's mass the second mode stands at 8.4 times the first. Profiling works the other way: a plucked accordion reed was **measured** at 355 and 1645 Hz, a ratio of 4.6 ([Ziegenhals 2009](https://www.ifm-zwota.de/tonzun09.pdf)). No measurement of a jaw harp reed's higher modes was found.

**Measured.** A load on the reed's tip "baisse plus ou moins le fondamental et toute la série de partiels, selon sa masse" ([Leipp 1967](https://www.lam.jussieu.fr/Publications/BulletinsGAM/GAM_25-La-Guimbarde.pdf), p. 13): the partials move together with the fundamental, which modes of a bar would not do. A harp fixed in a vice gives peaks "at the same frequencies" as when played at the mouth ([Vandré, hobby measurement](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html)).

**Measured here.** In all eight instruments the ten strongest partials lie within ±5 cents of whole multiples of the fundamental, most within ±2 cents (two exceptions of 7 cents; resolution 1–3 cents). The reed's pitch does not glide: one harp read 66.21 Hz in the first 200 ms after a pluck and 66.01 Hz later, another 63.8 Hz falling to 63.5 Hz one second into the decay. A search for partials off the series found only small ones, 15–49 dB under the strongest harmonic; three instruments have one at 6.2–6.5 times the fundamental, where a uniform bar's second mode would lie, which is suggestive and no more.

**Authors' interpretation.** A plucked reed in its plate starts in its first and second bending modes, and each makes its own pulse series in the sound; the second fades quickly ([Ziegenhals 2009](https://www.ifm-zwota.de/tonzun09.pdf)). Successive strokes differ because "on ne pince pas la lame exactement chaque fois dans le plan du coude", which adds torsion ([Leipp 1967](https://www.lam.jussieu.fr/Publications/BulletinsGAM/GAM_25-La-Guimbarde.pdf)).

**Derived in the notes.** The inharmonic modes can add a faint, short "clank" at the pluck; they are not the source of the harmonic series.

### The slot makes the sound: one pulse per passage, two per cycle

**Measured (abstract; checked for this report).** "It is shown that the jew's harp excites the mouth cavity by producing short pulses when the lamella passes through the frame. A model is proposed for the mechanism by which the pulses are generated." ([Adkins 1974](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1914580)). The paper is paywalled; its pulse model, dimensions and any equations were not read. A student's retelling has the reed, while level with the frame arms, acting "like a piston driving forward the air in front of it, and dragging the air behind" ([Li 2015, student report](https://courses.physics.illinois.edu/phys406/sp2017/Student_Projects/Spring15/Simon_Li_Physics_406_Final_Report_Sp15.pdf)).

**Measured, as reported by others.** Ledang 1972 showed by experiment that the distance between reed and frame arms "has to be very small, not exceeding 0.2 millimetres, to produce distinct partials" ([Kolltveit 2016](https://musark.no/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/J-Harps-of-Bone-Wood-and-Metal.Kolltveit-2016.pdf)), that a reed "vibrating on its own does not constitute a harmonic series; it is the frame ... that creates a spectrum of harmonics", and that "the smaller the distance between lamella and frame, the richer the harmonic spectrum and the longer it is sustained" ([Morgan 2008](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)). Crane 1968 reached the same view independently and called the instrument an aerophone; neither paper was read.

**Claimed (makers).** Clearances on instruments on sale run from 10 to 200 µm with a median of 60 µm; "the smaller they are the better the sound (volume/sensitivity is higher and spectrum is wider)"; tight gaps on soft frames clank when the frame is squeezed ([Oberton Pro table](https://www.oberton.pro/en/lab.htm?sort=frequency_avg); [FAQ](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/faq.htm); [handling](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/handling/)).

**Measured (free reeds, transferable).** In a blown free reed "the reed motion appears to be approximately sinusoidal" while "the sound pressure waveform somewhat resembles a pulse wave, as the reed acts as a valve, alternately opening and closing the opening in the reed frame twice in each cycle" ([Cottingham, Lilly and Reed](https://www.cs.bu.edu/fac/snyder/TablatureWeb/BERLIN1.PDF)). Of a plucked, unblown accordion reed: "Es wirkt der gleiche Effekt wie bei der Maultrommel", the plunging of the reed into its plate "erzeugt Schallimpulse" ([Ziegenhals 2009](https://www.ifm-zwota.de/tonzun09.pdf)).

**Measured here.** Folded over the reed's period, the energy above about 4 kHz comes in two bursts per period, 0.48–0.50 of a period apart, the second 5–8 dB weaker than the first, on all four instruments examined. Right after a pluck the stretch between bursts is 16–24 dB down and the crest factor of the waveform is 16–20 dB (a sine has 3 dB); 0.6–0.8 s later the bursts stand only about 10 dB above the rest; after 1.4 s they are gone.

**Dissent.** Leipp's 1967 account does not use pulses: the exciter is "tout l'ensemble complexe des éléments languette-lyre-dents fortement couplés". His two measurements, many partials away from the mouth and the whole series following the fundamental, are what the pulse mechanism predicts ([reed notes](../research_notes/Jaw%20harp%20acoustics%20and%20playing/reed_and_sound_production.md)).

### Up to 145 harmonics, a weak fundamental, and an open question about odd and even

**Measured.** Plucked in front of a microphone, away from the mouth, a harp radiates "une large série de partiels (jusqu'à 60...) dont les numéros impairs sont prédominants, et les pairs très faibles"; the spectrum "n'est pas régulièrement décroissant vers l'aigu", it has preferred regions "qui proviennent des caractéristiques de la lyre et de celles des dents", and "l'amplitude varie à chaque coup" ([Leipp 1967](https://www.lam.jussieu.fr/Publications/BulletinsGAM/GAM_25-La-Guimbarde.pdf), read for this report). On a Yakut khomus at the mouth "there are 145 harmonics discernible above the noise floor, peaking at f9", against 23 for the same vowel sung; "JH reserves higher intensity for odd harmonics"; the partials are far narrower and less noisy than sung ones ([Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)). Two hobby and student measurements also report odd harmonics leading ([Vandré](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html); [Li 2015](https://courses.physics.illinois.edu/phys406/sp2017/Student_Projects/Spring15/Simon_Li_Physics_406_Final_Report_Sp15.pdf)); one student page says the opposite ([UBC wiki](https://wiki.ubc.ca/Course:Phys341_2020/Kumbing)).

**Measured here, and it disagrees.** In the eight recordings no single rule holds. Over the first twenty harmonics the even ones are 0.6–6.1 dB stronger in the plain mean. By region the lead changes: on the Mongolian harp the even harmonics lead by 7–9 dB from the 11th to the 30th; on the Nepalese harp even harmonics lead by 7 dB up to the 10th and odd ones by 4–7 dB above the 21st; five instruments stay within ±2 dB almost everywhere, changing sign from region to region. These players hold the instrument at the mouth and probably breathe through it.

**Measured here.** The component at the fundamental is weak: 17–31 dB under the strongest harmonic on average, and 17.5–32 dB under the harmonic the mouth is bringing out at any moment; harmonics 2 and 3 are usually as weak. Harmonics stay within 17 dB of the strongest up to about 3 kHz on a 66 Hz harp, and each harmonic's best level falls 4–9.5 dB per octave between 0.5 and 5 kHz.

**Authors' interpretation.** The tanpura-like quality of the drone comes from "the absence or weakness of the low partials" (Leipp, as quoted in the [reed notes](../research_notes/Jaw%20harp%20acoustics%20and%20playing/reed_and_sound_production.md)).

**Derived in the notes.** Two pulses of opposite sign per cycle, exactly half a period apart, contain only odd harmonics; a rest position off the frame plane, or a steady breath flow, adds even ones. With an offset d and an amplitude A the even harmonics grow as the note decays, and the pulses stop once A is smaller than d although the reed still moves. No source states the polarity; Adkins's paper might.

**Reading for the engine.** The pitch a listener names is carried by the harmonic series as a whole, not by a component at the fundamental, so a synthesized drone must not be a loud sine. And the balance of odd and even harmonics is a control, not a constant: the literature says odd for a bare, centred reed; the recordings say "it depends".

### A pluck is at full level at once, loses its brightness first, and is stopped by the finger before the next

**Described.** The finger plucks the tip, which "'échappe' et vibre librement plus ou moins longtemps, selon la raideur de la languette et l'amortissement général du système" ([Leipp 1967](https://www.lam.jussieu.fr/Publications/BulletinsGAM/GAM_25-La-Guimbarde.pdf)). Both directions are used and are alternated for fast rhythm ([Vandré](http://www.rvandre.de/playtech.html); [Czech Wikipedia](https://cs.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brumle)); sources disagree on which is the usual one. As strokes get stronger "loudness is increasing more or less evenly, and then the moment comes when the force is increasing, but loudness is not" ([Oberton Pro FAQ](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/faq.htm)). A crooked or too strong stroke, or a squeezed frame, makes the reed clatter on the frame arms, "un 'ferraillement' similaire à celui d'une vieille voiture" ([Leipp 1967](https://www.lam.jussieu.fr/Publications/BulletinsGAM/GAM_25-La-Guimbarde.pdf); [Oberton Pro, handling](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/handling/)); makers and teachers treat it as a fault.

**Published numbers for the decay are thin.** An encyclopedia citing Leipp 1963, which was not obtained, gives the reed "une décroissance assez rapide, qui peut être de 20 décibels par seconde" ([French Wikipedia](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guimbarde_(instrument))). A student measured 0.41 s (soft pluck) to 0.72 s (loud) on a bamboo kubing, "not ... drastically" changed by the mouth ([UBC wiki](https://wiki.ubc.ca/Course:Phys341_2020/Kumbing)). In Leipp's sonagrams one stroke lasts roughly 0.35–0.5 s in the low partials and under 0.2 s above 1.5 kHz, down to an unknown floor (the notes' reading of his Figure 6). No tip amplitude, attack time, Q or sound level of a jaw harp was found; blown free reeds swing with "an amplitude of 15% of the tongue length" ([Cottingham 2011](https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/acoustics-of-free-reed-instruments)).

**Measured here.**

**Derived here (a fit, not heard).** If the fundamental's level follows the reed's amplitude, its −7 to −8.5 dB/s means that the reed itself rings with an amplitude time constant of about 1.0–1.2 s, 60 dB in 7–8 s, and the sound as a whole falls two to four times faster only because the bursts soften. A simulation confirms it: a sine at 66 Hz decaying with a time constant of 1.1 s, swinging 18 slot half-widths at the pluck, through a slot law in which the burst is the gate (the reed in the slot) times the square of the reed's speed, with a breath flow of 0.17 of the reed's speed, gives −15.6, −8.3, −4.7, −7.5 and −19.6 dB/s for all harmonics, the fundamental, and harmonics 2–4, 5–10 and 11 up, against the measured −17.8, −8.5, −5.8, −8.5 and −20.0. A source that merely displaces a fixed volume of air per passage gives −8, level low harmonics and −13, and does not fit ([recordings notes, section 6](../research_notes/Jaw%20harp%20acoustics%20and%20playing/recordings_measured.md)). The square law has a plain reading: a piston pressing air through a tight gap meets a resistance that grows with the flow. It would also explain why makers find a tighter gap louder and why Ledang found it longer sustained. This reading is this report's; it rests on one instrument and five plucks.

**Claimed (maker).** A reed bent out of the frame plane loses "sound loudness and duration after a single stroke"; bent far enough not to reach the plane, it gives no sound ([Oberton Pro, nobuzz](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/nobuzz/)). Without the teeth as a counter-mass a bow-shaped harp sounds "nur ganz kurz" (Vandré, in the [reed notes](../research_notes/Jaw%20harp%20acoustics%20and%20playing/reed_and_sound_production.md)).

### Breath makes it louder, changes the balance of harmonics, and can sound the reed without a pluck

**Measured and described.** "Lorsqu'on souffle en pinçant la languette, le son change manifestement de hauteur et de nature ... de lame vibrante encastrée-libre elle devient anche libre"; inhaling and exhaling give the same pitch and a different timbre, "car la languette est un système dissymétrique"; John Wright used "constamment des aspirations-expirations, mais faibles" ([Leipp 1967](https://www.lam.jussieu.fr/Publications/BulletinsGAM/GAM_25-La-Guimbarde.pdf); his sentence on which harmonics breath favours is damaged in the scan). "When breathing in and out, the air stream amplifies the fundamental note", and breathing between plucks gives "rasping sounds" ([Vandré](http://www.rvandre.de/playtech.html)). A student found the spectrum's general shape unchanged while breathing out ([Li 2015](https://courses.physics.illinois.edu/phys406/sp2017/Student_Projects/Spring15/Simon_Li_Physics_406_Final_Report_Sp15.pdf)); another found the range from 280 Hz to 1 kHz raised on a bamboo harp ([UBC wiki](https://wiki.ubc.ca/Course:Phys341_2020/Kumbing)).

**Claimed, and contradictory.** A reed lying exactly in the frame plane cannot be started "even by strongest breath"; one bent out of it can, and the maker calls that a defect ([Oberton Pro, nobuzz](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/nobuzz/)). The Czech encyclopedia says the opposite: "Dobrý nástroj zazní svým základním tónem i bez brnknutí při pouhém prosátí vzduchu štěrbinou" ([Czech Wikipedia](https://cs.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brumle)). A morsing can give "a very long duration note ... by sucking air through it", "sometimes (depending on the instrument) without even plucking it first" ([chinnapapa blog](https://chinnapapa.blogspot.com/2008/07/morsing.html)).

**Measured (free reeds).** "At low air flow rates aerodynamic forces add to the mechanical damping of the vibrating reed, but at higher flow rates, the aerodynamic forces contribute negative damping"; the threshold for reed-organ reeds is about 0.2 kPa ([Cottingham, Reed and Busha](https://www.cs.bu.edu/fac/snyder/TablatureWeb/BERLIN2.PDF); [Cottingham, Lilly and Reed](https://www.cs.bu.edu/fac/snyder/TablatureWeb/BERLIN1.PDF)).

**Measured here.** In the dense khomus and Indian takes the level between detected strokes rises as often as it falls, so something re-energises the sound without a stroke: soft plucks or breath. The method cannot tell which.

**Derived in the notes.** Breath does three separable things: it opens the glottis and couples the airways (see the next section), it adds a steady flow that the reed chops (louder, the fundamental most of all, a different balance of odd and even), and it makes noise. Gentle breath through a centred reed need not feed the reed's vibration at all. No sound level in decibels and no measured gain from breath exists.

## The mouth: one resonance stays near 400 Hz, the tongue moves the other over 0.5–3 kHz

### The pulses strike the mouth, and the harmonic nearest its resonance is the note

**Measured (abstract).** "When the mouth cavity is tuned to low frequencies, its natural resonance time is longer than the period between successive pulses and the harmonic series of the fundamental frequency of the lamella dominates the sound as the mouth tuning is changed. At high frequencies, the mouth cavity and ear respond to each successive pulse independently so that the dominant sensation of pitch coincides with the natural resonance frequency of the mouth and may be tuned continuously." ([Adkins 1974](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1914580)).

**Measured (hobby).** Subtracting the spectrum of a harp in a vice from that of the same harp played leaves a "formant-mountain around 1200 Hz" at the pitch played, and a knock on the cheek with the same mouth shape and no harp shows the same peak; "the most pronounced resonance region is the second formant of the vocal tract, being somewhere in the range from 500 to 2000 Hz", "the first formant can be anywhere in the range between 250 and 1000 Hz" and gives accompanying notes, in his experience tones up to about 2300 Hz can be played, and "several formants can be active at the same time" ([Vandré](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html)).

**Measured.** On a Yakut khomus (fundamental about 72 Hz, derived in the notes from the paper's figures) the authors find two formants: "F1 is instrumental; it homogenizes JH articulations and depends mostly on JH's construction. F2 is vocal; it differentiates between JH articulations". The instrumental one "falls on f5 in all vowels except 'Ü' and 'I,' where F1 broadens to f3–f5", about 220–365 Hz; the vocal one falls on "f13 for 'O,' f17 for 'A,' f23 for 'Ö,' f19 for 'Y,' f21 for 'Ä,' and f21-f23 for 'I'", about 950–1700 Hz. Four "poles" of articulation have salient bands of "2.4–3.1 kHz for 'front,' 0.3–1.6 kHz for 'back,' 1.2–2.5 kHz for 'high,' and 0.6–0.9 kHz for 'low' vowels" ([Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)).

**Measured here.** Every one of the eight instruments shows a lower peak that hardly moves and an upper one that does.

In the one take that holds the mouth still after each pluck ("vowels on tone c2") the upper peak sits at 1188 Hz, then 1782 Hz, then 2376–2574 Hz, then 1386 Hz with the lower peak almost as strong, then 792–924 Hz, while the lower peak stays at 396–462 Hz throughout. Which vowel each is was not heard.

**Sources disagree** on whether the lower resonance is the player's. Nikolsky and colleagues call it instrumental and fixed; Vandré, and Trias as reported, treat the low region as playable. The notes offer a reconciliation, untested: the frame at the teeth keeps the mouth opening small for every articulation, which holds the tract's first resonance low and nearly constant.

### The resonance is 50–120 Hz wide, and lifts one harmonic 5–17 dB above its neighbours

**Measured (hobby; the notes' readings of a bar chart, G harp at 98 Hz).** How far the played harmonic stands above the mean of its two neighbours:

| Playing mode | Odd harmonics 9–15 | Even harmonics 8–16 |
|---|---|---|
| Open tract, gentle breathing | about +9 dB | about +4 dB |
| Closed at the soft palate ("ng") | about +13 dB | about −3 dB |
| Glottis closed | about +17.5 dB | about −2.5 dB |
| In a vice, no mouth | about +6 dB | about −6 dB |

"The even-numbered overtones … are amplified and can be played especially clearly, but the odd-numbered overtones are not" with the tract closed (his overtone k is harmonic k + 1, so these are the odd harmonics); "the reason for this is not clear to me so far" ([Vandré](http://www.rvandre.de/tones.html)).

**Measured here.** The strongest harmonic above 300 Hz stands a median of 5–15 dB above the mean of its two neighbours (the top tenth of frames: 11–23 dB). Read as one two-pole resonance centred on that harmonic, the levels one and two harmonics away imply a width of 77–85 Hz (Molln c2), 85–116 Hz (Sicilian), 71–118 Hz (đàn môi) and 92–116 Hz (Indian). On the other four instruments the two estimates disagree by a factor of two to six, because neighbouring harmonics alternate in strength there.

**Measured (speech, as a stand-in).** With the glottis closed the first three resonances of men's vocal tracts are 45–65 Hz wide, 85–90 Hz during phonation; a model shows the first bandwidth growing "by 15% with open glottis, and by 35% with a rapid airflow" ([Hanna, Smith and Wolfe 2016](https://newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/HannaBandwidth.pdf)). Whispering raises the first four resonances by "255 Hz with standard deviation 90 Hz, 115 ± 105, 125 ± 125, and 75 ± 120 Hz" ([Swerdlin, Smith and Wolfe 2010](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/whisper.pdf)). These are speech data with the mouth free, not jaw harp data.

**Derived in the notes.** Vandré's closed-glottis figure, after subtracting the reed's own contrast, implies a width of about 52 Hz, a Q of 20–30 at 0.9–1.5 kHz; his knock response reads as 80–120 Hz at 1.22 kHz. Together with the recordings: a Q of roughly 10–25 for the moving resonance, sharper with the throat closed. A resonance of width B rings for 1/(πB), 3–7 ms, and the bursts of a 64–146 Hz reed come every 3.4–7.8 ms: ring time and burst spacing are of the same order across the playing range, so both of Adkins's regimes are in play at once, and a resonator struck by the bursts reproduces both without a switch. Overtone singers, who merge two formants, reach 15–35 dB over the neighbouring harmonics ([Bergevin et al. 2020](https://elifesciences.org/articles/50476)); nothing published shows jaw harp players doing the same.

### Low harps give more notes; the melody window is the same for all

**Derived in the notes.** The usable harmonics are whichever fall into the mouth's window of roughly 0.5–2 kHz: 10–36 for a 55 Hz reed, 7–27 at 72 Hz, 6–20 at 98 Hz, 4–13 at 147 Hz, 3–10 at 196 Hz. Neighbouring harmonics are 204 cents apart at the 8th, 105 at the 16th and 53 at the 32nd, so a low harp can approximate any tune and a high one cannot. The classic textbook figure of harmonics 4–12 and the Balinese 5–10 ([Morgan 2008](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)) fit the same window for fundamentals around 130–170 Hz.

**Measured here.** The upper peak moved within 100 ms in 31–76 % of the cases examined; where it moved, the median speed was 1.2–2.2 octaves per second (5.0 in the fast Molln take), and a tenth of the moves were faster than 4.5–6.9 octaves per second (10.7 in the fast take). The 85 ms analysis window blurs anything faster. Overtone singers retune in "∼40–60 ms" ([Bergevin et al. 2020](https://elifesciences.org/articles/50476)).

### The tongue tunes, the lips are held by the frame, and the throat is a three-way switch

**Described.** Melody is made with the tongue movements of "u" to "i", as in whistling; low accompaniment with "u - o - a - o - u" formed in the throat while the lips stay on the frame ([Vandré](http://www.rvandre.de/playtech.html)). Closing the lips on the frame arms makes the sound louder ([Oberton Pro, handling](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/handling/)).

**Described and observed.** Three positions of the throat end are named. Glottis open: the airways are coupled, the sound is louder and deeper, and breath passes; the Balinese genggong "uses an open glottis ... That is what gives genggong its characteristically deep and guttural sound" ([Morgan 2008](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)). Glottis closed: sharper and clearer; the "closed" tone is the hallmark of Norwegian playing, and with it "in addition to the 'normal' overtones deeper overtones are amplified. Two overtones can be heard simultaneously" ([Vandré](http://www.rvandre.de/playtech.html)); the Ainu name mukkuri is glossed "closed-throat instrument" ([Shimomura 2010](https://acoustics.jp/cms/wp_asj/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/museum21.pdf)). Tongue against the soft palate: only the front of the mouth resonates, with formants about twice as high (an anatomist's reading of spectrograms, [Abe 2010](https://jewsharpsociety.org/storage/app/media/IJHS%20Publications/NEWSLETTERS/2010.12.24%20IJHS%20Newsletter%2011.pdf)). A student found that holding the breath with the throat open to the lungs removed a peak near 3 kHz ([Li 2015](https://courses.physics.illinois.edu/phys406/sp2017/Student_Projects/Spring15/Simon_Li_Physics_406_Final_Report_Sp15.pdf)).

**Thin.** Two MRI efforts exist and neither has published numbers: Trias's 2010 master's thesis, not obtained, and Áron Szilágyi's static scans of "five tones: highest, middle, low and two throat tones" made for teaching ([Szilágyi 2023](https://jewsharpsociety.org/storage/app/media/IJHS%20Publications/NEWSLETTERS/2023%20IJHS%20Newsletter_BOING_2023_01_sr.pdf)). There is no formant table in hertz for jaw harp articulations and no measurement of what the soft palate, the cheeks or the larynx height do. On the nose there is only an encyclopedia's account of the morsing: "the syllable /ŋg/ or a variant thereof, is sounded through the nose while air is pushed out or pulled in through the mouth", and constricting the mouth lets "the nostrils ... produce sounds in different phases, similar to phasers in electronic music" ([Wikipedia, Morsing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morsing)). Nothing was found on tongue clicks.

**Derived in the notes.** For a model, "throat open" should widen the moving resonance by half to double, push the low one up by 150–300 Hz, raise the even harmonics by several decibels and let breath through; "throat closed" does the reverse and excludes breath. The mouth does not pull the reed's pitch: every source treats the reed's frequencies as fixed, and the only contact effects are a finger or the lips touching the reed.

### Voice is not needed; the consonants are gates

**Described and observed.** Syllables are "enunciated into the instrument in a kind of projected whisper" ([Morgan 2008](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)); in the Sakha manner *syyia tardy* "silent mouth articulations as though speak out syllables while the plucked lamella keeps vibrating" ([Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)); the Hmong harp carries the tones of speech in courtship ([Smithsonian 1980](https://folklife-media.si.edu/docs/festival/program-book-articles/FESTBK1980_09.pdf)); 8Dio sells a library of the letters A to Z "spoken" on a harp ([8Dio](https://8dio.com/products/jaw-harp), snippet). Tongue articulation ("de de de", "doodle") "opens and closes the oral cavity, allowing and interrupting resonance"; the opening and closing of the glottis is itself heard ([Vandré](http://www.rvandre.de/playtech.html)). Sung or hummed voice with the harp is documented for Sakha and Tuvan playing and described as interfering ([mouth notes](../research_notes/Jaw%20harp%20acoustics%20and%20playing/mouth_and_vocal_tract.md)).

**Derived in the notes.** The "consonants" of the instrument reduce to four gates on an otherwise continuous reed: the pluck pattern, tongue closures that cut the moving resonance while the drone carries on, glottal closures that switch the throat between its two states, and breath bursts. None needs a voice source.

## Playing: the strokes are one layer, and the words are made inside each ring

### What the hand does: both directions, 8–10 strokes a second in fast playing, a choke before each

**Described.** Teachers name a single-direction pluck, a "there and back" stroke from a loose wrist for speed, and a roll of three or four fingers for tremolo. "Accomplished players may pluck the reed as many as 1,000 times per minute, though usually for short periods" ([Harman, Mouthing Off #4](http://www.jewsharpguild.org/whmo4.html)), a teacher's estimate and the only stroke rate in the text sources. "The pluck sets the tempo of your music. Maintaining a steady beat is critical." ([Harman, Mouthing Off #2](http://www.jewsharpguild.org/whmo2.html)). Morsing is plucked "backwards, not forwards" ([chinnapapa blog](https://chinnapapa.blogspot.com/2008/07/morsing.html)); Russian and Sakha teaching texts pluck toward the player, Harman and Vandré away ([techniques notes](../research_notes/Jaw%20harp%20acoustics%20and%20playing/playing_techniques.md)).

**Measured.** Carnatic percussion solos (mridangam, ghatam, khanjira; no morsing in the data) run at a mean of 8.6 strokes per second with a standard deviation of 3.8 over about twelve hours of recordings ([Gowriprasad et al. 2023](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2023/paper/000005.pdf)).

**Measured here.** The fast passages of a khomus and a Molln harp have a median of 113–117 ms between strokes with a tenth of the intervals at or under 93–100 ms; the demonstration takes of the other harps sit near three strokes per second. Before each stroke on a ringing reed the level falls 20–25 dB during the last 25–50 ms: the finger arrives, stops the reed, and lets go. No text source describes this; the techniques notes had flagged it as needing the ear or an analysis.

**Described.** The reed is also stopped or shortened on purpose: a finger laid on it (which raises the pitch; the Sakha "drops" strike a reed held at mid-length "from both sides"), the tongue pushed against it, the lips closed on it (a percussive clap), and the throat ([Stepanova's lesson](https://www.varganist.ru/43e.html); [Vandré](http://www.rvandre.de/playtech.html)). "Staccato" means three different things in the sources: a damped reed, a ringing reed chopped by the tongue, and the Sakha imitation of hooves and drips.

### What happens inside one ring, and what the sequence of strokes adds

**Described.** A notation system for the instrument says it outright: under a tie "the finger beat should be made only at the first note of all the tied notes, the rest of notes are produced with the help of your speech apparatus and breath"; its other signs are overtone, open or closed sound, breath direction, stroke direction, accent, glottal start, glottal stop, finger damping, trill and syllable ([Markov](https://varganist.ru/491e.html)). A Russian school counts exercises "on one stroke": several lip closings, a pair of vowels, "three short and sharp inhales and exhales" ([Aronov, archived](http://web.archive.org/web/20200225231341/http://www.vargan.ru:80/varganzal/varganschools/94-uprazhneniya-s-varganom-nizhegorodskaya-vargannaya-shkola)).

**Derived in the notes (condensed from the techniques notes' table of 31 entries).** A = lives inside one hit, the engine must produce it. B = arises from the order and timing of hits, the notes supply it.

| Sound or gesture | Kind | What makes it | Timing evidence |
|---|---|---|---|

| Accented against passing notes | B | the pattern of strong and weak hits | none |

**Not found.** The words the brief asked about, "doing" or "dojng", "yoi", "ee-oo", "da-ga-da-ga" and "tiki-taka", do not occur in the sources read; nor does "ghost note" as a jaw harp term. What the sources print is "wow", "bend", "de de de", "doodle", "dü dl", "taka-taka", "Ka-Ku", "iy ia io iu", the Sakha vocable "hyttya", and from outsiders "wobbles, boings and onomatopoeic springing sounds" ([Classic FM](https://www.classicfm.com/discover-music/instruments/morsing-introduction-tuning-use/)) and, as an insult to a bad imitation, "cartoony spring noises" ([plugins4free](https://plugins4free.com/plugin/1125)). A "boing" is by this evidence a single stroke with a vowel glide, and a "wah" the same with the mouth opening; that reading is this report's.

**Described.** Loudness and accent come "less from plucking harder than from the mouth and the air" in the teachers' accounts; "K" with a diaphragm push gives a bass accent ([techniques notes](../research_notes/Jaw%20harp%20acoustics%20and%20playing/playing_techniques.md)).

### Two traditions treat it as percussion; the rest make rhythm inside a drone

**Morsing (South India).** **Observed.** "The morsing is made of very thick iron, and the lamella has very high tension and short sustain. The result is a loud, high-pitched staccato effect"; it "is unique among Jew's harp traditions in that the instrument is conceptualized in terms of its rhythmic possibilities" ([Morgan 2008](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)). Among the Carnatic percussion it "predominantly spreads over the high-mid frequency spectrum and has a larger resonance" ([Gowriprasad et al. 2023](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2023/paper/000005.pdf)). **Described and claimed.** It shadows the mridangam; the player silently speaks the drum syllables (tha ka dhi mi, tha ki ta, tha dhi gi na thom) so that "the air passages gets blocked and cleared in a pattern" ([Wikipedia, Morsing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morsing)); "notes are sustained by the breath", "you can breathe in or out through the instrument" ([Classic FM](https://www.classicfm.com/discover-music/instruments/morsing-introduction-tuning-use/)); "the effect of gumkis ... is produced with no plucking at all"; "double speed nuances ... are really hard to play on this instrument"; "knowing where not to play is actually critical" ([Lakshmi Anand, on Bhagyalakshmi M. Krishna](https://lakshmianand.com/?p=3384) and [on V. Sai Subramaniam](https://lakshmianand.com/?p=4027)). **Not found:** how each syllable is realised (stroke direction, tongue, breath), any tempo of morsing playing, and its pitch in hertz.

**Genggong (Bali).** **Observed**, and the best-documented jaw harp rhythm found: a palm-stem harp jerked by a string, with "a relatively short sustain", played in pairs whose fundamentals lie about a minor third apart, each player taking alternate notes of an interlocking line; "half the notes are played while exhaling and the other half are played by inhaling", the exhaled ones accented and the inhaled ones passing; an older style runs in eighth notes at half the density ([Morgan 2008](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~amcgraw/gamelan/ubc_2008_fall_morgan_deirdre-libre.pdf)).

**Khomus (Sakha).** **Observed and described.** A solo, improvising instrument of "speech-like articulations and special sound effects", judged "not as 'pitch,' but as 'coloration.'" ([Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)). The old manner *syyia tardy* gives one stroke per syllable at a steady pace; the concert style strings together named effects: *tabygyr* (staccato; horse trot, dripping icicles), *kebe* (cuckoo), the lark call, the round-dance formula *okhuokhai*, a crescendo; "rhythm often changes cardinally several times in one composition" ([Shishigin interview](https://www.varganist.ru/53e.html)). "A khomus with low sounding sounds longer and suits temperate playing. Quick playing needs a khomus with high tone." (the same interview). No tempo and no notated rhythm were found.

**Central and Inner Asia, and the lamellate harps of the south-east.** **Observed.** Kyrgyz pieces (*küü*) on the metal harp are variations on songs, pictures of animals and landscape ("Black lark", "Pacer horse") and personal stories; in one example "the performer plays a traditional küü, gradually accelerating the tempo, then adding a second traditional theme and improvising on it", and an ensemble piece uses two metal harps "tuned a fourth apart" ([Nyshanov 2016](https://musc102.blogs.wesleyan.edu/files/2022/02/The-Music-of-Central-Asia_Chapters-15-16.pdf)). Tuvan players name a "singing play" and a "speaking play", the silent execution of a song's words, beside imitation of nature and playing together with throat-singing ([Tadagawa 2017](https://nit.tuva.asia/nit/article/download/718/1107)). The Hmong harp is held at the mouth "while inhaling or exhaling at each rhythmic stroke" and speaks courtship messages in a tonal language ([Smithsonian 1980](https://folklife-media.si.edu/docs/festival/program-book-articles/FESTBK1980_09.pdf)). None of these sources gives a tempo or a pattern.

**Europe.** **Claimed and described.** Alpine playing is melodic: two to four harps on tonic, dominant and subdominant are swapped at chord changes ([Wimmer-Bades](https://www.maultrommel.at/produkte/gestimmte-maultrommel/); the measured Molln takes do exactly this). The Norwegian munnharpe plays fiddle dance tunes with a foot stomp and "closed" tones; the only tempo found is a general one for the halling dance, 95–106 bpm ([Wikipedia, Halling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halling_%28dance%29)). In Hungary the drone "can be strengthened by breathing in and out, and turned into a rhythm accompaniment by the pulsing movement of the diaphragm" ([Hungarian Wikipedia](https://hu.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doromb), the notes' translation). For the Czech brumle: "Brnkání je možné oběma směry", and "rytmickým prosáváním vzduchu štěrbinou při hraní se dosahuje efektu vlastního nepřízvučného a barvitého doprovodu, tak zvaného kontrování": breath drawn through the slit in rhythm gives an unaccented accompaniment of one's own ([Czech Wikipedia](https://cs.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brumle), unsourced there). A Czech teacher starts "with explaining the rhythm, because it is absolutely essential" and uses the harp as "a sort of baseline" under a drum ([Radio Prague International](https://english.radio.cz/node/8798977)). Scottish and Irish players pluck every note of a reel, or beats one and two of three in a jig ([traditions notes](../research_notes/Jaw%20harp%20acoustics%20and%20playing/traditions_and_rhythm.md)). No ethnographic source on historical playing in Bohemia, Moravia or Slovakia was found.

**Contemporary.** **Claimed.** A "trance" idiom of fast, breath-driven playing with didgeridoo, drums and beatbox; a listener calls an expertly played harp "basically an acoustic TB-303" (snippet, [Boing Boing BBS](https://bbs.boingboing.net/t/techno-performed-with-a-jaw-harp/154884)). No text describes its technique or tempo.

**Claimed (makers), on which instrument suits what.** "As a rule, a lower jaw harp is slower and more inertial, while a higher one lends itself to fast playing" ([Oberton Pro, choosing](https://www.oberton.pro/en/info/choice.htm)). For fast rhythm: high pitch, a stiff reed, a short bent end, tolerance of two-way strokes, a "saturated" timbre and a shorter ring; one such harp "decays rather quickly" and is called very convenient for fast, intensive two-way stroking ([Oberton Pro, Pripyat](https://www.oberton.pro/en/shop/pripyat-vargan.html)). For overtone melody: low to medium pitch, a soft reed, a long ring, a "clean" timbre with evenly loud harmonics ([instruments notes](../research_notes/Jaw%20harp%20acoustics%20and%20playing/instruments_and_tunings.md)).

**Derived in the notes.** The sound descriptions split into a short, bright, loud staccato type (morsing) and a long-ringing, low, vowel-rich type (khomus), with Alpine, Norwegian and Celtic playing in between as melody over a drone. Because the pluck is the only attack, rhythm in most traditions is two layers, the stroke stream and the articulation inside each ring.

## Synthesis: no published model, one open-source attempt, and sample libraries whose mouths cannot glide

### The literature stops at the mechanism

**Found, and not found.** No peer-reviewed or conference paper presents a synthesis model of the jaw harp, physical or signal: searches of JASA, Acta Acustica, DAFx, ICMC, SMC, NIME, ISMA, Forum Acusticum, arXiv and HAL in English, German, French, Russian and Japanese returned none, and the Faust, STK, SuperCollider, Csound, Pure Data and VCV libraries contain no jaw harp ([synthesis notes](../research_notes/Jaw%20harp%20acoustics%20and%20playing/synthesis_and_prior_art.md)). The scientific work ends with Adkins's pulses, Ledang's gap and Leipp's spectra. No published parameter set exists: no reed mass, stiffness or damping, no pulse shape, no listening test of any imitation.

**Published code (checked for this report).** One open-source plug-in, "SuperDuper Kubyz" (Rust, MIT, 2026), shares its engine with an iOS app advertised as "the first physics-based jaw harp synthesizer". The code is a signal model: "additive 16-harmonic oscillator + ADSR + 1–6 band formant bank", the harmonics from a fixed amplitude table, the formants parallel band-passes with Q capped at 12 and targets taken from speech vowels, the mouth moved by an LFO on a vowel pad, velocity raising the formants by 15 %, decay times of 1.0–1.8 s with "sustain must be 0" ([voice.rs](https://github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp/blob/main/synth-core/src/kubyz/voice.rs); [presets.rs](https://github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp/blob/main/effects/superduper-kubyz/src/presets.rs)). Its author's own test notes are the most useful critique found: with the reed at 68.93 Hz, 16 harmonics "end at 1103 Hz", which is "not an approximation of the instrument but its lower third"; the spectrum's shape "is the jaw harp's signature, not a roll-off: maximum at H6 (414 Hz), H1 53 dB quieter" ([kubyz_f2.rs](https://github.com/fortunto2/superduper-dsp/blob/main/synth-core/examples/kubyz_f2.rs), in Russian; the notes' translation). That maximum at 414 Hz and the weak fundamental agree with the lower peak and the weak fundamental measured here.

**Claimed (vendors) and users' words.** Everything else is samples. Loops de la Crème's "Magic Jaw Harps" has 519 samples of seven harps with "up to 5 velocity layers and up to 7 Round-robins" and the mouth on the modulation wheel ([vendor page](https://www.loopsdelacreme.com/magic-jaw-harps)); a Pianobook pack records open, middle and closed mouth positions and "Mod wheel crossfades between position groups", with breathy plucks in the top velocities ([Time Warp Mouth Harp](https://www.pianobook.co.uk/packs/time-warp-mouth-harp/)); others offer five vowels times twelve techniques, a Morsing loop library, a SwarPlug pack with two morsings, and a Roland expansion board with "Jaw Harp Opn" and "Jaw Harp Wow" ([synthesis notes](../research_notes/Jaw%20harp%20acoustics%20and%20playing/synthesis_and_prior_art.md)). Two phone apps play a twang from the speaker into the user's own mouth ([Mouth Harp](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id376563785)). What users say: of a free sample plug-in, "If you want an actual mouth harp sound: NO. If you just want cartoony spring noises ... you're in the right place" and its presets are "all out-of-key" ([plugins4free](https://plugins4free.com/plugin/1125)); of a sampled Austrian harp, "the thing about this 'simple' single-note instrument is the change of harmonics, shaped by the oral cavity" ([Pianobook](https://pianobook.co.uk/packs/austrian-jaw-harp)). The few forum recipes are all one idea, a rich source at a fixed pitch with a formant filter doing the playing ("Use a mono synth, always play the same note but with different formant filter settings", [KVR](https://www.kvraudio.com/forum/viewtopic.php?t=455311)); none gives numbers and none claims the result convinces.

**Derived in the notes.** The libraries converge on one control map: a note is a pluck, velocity is its strength, the wheel is the mouth, round robins fight repetition. Their mouths have a ceiling: crossfading between recorded positions fades one fixed formant out and another in and cannot glide, and a filter on a recording adds a second formant to the one already in it. A synthesized resonance has neither limit, and free choice of the reed's pitch answers the commonest practical complaint.

### Five ways to build it, compared for sixteen voices

Costs are the notes' counts from published code and tables, or this report's where marked; none is a measured CPU figure for a jaw harp.

| Method | What it would be here | Cost for 16 voices | Fits the physics? | Main risk for the ear |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Prescribed reed, slot law, struck resonators** (recommended) | a decaying sine; a non-linearity that turns each passage through the slot into a burst; three or four band-passes in parallel struck by the bursts | about 5 multiplies per state-variable filter and sample; 16 voices × 5 filters = 400 multiplies per sample; at 96 kHz that is 2.7 times the filter work of one Mutable Instruments Rings, which runs 60 such filters at 48 kHz on a 168 MHz microcontroller ([resonator.h](https://github.com/pichenettes/eurorack/blob/master/rings/dsp/resonator.h)); with the source, about 80–90 operations per voice and sample (this report's count) | yes: it is Adkins's mechanism; both of his regimes come out without a switch | "a filter on a buzz" if the gestures are static; aliasing if bursts get shorter than about three samples |
| Additive | one sine per harmonic, amplitudes computed from a source law and a mouth curve | 145 harmonics on a khomus ([Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)) means 2320 sines; 16 partials is the documented failure | steady state yes; the ring after each burst only by proxy | amplitude curves updated at control rate smear fast taps and glides |
| Modal (a bank of ringing modes per harmonic) | the harmonics treated as resonances | as additive | no: the partials are locked lines of a pulse train, not modes; a load moves them all together | wrong decay behaviour; no brightness tied to amplitude |
| Formant-wave grains (FOF) | a grain at the mouth's frequency per burst | grows with pitch times grain length, per formant | yes in kind | grains are fixed at birth, so a moving mouth steps |
| Full physical model | reed as mass and spring, flow through the gaps, a waveguide mouth | a Pink-Trombone-style tract is 44 oral plus 28 nasal sections stepped twice per sample ([source](https://dood.al/pinktrombone/)): about 2300 junctions per sample; the published free-reed models run at 4 × 44.1 kHz with an iterative solver ([Puranik and Scavone 2023](https://dael.euracoustics.org/confs/fa2023/data/articles/000853.pdf)) | most complete | control by tract shapes nobody has measured; "prone to aliasing" when the shape moves |

Three building-block findings carry over directly. The trapezoidal state-variable filter is proven stable while its frequency moves and passed a listening test ([Wishnick 2014](https://www.dafx14.fau.de/papers/dafx14_aaron_wishnick_time_varying_filters_for_.pdf)), where direct-form resonators give "clicks and burps" on sudden changes ([Klatt 1980](https://www.fon.hum.uva.nl/david/ma_ssp/doc/Klatt-1980-JAS000971.pdf)); Didgeridoo's `TractResonance` already is that filter. Klatt uses resonators in parallel when amplitudes must be set one by one, which is the case here. And the free-reed models solve a problem a plucked reed does not have: they exist to explain how air starts and sustains the reed.

### Why the recommended method, and where it can still fail the ear

**Proposal, with reasons.**

1. It is the mechanism the measurements describe, not an analogy: a timing oscillator, a slot that fires bursts, a mouth that rings.
2. The pitch is an input without approximation. Didgeridoo's lips are a valve that oscillates only because of the tube, and its engine prescribes their pitch against the physics; the overblown note, where the physics is a jump between regimes, failed the author's ear three times (`.planning/phases/15-one-model/PLAN.md` in Didgeridoo). A jaw harp reed is plucked and rings at its own frequency whatever the mouth does; the recordings show its partials within a few cents and its pitch steady to 5–8 cents. Prescribing its motion gives up very little.
3. The brightness contour comes by itself. The bursts shorten with the reed's speed, so a hard stroke is bright and every note darkens as it dies, as measured, with no filter envelope to tune.
4. A struck resonator glides, taps and rings as the real mouth does, which is the one thing the sampled products cannot do.
5. It is the cheapest of the candidates that fit.

**Risks for the ear, in the order this report would test them.**

- **The source law is a fit to one instrument.** Square-of-speed bursts reproduce five slopes of the Molln c2 harp; the sign of successive bursts, the reed's rest offset and the breath flow set the balance of odd and even harmonics, on which the literature and the recordings disagree. All three must be knobs, and their defaults are the ear's.
- **Two resonances are not enough.** The simulation had a crest factor of 11–13 dB against a measured 16–20 dB and too little above 2 kHz. The bursts themselves must be heard beside the resonances, and a third, fixed resonance near 2.5–3 kHz is likely needed.
- **Sameness.** Leipp found that successive strokes "diffèrent souvent beaucoup de l'un à l'autre dans le détail"; sample libraries spend up to seven round robins on this. A synthesized hit repeats exactly unless something varies.
- **A missing dip.** Every real re-pluck is preceded by 25–50 ms of choke. A MIDI instrument learns of the stroke when it happens.
- **The fundamental.** Recordings have it 17–31 dB down. The player, who hears through the teeth, hears more of it, and a user may expect a drone.
- **Gestures nobody measured.** Sweep times, tap rates and breath pulses have no published values; the only numbers are this research's 1–10 octaves per second.
- **Aliasing.** A burst lasts about 1/(2π·f0·swing): 130 µs for a 66 Hz reed swinging 18 slot half-widths, 60 µs at 146 Hz. Below about 50 µs at 44.1 kHz the spectrum is no longer 70 dB down at the Nyquist frequency (this report's arithmetic for a sech² burst), so the swing must be limited as pitch rises, or the slot stage run at twice the rate.

## Recommendation for Brumle's engine

Everything in this section is a **proposal**. Basis letters: M published measurement, H measured here, C described or claimed, D derived in the notes or here, P this report's choice with no evidence behind the number.

### The signal path of one voice

```
note-on: pitch, velocity, and the knob set this hit plays with (the panel's, or the pad's)

reed     a sine at the note's pitch. Its swing starts at the stroke's strength and dies away
         ("Ring"). It is let go at full deflection, so the first burst comes a quarter period
         later. A finger stops it at key-up or when the same note is struck again ("Damp").
slot     each passage of the reed through the frame makes a burst:
           gate(position) x (speed + breath) x |speed + breath|
         "Edge": how far the reed swings past the frame, so how short the bursts are.
         "Offset": where the reed rests against the frame plane. "Breath": a steady flow,
         which makes the two bursts of a cycle unequal; breath noise passes the same gate.
mouth    resonances in parallel, struck by the bursts:
           the one that stays ("Throat", near 430 Hz),
           the one the tongue moves ("Vowel", 0.5 to 3.3 kHz, as sharp as "Reso"),
           a fixed high one near 2.8 kHz,
         beside them the bursts themselves ("Buzz") and a trace of the reed's motion ("Body").
gesture  the Vowel travels by "Sweep" over "Sweep Time" along "Curve"; the tongue can shut
         the moving resonance ("Close"); the throat can open ("Open"); the LFOs start with
         the hit.
         -> voices summed -> DC blocker -> volume -> limiter
```

### What a single hit contains

| Time | What happens | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | the finger lets go: the reed is at full deflection, to one side or the other ("Stroke") | C |
| a quarter period later (1.7–3.9 ms for 64–146 Hz) | the first burst, at full size: no attack ramp | H: full level within two periods; no separate bright click |
| the first 50 ms or so | optionally a second, faster burst series from the reed's second bending mode, and frame contact on a hard stroke ("Clang") | M for a plucked accordion reed; C for the clatter |
| 0 to Puff Time | a push of breath: louder, the two bursts of a cycle unequal, noise | C: the accent "stack" |
| 0 to Sweep Time | the moving resonance travels from Vowel by Sweep | H: 1–2 octaves per second typical, 5–10 fast |
| all along | the reed's swing dies (Ring); the bursts lengthen, so the upper harmonics fall two to six times faster than the low ones | H, and the fit |
| all along | LFOs started by the hit: tongue taps (Close), lark (Vowel), breath pulses (Breath) | C |
| key-up, or the same note again | the finger stops the reed over Damp | H: 25–50 ms on real instruments |

### Default character: a medium steel harp that says "dojng", ringing about a second

**Proposal.** The documented percussive type is the morsing: short, bright, loud, high. The documented nature of the instrument, and the sound a listener calls a jaw harp, is a reed that rings while the mouth moves. For a rhythm-first instrument whose notes will often be taps, the default should sit between them:

- a reed in the octave where most real harps are, played from C2 to D3 (the pads), bright (Edge 18, the fitted value);
- one clear gesture per hit: the moving resonance rising from 800 Hz by 14 semitones (to about 1.8 kHz) in 160 ms, the documented window of 0.5–2.3 kHz and a speed of about 7 octaves per second, at the fast end of what the recordings show;
- an audible ring of about one second: the reed's own 60 dB time at 4 s, half the value fitted to a low harp, which makes the sound fall about 30 dB per second, the rate of the two higher harps measured;
- a little breath pushed at each stroke, the throat mostly closed, a small difference from hit to hit, strokes alternating in direction;
- a tap rings out by itself, and striking the same note again stops the old ring.

Whether this is right is the first thing to play to the author; the opposite poles (dry and short, or ringing for four seconds) are programs.

### Pitch, note length, velocity, wheels

- **Note number.** The reed's fundamental, at the key's own pitch when Octave is 0. Notes 36–51 are C2–D#3, 65–156 Hz, where the measured instruments and the commonest catalogue notes are (H, C). Steel harps span notes 21–52 and brass leaf reeds reach 63; above about D3 fewer than ten harmonics fall into the mouth's window, the melody steps get coarse and the sound thins (D). The morsing's pitch is unknown; F3 is one snippet.
- **One voice per note number.** The same note struck again stops its own ring and plucks anew: that is one reed. Different notes ring over each other: those are different harps, as an Alpine player holds two or three. Keyboard mode and pad mode are then one path through the engine, and a pad hit is literally a frozen key press; the modes differ only in where a note's knob values come from.
- **Note length.** Key down is the finger letting go. Key up is the finger coming back (Key Up = Damp), or nothing (Ring), in which case a tap rings out and only the next stroke on the same note stops it. With Damp, a sequence's note lengths reproduce the measured choke before each stroke for free: end each note 25–50 ms before the next.
- **Velocity.** The swing at release. Through the slot law that is one control with three results: the low harmonics rise by about 6 dB per doubling of the swing, the spectrum extends by about an octave per doubling, and the brightness lasts longer. Velocity also scales Puff, because teachers say accents come more from air and mouth than from the finger, and at the very top brings Clang. A maker notes that past a certain force loudness stops growing; the mapping should flatten at the top.
- **Wheels.** Pitch bend moves the Vowel (the forums' consensus: the controller should address the resonance). The modulation wheel is best spent on Close or Open; aftertouch on Breath. All three act on the panel's knob set, not on ringing pad voices.

### Which knobs deserve LFOs

Vowel (the lark; a slow sweep across many hits in the trance manner), Close (tongue taps: a square LFO is "de de de"), Breath (diaphragm pulses, the "kontrování" between strokes on a ringing reed), Open (glottal gating), and with less use Reso, Throat, Buzz, Body, Volume, Edge and Offset. Not the times, the switches or Tune. Two LFOs as in Didgeridoo, each voice carrying its own phase when LFO Start is Hit, so that a hit repeats exactly, as the pad rule demands. Players lock these gestures to the pulse; rates in hertz are the simpler start, and syncing them to the host's tempo is a possible later step that would not contradict "rhythm comes from the notes", since it shapes what is inside a hit.

### What in Didgeridoo's architecture does not fit, and what to change

1. **One voice becomes a pool.** Sixteen voices, one per sounding note number, the quietest or oldest stolen with a fade of a few milliseconds. Each voice holds its reed, slot state, resonators, gesture clocks and LFO phases.
2. **Each voice reads its own knob set.** The panel's set for keys, a pad's frozen set for pads. A voice started from the panel should follow the knobs while it rings; a pad voice should not. The engine therefore holds 17 tables, not one.
3. **The parameter table times seventeen.** About 33 knobs plus 22 LFO amounts per set. Only the panel's set should be Audio Unit parameters; sixteen copies would be about 900. The pads' sets belong in the saved state as a block, and with them a pad's note. The rule that identifiers and order are permanent applies from the first release.
4. **No amplitude envelope.** The reed's swing is the envelope: exponential decay, a choke, nothing else. Attack, Release, Glide and the tempo-synced Rhythm section have no counterpart; time-shaped behaviour moves to per-hit gestures.
5. **Events, not a drone.** Didgeridoo's clocks run on while silent so that nothing jumps; here every hit restarts its gesture and, by default, its LFOs. Random variation must come from a generator that is reset with the engine, or `SoundRegressionTests` cannot hold references.
6. **Control rate per voice.** The filter coefficients need a tangent each; every 16 samples is fine for sweeps of 10 octaves per second (0.004 octave per step at 44.1 kHz). Stagger the voices' updates to spread the load.
7. **Dying voices.** A decaying sine and ringing filters run into denormal numbers; a voice must be retired below a floor, and the regression suite should include a long tail.
8. **Level.** Sixteen spiky voices can add up to far more than one; fix the per-voice level low and let the limiter work on the sum.
9. **Regression scores.** A reference per program on one hit is not enough: the suite needs scores with overlapping pads, a re-struck note, a damped note, and both stroke directions. Didgeridoo's `#pragma clang fp contract(off)` lesson applies unchanged.
10. **Mono or stereo.** Didgeridoo is mono. An MPC-style pad instrument usually gives each pad a place; a Pan knob per knob set would do it. This is a decision for the author, not something the research settles.

What carries over unchanged: the C interface over a C++ engine, sample-accurate MIDI, lock-free parameter and MIDI queues, the control interval, the trapezoidal resonator, the program table as overrides of the defaults, controller learning for the panel's knobs.

## Where the evidence runs out, and what only the ear can settle

### Nobody has published decay times, loudness, gesture times or the sign of the bursts

**Time behaviour.** No decay time of a metal harp in seconds, from a maker or a measurement; the only published timings are a bamboo harp's 0.4–0.7 s on a student page and an encyclopedia's "20 décibels par seconde" for a paper that was not obtained. No attack time, no tip amplitude in millimetres, no Q. No sweep, trill, tap, vibrato or breath-pulse time in milliseconds anywhere in the teaching literature; no tempo of any jaw harp performance and no transcription beyond one figure of Balinese breath and accent. The numbers in this report for all of these are **measured here**, on eleven takes by two players of one workshop.

**The source.** Adkins's pulse model, Ledang's experiments and Crane's argument were read only as an abstract or through other authors; Leipp's 1963 papers and Trias's 2010 thesis were not obtained. Nobody has published the shape, length or sign of the pulses, the slot's geometry as the reed sees it, or how any of it changes with amplitude. The square-of-speed law in this report is a fit to five slopes of one instrument. Which of odd and even harmonics leads is reported one way by four published sources and found otherwise here.

**Levels.** No sound level in decibels for any instrument, no gain from breath, no level of breath noise; "barely audible beyond a few meters" is the only statement ([Nikolsky et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03051)). The recordings analysed are not calibrated.

**The mouth.** No calibrated measurement of the mouth's resonance, its width or its dependence on glottis, soft palate, cheeks or larynx during playing; one hobbyist's bar chart and speech data stand in. No formant table in hertz. Why a closed throat favours odd harmonics is unexplained.

**Traditions.** No source maps the Carnatic syllables to strokes, tongue or breath on the morsing; its pitch and tempo are unpublished. The trance style, the Rajasthani morchang, the kubing, the Sicilian and the Czech and Slovak traditions rest on encyclopedia-level text. The Sakha methods (Alekseev 1988, Shishigin) were not available.

**Limits of this research.** The session's shared web-search allowance ran out before all leads were checked, which thinned the later traditions, the search for electric instruments and several language-specific searches. danmoi.com refused automated requests, so its material is snippet-level. The recordings were analysed without being heard: the stroke detector was checked only against printed envelope traces, the fundamental had to be set by hand for two instruments, and none of the players is a specialist of the traditions the instruments come from.

### Twelve questions for the author's ear

Each names what to compare and the knob that would move.

1. **Dry or ringing?** A rhythm default that rings about a second (Ring 4 s) against a morsing-like stab (1 s) and a let-ring harp (7.5 s).
2. **How bright, and for how long?** Edge at 9, 18 and 30: the brightness and its decay are one knob, and 18 is fitted to one harp.
3. **Odd or even?** Offset 0 and Breath 0 (the literature's bare reed: hollow, odd harmonics) against Offset 0.2 and Breath +30 %, and against an inhaled hit. This also decides whether in-strokes and out-strokes should differ audibly.
4. **How much fundamental?** Body at −24 dB (what the recordings have) against −10 dB (nearer what a player hears through the teeth).
5. **Whistle or twang?** Reso 8, 15 and 30 with Buzz at 0, 40 and 80 %: a sharp resonance on a clean buzz sings one harmonic; a broad one on a strong buzz is a spring.
6. **The gesture.** Sweep up 14 semitones in 160 ms against half and double the time, against a downward sweep, against there-and-back: which of these is "the" jaw harp hit. Nothing published says.
7. **The stroke itself.** Does the hit need Clang, or is the instant burst train enough? The recordings show no separate bright click.
8. **Re-striking.** The old ring stopped in 10 ms at the new stroke, against the measured 25–50 ms of choke before it (possible only when key-up damps and the notes are short).
9. **Sameness.** Vary at 0, 20 and 50 % on a fast pattern of one pad.
10. **Velocity.** Level only, against level with brightness and breath; soft hits should be darker and breathier, not merely quieter.
11. **The top of the range.** Notes above D3, where real steel harps end and the melody steps get coarse; and whether a "morsing" a fourth or more above that still sounds like a jaw harp.
12. **Sixteen at once.** Overlapping pads at different pitches: real players hold at most a few harps, so nothing in the sources says what a chord of sixteen should sound like, or whether it needs places in a stereo field.

**A first experiment worth more than any of the numbers above.** The eleven recordings are public (list and links in the [recordings notes](../research_notes/Jaw%20harp%20acoustics%20and%20playing/recordings_measured.md); CC BY-SA 4.0, usable as references, not as shipped samples without honouring that licence). The author listens and names the one or two that sound like the instrument he means. Ring, Edge, Offset and Breath are then fitted to those takes with the analysis already written, and a prototype of the slot law with three resonators is compared with them by ear before any program is voiced. Didgeridoo's phase 15 showed what happens otherwise: every measured number matched and the ear still said no.

## Conclusion

The jaw harp turns out to be the opposite of the didgeridoo in the way that matters for a synthesizer. There the pitch was the output of a coupled, self-sustained valve, and prescribing it was the approximation that failed. Here the reed is a nearly free oscillator that only keeps time; the sound is made where it passes its frame and shaped where it enters the mouth. A model can therefore prescribe the reed exactly, and spend its effort on two things the literature names and this research measured: bursts whose sharpness follows the reed's swing, and a narrow resonance that moves. One law for the slot gives the instant attack, the brightness that dies before the body, the dependence on stroke strength and the effect of breath, and it was fitted to a real instrument's decay with two numbers.

What the evidence cannot give is the character. The published acoustics describe the mechanism and stop; the playing literature names every gesture and times none; the one existing synthesizer and the sample libraries show mostly what to avoid: too few harmonics, speech vowels, a mouth that crossfades. The measured recordings fill some of the gap, from one workshop's players, unheard. So the design should be built to be decided by ear: the defaults proposed here are a starting position between the two documented poles, the short loud morsing and the long vowel-rich khomus, and the twelve questions above are the ones a prototype should put to the author before any program is voiced.
